Measures of Disease Frequency

Two families of measure: incidence counts new disease over time (risk), prevalence counts existing disease at a moment (burden). Mixing them up is the most common error in this subdomain.

Ratio, proportion, and rate ref

Every disease-frequency measure on this page is built from one of three underlying quantity types.

TermDefinitionExample
RatioOne quantity divided by another; the numerator is not necessarily part of the denominatorMale-to-female ratio at intake
ProportionA ratio in which the numerator is a subset of the denominator, A / (A+B); dimensionless, no time dimensionPercentage of intake that is feline
RateA ratio that incorporates the passage of time, measuring how quickly events occur in a population over a defined periodIncidence rate (cases per 100 dog-days)
Exam trap

"Incidence rate" and "cumulative incidence" get used loosely and interchangeably, but only incidence density (person-time in the denominator) is a true rate. Cumulative incidence is technically a proportion, despite the word "risk" — it has a stated observation window but no built-in per-unit-time component.

Incidence ref

  • Cumulative incidence (incidence risk): the proportion of an at-risk group that develops new disease over a defined period. A dimensionless proportion, so the time period must always be stated. CI = new cases / population at risk.
  • Incidence rate (incidence density): new cases divided by total animal-time at risk, giving cases per unit time (for example, cases per 100 dog-days). It accommodates animals entering and leaving at different times.
  • Attack rate: cumulative incidence measured over the limited period of an outbreak.
Shelter note

Shelter populations are unstable, with widely varying lengths of stay, so the animal-time denominator of the incidence rate is the more rigorous choice. Cumulative incidence still tracks trends closely and is simpler for routine monitoring.

The at-risk denominator

Only animals capable of developing new disease belong in the denominator. Exclude animals already infected at the start, animals arriving already infected, animals that recovered before the interval, and animals removed so quickly they had no exposure opportunity.

Filtering the at-risk denominator. Reconstructed from your worked example. Original construction.
Clarification

Excluding the recovered cats assumes they are immune. For feline URI (herpesvirus and calicivirus) recovered cats can be reinfected or relapse, so they are arguably still at risk. The exclusion is a valid simplification only if recurrent cases are tracked as a separate metric.

Prevalence ref

  • Point prevalence: the proportion with disease at a single instant (for example, FeLV positivity on intake day).
  • Period prevalence: the proportion with disease at any time during an interval.
  • Real-world seroprevalence example: a survey of 552 Australian stray and feral cats found a point seroprevalence for Toxoplasma gondii antibodies of 40.4% (223/552); multivariable logistic regression identified bodyweight (a proxy for age), geographic location, season, and feral-versus-stray status as risk factors for seropositivity, but sex was not — a reminder that a plausible risk factor still has to be confirmed statistically, not assumed (Liyanage et al., Veterinary Parasitology, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Prevalence reflects existing burden, which is what drives confirmatory-test interpretation, pharmacy budgeting, and housing planning.

Linking incidence and prevalence ref

Incidence fills the pool, recovery and death drain it. Original construction.

When prevalence is low and stable, Prevalence ≈ Incidence × average Duration. A chronic, long-duration disease therefore shows a high prevalence relative to its incidence; a rapidly fatal or quickly resolving disease shows a low one.

Measures of Association and Impact

Association measures ask how strongly an exposure and an outcome travel together. The design dictates which measure you can compute, and ratio measures share a null value of 1.

The 2x2 table ref

The exposure-outcome 2x2 and the measures each design produces. Original construction.

The measures ref

MeasureDefinitionFromNull
Relative risk (risk ratio)Incidence in exposed / incidence in unexposedCohort, experimental1
Odds ratioOdds of exposure in cases / in controls (ad/bc)Case-control1
Prevalence ratioPrevalence in exposed / unexposedCross-sectional1
Attributable risk (risk difference)Incidence exposed minus incidence unexposedCohort, experimental0

Above 1 means increased risk, below 1 means protection, for ratio measures. The attributable risk is an absolute measure of the excess risk carried by the exposure, useful for judging public-health impact rather than just strength of association.

Exam trap

The odds ratio approximates the relative risk only when the disease is rare. With a common outcome the odds ratio overstates the relative risk. Case-control studies cannot yield a true relative risk because they sample on outcome, not on the population at risk.

Number Needed to Treat (NNT) and Number Needed to Harm (NNH)

These statistics translate relative measures into clinical terms — how many animals must be treated (or exposed) to produce one additional beneficial (or harmful) outcome? They bridge the gap between statistical significance and clinical relevance.

StatisticFormulaInterpretation
NNT1 ÷ ARRAnimals needed to treat to prevent one additional case — lower = more effective
NNH1 ÷ ARIAnimals needed to expose before one additional harm occurs — higher = safer

ARR (absolute risk reduction) = riskunexposed − risktreated. ARI (absolute risk increase) = riskexposed − riskunexposed. Both are derived directly from the 2×2 table.

Worked example

A shelter trial shows URI incidence of 30% in unvaccinated cats and 10% in vaccinated cats. ARR = 30% − 10% = 0.20. NNT = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5. Five cats must be vaccinated to prevent one URI case.

Exam trap

A low NNT is good (fewer animals needed per benefit). A low NNH is bad (harm occurs with very few exposures). Do not confuse direction. Also: two interventions with identical RR can have very different NNTs if baseline risk differs — always check the absolute risk difference.

Mortality and Morbidity

Three death metrics that look similar and are routinely confused. The entire distinction is the denominator.

Death metrics ref

MetricNumeratorDenominatorTells you
Crude (all-cause) mortality rateDeaths from any causeTotal population at risk of dyingOverall death burden
Cause-specific mortality rateDeaths from one diseaseTotal population at risk of dyingPopulation threat of one disease
Case fatality rateDeaths from one diseaseConfirmed cases of that diseaseVirulence or severity
High-yield trap

Cause-specific mortality and case fatality rate share a numerator but differ in denominator. Cause-specific mortality uses the whole population; case fatality rate uses only confirmed cases. A disease can have a high case fatality rate (very lethal once caught) yet a low cause-specific mortality (few animals catch it).

Worked example

Ten dogs are confirmed with parvovirus in an outbreak and four die. Case fatality rate = 4 / 10 = 40%. If those 10 cases occurred among 200 dogs at risk, the cause-specific mortality = 4 / 200 = 2%.

Morbidity and the population approach ref

Morbidity is the occurrence of illness, captured by incidence or prevalence. Managing a shelter population means shifting from individual diagnostics to herd-scale reasoning.

Surveillance

Surveillance is the ongoing systematic collection and interpretation of health data so problems are caught early and interventions can be judged. It is the population equivalent of an examination.

Types of surveillance ref

  • Passive: the system waits for cases or reports to come to it. Cheap but under-detects.
  • Active: the team deliberately seeks out cases (for example, routine intake testing or daily walk-throughs). More complete, more resource-intensive.
  • Syndromic: tracks clinical syndromes (for example, coughing dogs) rather than confirmed diagnoses, for earlier warning.
  • Sentinel: monitors a defined subset as an early indicator for the wider population.

The sensitivity of a surveillance system is the proportion of true cases it actually detects. A passive system usually has lower sensitivity than an active one.

Natural history of disease ref

The natural history of disease is the course a disease follows from onset to its clinical endpoint — recovery, chronic disease, disability, or death — in the absence of intervention. Two phases matter for exam purposes:

  • Prepathogenesis period: the stage before pathologic change begins, where host, agent, and environment interact and susceptibility is established, but no disease process has yet started.
  • Pathogenesis period: begins once pathologic changes start, running from subclinical (preclinical) disease through clinical disease to an outcome.
Link to frequency

Natural history supplies the duration term in the incidence-prevalence relationship on the Disease Frequency tab. A disease with a long natural history (chronic course) accumulates high prevalence relative to its incidence; a short, rapidly resolving or rapidly fatal natural history keeps prevalence low.

Endemic, epidemic, and the epidemic curve ref

Endemic is the usual, expected baseline level of a disease in a population. An epidemic (outbreak) is occurrence clearly in excess of that expected level. Plotting cases by time of onset produces an epidemic curve, whose shape suggests the mode of spread.

Curve shape points to mechanism: one peak for a shared source, repeating waves for animal-to-animal spread. Original construction.

Two further shapes complete the four classic patterns:

Continuous exposure produces a broad hump instead of a sharp spike; intermittent exposure produces ragged, unevenly-spaced clusters rather than the propagated curve's regular one-incubation-period spacing. Original construction.

During an investigation a clear case definition lets cases be counted consistently by time, place and animal, and attack rates compared across subgroups point to the highest-risk group and the likely source.

Reproduction number ref

Basic reproduction number (R₀): the expected number of secondary cases arising from one case in a population that is wholly susceptible. It is dimensionless, describes the pathogen's inherent transmissibility under given conditions, and — because it assumes no existing immunity — is not itself changed by vaccination.

Effective reproduction number (R): the actual number of secondary cases a typical case generates in the population as it currently stands, given existing immunity, vaccination coverage, and control measures already in place. Unlike R₀, R moves over the course of an outbreak.

Exam trap

R₀ is a fixed property of the pathogen and setting, not a real-time metric — it does not fall as vaccination coverage rises. R is the moving figure, and driving R below 1 (through vaccination, isolation, or other control measures) is what ends transmission, even though R₀ for that pathogen is unchanged.

Disease outbreak investigation ref

A structured sequence, adapted from the CDC field-epidemiology framework, keeps an investigation systematic:

  1. Prepare for fieldwork
  2. Confirm the existence of an outbreak
  3. Verify the diagnosis
  4. Establish a working case definition
  5. Find cases systematically
  6. Apply descriptive epidemiology (person, place, time)
  7. Develop and test hypotheses (analytical epidemiology)
  8. Implement control measures
  9. Communicate findings

The case definition is built from the same three components used to describe any outbreak descriptively:

ComponentCaptures
Person (animal)Vulnerability factors: age, sex, signalment, clinical signs, exposure history
PlaceGeographic or facility boundaries: ward, building, farm, region
TimeThe timeframe relative to exposure and onset
Shelter note

Control measures need not wait for the full sequence to finish. In a shelter outbreak, isolating suspect cases and pausing intake to the affected ward (step 8) often starts well before hypothesis testing (step 7) is complete, because the cost of delay — ongoing transmission — usually outweighs the cost of acting on an imperfect early case definition.

Seroprevalence and risk factors for Toxoplasma gondii exposure in Australian feral and stray cats using an in-house modified agglutination test
Liyanage KLDTD, Amery-Gale J, Uboldi AD, Adriaanse K, Firestone SM, Tonkin CJ, Jabbar A, Hufschmid J · Veterinary Parasitology · 2024 · 10.1016/j.vetpar.2024.110306

Abstract. Toxoplasma gondii is a globally distributed zoonotic protist, capable of infecting all warm-blooded animals. In Australia, cats (Felis catus) are the only definitive host capable of spreading T. gondii infection via oocysts. Free-roaming cats are widespread in Australia and can play a central role in the ecology of T. gondii. Therefore, understanding the epidemiology of this parasite in stray and feral cats is essential to understanding the potential risk of infection in animals and humans. Due to a lack of easily accessible commercial kits, an in-house modified agglutination test (MAT) was established to test for IgG antibodies against T. gondii, using cell culture-derived T. gondii tachyzoites, and compared with a commercial MAT. A total of 552 serum samples collected during 2018–2021 from stray (n = 456) and feral cats (n = 90) (samples with missing data n = 6) from four Australian states, representing different age groups of both sexes, were screened for antibodies against T. gondii. Risk factors for T. gondii infection were assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis. The in-house MAT had excellent agreement with the commercial MAT and provided a reliable and economical serological tool for T. gondii screening in animals. The overall observed seroprevalence for T. gondii in cats was 40.4% (223/552). Bodyweight (as a proxy for age), geographical location, season and whether cats were feral or stray, were factors associated with T. gondii seropositivity in cats. Sex was not found to be a risk factor for T. gondii infection in feral and stray cats. This study shows that Australian stray and feral cats have a high T. gondii seroprevalence, which may translate to significant health impacts for wildlife species, livestock and the public.

Cats Are Not Fish: A Ricker Model Fails to Account for Key Aspects of Trap–Neuter–Return Programs
Wolf, Kreisler, Levy · Animals · 2021

Abstract. In a frequently cited 2005 paper, a Ricker model was used to assess the effectiveness of trap–neuter–return (TNR) programs for managing free-roaming domestic cat populations. The model (which was originally developed for application in the management of fisheries) used data obtained from two countywide programs, and the results indicated that any population reductions, if they existed, were at best modest. In the present study, we applied the same analysis methods to data from two long-term (i.e., >20 years) TNR programs for which significant population reductions have been documented. Our results revealed that the model cannot account for some key aspects of typical TNR programs, and the wild population swings it predicts do not correspond to the relative stability of free-roaming cat populations. A Ricker model is therefore inappropriate for use in assessing the effectiveness of TNR programs. A more recently developed, stochastic model, which accounts for the movement of cats in and out of a given area, is better suited for predicting the sterilization effort necessary to reduce free-roaming cat numbers through TNR programs.

Use of Kaplan-Meier and Cox regressions in the distribution of length of stay in animal shelters for pre-specified calendar periods: Definition, computation, and examples of dog length of stay in Orange County California
Michael Loizos Mavrovouniotis · PLOS One · 2026

Abstract. Computations of length of stay in animal shelters rely on fixed animal cohorts. This is appropriate for research studies that pre-select cohorts, but it is problematic for operational assessments of animal shelters in fixed calendar periods or for comparisons among periods or shelters. Considering only the length of stay of animals whose stay ended within the study period leads to misinterpretation. The use of the Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards methods with left-truncation and right-censoring is proposed to correctly account for all animals present in the shelter for any fraction of a study period, including those that were present at the beginning and those that remain in care at the end of the period. Examples of dog length of stay in Orange County Animal Care in California show that this computation method corrects the misleading view of historically used calculations of length of stay. Statistically significant changes in length of stay are observed in 8 out of 23 quarterly periods. In a comparison of length of stay before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, the observed significant change in length of stay cannot be explained by variations in sizes and ages of incoming dogs and may be connected to operational policies that restricted visitor access. The proposed approach enables timely tracking of length of stay, accurate comparisons, and assessment of shelter practices and resource needs.
