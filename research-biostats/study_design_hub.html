Study Designs

Every design answers a different question and yields a different measure. The fastest exam hook is direction of reasoning: cohort reasons forward from exposure, case-control reasons backward from outcome, cross-sectional captures both at one instant.

Speed

The organising framework ref

  • Descriptive studies describe who, what, where and when. No comparison group, so they generate hypotheses rather than test them.
  • Analytic observational studies add a comparison group and measure an association, but the investigator only observes; the exposure is not assigned.
  • Experimental studies have the investigator assign the intervention, ideally at random. This is the only category that establishes causation directly.

Exam trap

The ecological fallacy: an association seen between group-level rates does not prove the same association holds at the individual level. A case report or series can never prove a risk factor, because it has no comparison group.

Cohort starts from exposure and follows forward; case-control starts from the outcome and looks back. Original construction.

Cohort: yields incidence and relative risk; establishes temporality; inefficient for rare outcomes. Case-control: yields the odds ratio; efficient for rare or slow diseases; prone to recall and selection bias; the odds ratio approximates relative risk only when the disease is rare. Analytic cross-sectional: yields prevalence; cannot establish temporality, so reverse causation is possible.

Longitudinal designs and repeated measurement ref

A design is longitudinal when the same units are measured more than once. That single fact drives everything else in this section: repeated observations on the same animal are correlated, so both the design and the analysis have to account for it.

Panel study versus repeated cross-sectional survey

Both produce a series of measurements over time, and they answer different questions. This is the distinction most often got wrong.

Panel studyRepeated cross-sectional survey
Who is measuredThe same individuals each roundA fresh sample each round
Can measureWithin-individual changeNet population change only
AnswersDid these individuals change?Did the population level move?
Main weaknessAttrition (drop-out), and repeated testing may itself change behaviourComposition shifts between rounds can masquerade as change
Shelter example

Surveying the same 200 foster carers every year is a panel study: it can tell you that a third of individual carers became more burnt out. Surveying a fresh 200 carers every year is a repeated cross-sectional survey: it can only tell you that overall burnout prevalence held steady, which might be because nobody changed, or because burnt-out carers quit and were replaced by fresh ones. Same headline number, opposite meaning.

Directionality: prospective, retrospective, ambidirectional

A cohort is defined by reasoning forward from exposure, not by when the investigator happened to arrive.

  • Prospective cohort: exposure is recorded now and outcomes accrue in the future. Best data quality, because you choose what to measure; slow and expensive.
  • Retrospective (historical) cohort: the investigator assembles the cohort from existing records, so exposure and outcome have both already happened. Fast and cheap, but you are limited to what was recorded, and recording is rarely complete.
  • Ambidirectional cohort: both at once. Historical records supply the past outcomes and follow-up then continues forward from today.
Exam trap

A retrospective cohort is still a cohort. It reasons forward from exposure to outcome and yields incidence and relative risk. It is not a case-control study, which reasons backward from outcome to exposure and yields an odds ratio. "Retrospective" describes the investigator's vantage point in time; "case-control" describes the direction of reasoning. Mixing the two is one of the most reliably examined confusions in this subdomain.

Repeated measures and pseudoreplication

When each animal contributes several measurements, those measurements are not independent: an anxious cat scores high on every day you assess it. Treating them as independent observations is pseudoreplication. It inflates the apparent sample size, shrinks the standard errors, and manufactures significance that is not there.

  • Two time points: paired t-test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank if the data are skewed.
  • Three or more time points: repeated-measures analysis of variance, or Friedman's test as the non-parametric analogue.
  • The general solution: a mixed-effects (multilevel) model with a random effect for the individual animal. It handles unequal numbers of observations and missing time points, which repeated-measures analysis of variance does not.
Shelter example

Cat stress scores recorded on days 1, 3, 7 and 14 for 40 cats give 160 measurements but only 40 independent animals. Analysing 160 rows as though they were 160 cats is the error.

Age, period and cohort effects

Three different explanations for a change over time, routinely confused:

  • Age effect: the change happens because animals get older.
  • Period effect: something affects everyone alive at that moment, regardless of age or when they entered, such as an outbreak year or a change in the law.
  • Cohort effect: something specific to the group that entered together and travels with them for life.
Shelter example

Dogs acquired during the 2020 to 2021 pandemic surge show more separation-related behaviour. That is a cohort effect: it is not that dogs of that age are generally worse (age), nor that this year is generally bad for all dogs (period), but that the socialisation environment of the group that entered at that time was unusual, and it stays with them.

Exam trap

Age, period and cohort are mathematically entangled: cohort = period minus age. Knowing any two fixes the third, so the three effects cannot all be estimated at once from observational data without adding an outside assumption. Any paper claiming to have cleanly separated all three has assumed something; look for what.

Time series and interrupted time series

A time series is repeated measurement of a population at regular intervals. Its three characteristic features are a trend (the underlying direction of travel), seasonality (a repeating within-year pattern, which in shelters is dominated by kitten season), and autocorrelation (this month resembles last month, so points are not independent).

The interrupted time series is the strong quasi-experimental design for evaluating something you could not randomise, such as a policy or programme launch. You model the pre-intervention trend, project it forward, and test for a change in level (an immediate step) and a change in slope (a different trajectory afterwards).

Interrupted time series. The pre-intervention trend is modelled and projected forward, so the effect is measured as the departure from that projection, in level and in slope. Original construction.
Exam trap

Comparing a raw mean for the year before against the year after is not an interrupted time series and will mislead you twice over. If intake was already falling, a simple before-and-after difference credits the programme with the pre-existing trend. If the two windows straddle kitten season differently, seasonality alone can produce or erase the whole effect.

Experimental studies ref

The investigator assigns the intervention. A randomised controlled trial allocates subjects at random to intervention or control. Randomisation balances known and unknown confounders, which is the source of the RCT's causal strength.

  • Control group: comparison without the intervention (placebo or standard of care).
  • Blinding: single-blind (participants or assessors unaware of allocation, not both), double-blind (both participants and investigators unaware), triple-blind (the outcome assessors or data analysts are unaware too). Each added layer removes another route for the placebo, observer or analyst to bias the result.
  • Intention-to-treat: analyse subjects in their originally assigned group regardless of protocol completion, preserving the benefits of randomisation.

Cluster randomisation

Sometimes the individual animal cannot be the unit of randomisation. You randomise groups instead: kennel wards, foster homes, clinic days, whole shelters. Two situations force it:

  • The intervention is delivered at group level. A ward-wide sanitation protocol or a change to the ventilation cannot be given to some dogs in a ward and not others.
  • Contamination is unavoidable. If treated and untreated animals share air, staff or equipment, the control group is no longer a clean comparison.

The cost is statistical. Animals within a cluster are more alike than animals in different clusters, quantified by the intracluster correlation coefficient. So the effective sample size is smaller than the number of animals, and the required sample size must be inflated by the design effect, which equals 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × intracluster correlation coefficient. Analysis must respect the clustering too, either at cluster level or with a mixed-effects model.

A stepped-wedge design is the cluster trial worth knowing for shelter work: every cluster eventually receives the intervention, but the order in which they cross over is randomised. It suits a programme that must be rolled out to everyone anyway, and it lets each cluster act as its own control before it crosses over.

Exam trap

Analysing a cluster-randomised trial as though animals had been randomised individually is the classic unit of analysis error. It understates the standard errors and produces spuriously significant results. If randomisation happened at ward level, the analysis has to acknowledge the ward. Randomising 12 wards is closer to a study of 12 units than a study of 300 dogs.

Hierarchy of evidence and EBVM ref

Evidence hierarchy for therapy and causation questions in clinical populations. Original construction.
Off the ladder

Laboratory, in vitro and challenge studies in purpose-bred animals do not sit on this ladder at all. They answer a different question, mechanism or efficacy under controlled conditions, and they trade external validity for internal validity: tightly controlled, but the animals, the pathogen dose and the housing are not the shelter. A challenge study showing a vaccine protects specific-pathogen-free kittens under a uniform intranasal dose does not tell you the protection rate in a shelter with mixed ages, maternal antibody and variable exposure. Human-medicine pyramids place animal work at the base because it is a step removed from the human patient; in veterinary medicine the species is the target, so the limitation is the artificial conditions, not the species.

EBVM, the five A's: Ask, Acquire, Appraise, Apply, Assess.

Exam trap

The pyramid ranks designs, not individual studies. A large, well-conducted cohort study can be stronger evidence than a small randomised controlled trial with unblinded outcome assessment and 40% loss to follow-up. Design sets the starting point; conduct decides where the study actually lands.

Note also that the case series and case report tier still contains primary observation, whereas expert opinion and the narrative review contain none. That is why they are separated here even though they are often drawn as one block.

Types of review ref

Four things are all called "reviews" and they are not interchangeable. The abstract usually tells you which one you are reading in its first sentence.

TypeSearchQuestion it answersAppraises quality?
Narrative reviewInformal, at the author's discretionWhat does an expert think about this topic?No
Scoping reviewSystematic and reproducibleWhat evidence exists, and where are the gaps?No, by design
Systematic reviewExhaustive, prespecified protocolWhat does the whole body of evidence say?Yes, formal risk-of-bias assessment
Meta-analysisInherits the systematic review's searchWhat is the single pooled effect estimate?Yes, plus statistical pooling
Exam trap

A systematic review is not just a thorough narrative review. The defining feature is the prespecified, reproducible protocol, written before the search, so that another team following it would retrieve the same studies. Thoroughness alone does not make a review systematic. Equally, not every systematic review contains a meta-analysis; pooling is only legitimate when the studies are similar enough to combine.

Scoping review

A scoping review deliberately does not grade quality. Finding that only three small studies address a question, and that all three are uncontrolled, is a legitimate and useful result. In shelter medicine, where the literature is thin, scoping reviews are common and are often the honest answer to a new question.

Systematic review and meta-analysis machinery ref

  • PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome): the framework that structures the question and generates the search terms. A question that cannot be written as a PICO is usually not yet answerable.
  • PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses): the reporting standard. Its flow diagram accounts for every record from identification through screening and exclusion to final inclusion, so a reader can see what was discarded and why.
  • Heterogeneity: the extent to which studies genuinely differ in their effects, rather than differing by chance. The I-squared statistic expresses the percentage of the variation that is due to real differences between studies. High heterogeneity is a signal that pooling into one number may be misleading.
  • Forest plot: one row per study. The box is that study's effect estimate and its size reflects the study's weight; the horizontal line is the confidence interval; the diamond at the foot is the pooled estimate. The vertical line of no effect sits at 1 for ratio measures such as relative risk and odds ratio, and at 0 for differences.
  • Funnel plot and publication bias: effect size is plotted against study precision. Because small studies scatter widely and large studies cluster tightly, a complete literature forms a symmetrical inverted funnel. Asymmetry, typically a missing corner of small studies showing no effect, suggests publication bias: negative and small studies are less likely to be written up, submitted and accepted.
Exam trap

Publication bias inflates the pooled estimate of a meta-analysis, because the missing studies are disproportionately the ones that found nothing. A meta-analysis sitting at the apex of the pyramid is only as good as the literature it could find, and that literature is systematically incomplete.

Critical appraisal and GRADE ref

Critical appraisal is the structured judgement of a single study, and it reduces to three questions:

  • Are the results valid? Internal validity: allocation, blinding, follow-up, confounding control, appropriate analysis.
  • What are the results? The size of the effect and the precision around it, not merely whether a p-value cleared 0.05.
  • Will they help my animals? External validity: were the study's animals, setting and comparator close enough to a shelter population for the finding to transfer?

GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) grades a whole body of evidence rather than one paper. Randomised trials enter at high certainty and observational studies at low, and the rating then moves:

  • Rated down for risk of bias, inconsistency between studies, indirectness (wrong population, comparator or outcome), imprecision, and suspected publication bias.
  • Rated up for a very large effect, a dose-response gradient, or where plausible confounding would have worked against the effect that was still observed.
Why this matters here

GRADE is the formal expression of the trap noted above: design is where a study starts, not where it ends. This is also why observational shelter data is not disqualified in advance. A large intake cohort showing a strong, dose-dependent association, in circumstances where confounding would have diluted rather than created it, can be rated up.

Statistical Inference

Hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and the two ways a test can be wrong. The traps here are conceptual, not arithmetic.

Hypothesis testing ref

  • Null hypothesis (H0): no difference or association (for example, relative risk = 1).
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1): a difference or association exists.
  • p-value: the probability of the observed data, or something more extreme, if the null hypothesis were true, compared to a preset alpha (commonly 0.05).
High-yield trap

A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and not the probability the result occurred by chance. It only says the data are unlikely under the null.

Statistical vs clinical significance

With a large enough sample, a trivially small, clinically meaningless difference can reach statistical significance. Always judge effect size, not just whether p is below 0.05.

Confidence intervals ref

A 95% confidence interval is the range that would contain the true value 95% of the time on repeated sampling. It conveys both direction and magnitude, which a bare p-value does not.

  • For a ratio measure (relative risk, odds ratio) the null value is 1. An interval that includes 1 is not significant.
  • For a difference measure (mean or risk difference) the null value is 0. An interval that includes 0 is not significant.
  • Interval width is precision; larger samples and lower variability narrow it.

Error types and power ref

The two ways an inference can be wrong. Original construction.
  • Type I (alpha): a false positive; concluding an effect exists when it does not.
  • Type II (beta): a false negative; missing a real effect.
  • Power = 1 minus beta; conventionally 0.80 or higher. It increases with larger sample size, larger true effect, higher alpha, and lower variability. Sample size is the main lever.

Mnemonic: Type I is the false alarm (cried wolf); Type II is the miss (missed the wolf).

Bias and Validity

Bias is systematic error built into the study; it does not shrink with a larger sample. Random error does. Keeping that line clear answers a surprising share of items.

Bias ref

BiasWhat happens
Selection biasSubjects selected in a way that distorts the exposure to outcome relationship
Information (measurement) biasSystematic error in how exposure or outcome is measured or recorded
MisclassificationA form of information bias. Non-differential (errors equal across groups) usually biases toward the null; differential (errors differ by group) can bias either way

Exam trap

Random error reflects chance, shrinks with larger samples, and shows up as wider confidence intervals. Bias is systematic and is not fixed by a larger sample.

Confounding and effect modification ref

A confounder must meet all three criteria:

  • associated with the exposure,
  • an independent risk factor for the outcome, and
  • not on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome.

Control by design: randomisation (balances unknown confounders too), restriction, matching. Control by analysis: stratification (Mantel-Haenszel) and multivariable regression.

Clarification

Effect modification is different from confounding. Here the true effect genuinely differs across levels of a third variable (for example, a handling intervention helps adults but not puppies). It is a real finding to report by stratum, not a nuisance to adjust away.

Validity, reliability and sampling ref

  • Internal validity: the study correctly measures what it set out to, free of bias and confounding.
  • External validity (generalisability): results apply beyond the sample. A tightly controlled study can have high internal but low external validity.
  • Reliability (precision) is consistency on repetition; validity (accuracy) is closeness to truth. A measure can be reliable but not valid. Observer agreement is quantified with the kappa statistic, which corrects for chance agreement.
  • Measurement equivalence across groups: a 2022 validation study of a new human-animal bond measure, the Family Bondedness Scale, used multi-group confirmatory factor analysis and found the scale's scores were equivalent between cat and dog owners — a specific form of validity evidence (the instrument measures the same underlying construct comparably across groups) that the same study noted is often missing from human-animal-bond measures generally (Nugent, Frontiers, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Probability sampling (supports inference): simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster. Non-probability sampling (weaker): convenience, purposive. Convenience samples are a common source of selection bias and a frequent distractor for "what weakens generalisability".

Tests and Measures

Diagnostic test characteristics with worked numbers, the measure each design produces, choosing a statistical test, and the line between association and causation.

Diagnostic test evaluation ref

A diagnostic accuracy study compares a test against a reference standard in a 2x2 table.

The diagnostic 2x2. Sensitivity and specificity read down the columns; predictive values read across the rows. Original construction.
  • Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN): the proportion of truly diseased animals correctly identified as positive (true positive rate) — how well the test catches disease that's really there. A property of the test, prevalence-independent.
  • Specificity = TN / (TN + FP): the proportion of truly healthy animals correctly identified as negative (true negative rate) — how well the test clears animals that are really healthy. Also prevalence-independent.
  • PPV = TP / (TP + FP): the proportion of positive results that are true positives — answers "if this test is positive, what's the probability the animal truly has the disease?" Depends on prevalence.
  • NPV = TN / (TN + FN): the proportion of negative results that are true negatives — answers "if this test is negative, what's the probability the animal is truly free of disease?" Depends on prevalence.

Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)  ·  Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
PPV = TP / (TP + FP)  ·  NPV = TN / (TN + FN)

Worked example

100 truly infected cats: the test flags 90. 100 truly uninfected cats: the test flags 5. Then Sensitivity = 90/100 = 90%, Specificity = 95/100 = 95%. If only 20 of 1000 screened cats are truly infected (2% prevalence): true positives = 18, false positives = 49, so PPV = 18/(18+49) = 27%, despite a sensitive and specific test.

High-yield trap

As prevalence falls, PPV falls (more positives are false positives) even though sensitivity and specificity are unchanged. This is why low-prevalence intake screening (for example, FeLV) needs confirmatory testing. Mnemonics: SnNout (sensitive, Negative rules out), SpPin (specific, Positive rules in).

Measures of association by design ref note

Full calculations live in your Data and Analysis material; here is only the design-to-measure mapping.

  • Relative risk: ratio of incidence in exposed to unexposed; from cohort and experimental designs. Null = 1.
  • Odds ratio: from case-control designs; approximates relative risk when disease is rare. Null = 1.
  • Prevalence ratio: from cross-sectional designs.
  • Attributable risk (risk difference): absolute excess risk in the exposed; from cohort and experimental designs.

Test by test, with shelter examples ref

The table above is the quick lookup; this section is the reasoning behind each row, with the shelter-medicine example you are most likely to meet on the exam.

Comparing group means (continuous outcome)

  • Independent t-test: compares the mean of a continuous, roughly normally distributed outcome between exactly two independent groups. It assumes independent observations, approximate normality (which matters most at small n, because the Central Limit Theorem rescues you at larger n), and equal variances. Welch's t-test relaxes that last assumption and is often the safer default. Shelter example: mean length of stay in single versus group housing.
  • Paired t-test: use this instead when the two measurements come from the same animals (for example weight before and after a deworming protocol), because the observations are no longer independent.
  • One-way ANOVA: the same idea extended to three or more groups. You use it rather than running multiple t-tests because each pairwise t-test carries its own false-positive risk, and stacking them inflates the family-wise error rate. ANOVA gives one omnibus test of "do any group means differ"; if it is significant you follow with post-hoc comparisons (Tukey HSD, Bonferroni) to identify which pairs differ. Shelter example: mean length of stay across owner-surrender, stray, and transfer intake.

Comparing proportions (categorical outcome)

  • Chi-square: tests whether the distribution of counts across categories departs from what you would expect if the variables were independent. The standard assumption is expected cell counts of at least 5. Shelter example: adoption versus euthanasia outcome by feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) status.
  • Fisher's exact test: switch to this when cells are sparse; it computes the probability directly rather than relying on the chi-square approximation.

Measuring association (two continuous variables)

  • Pearson correlation (r, ranging -1 to +1): quantifies the strength and direction of a linear relationship; for inference it assumes linearity, normally distributed residuals, and constant variance.
  • Linear regression: goes a step further, modelling the outcome as a function of one or more predictors. It gives you a slope (effect size), the ability to predict, and the ability to adjust for confounders. Shelter example: weight regressed on age, or surgery time on body weight.

Modelling a binary outcome with several predictors

Logistic regression is the workhorse of shelter outcome research, because most shelter outcomes are binary (adopted or not, live-release or not, returned or not) and you almost always want to adjust for multiple predictors at once. It models the log-odds of the outcome, and its coefficients exponentiate into odds ratios, which are directly interpretable.

Skewed or ordinal data (non-parametric)

These tests work on ranks rather than raw values, so they make no normality assumption. Each one is the non-parametric analogue of a parametric test:

  • Mann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum) replaces the two-group t-test.
  • Kruskal-Wallis replaces one-way ANOVA.
  • Spearman's rho replaces Pearson and detects a monotonic rather than strictly linear association.
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank replaces the paired t-test, and Friedman's test replaces repeated-measures analysis of variance, for the repeated-measurement designs below.

This matters for shelter work specifically because length-of-stay data are classically right-skewed — which is exactly why the Shelter link box above flags survival methods, and why a Mann-Whitney is often more defensible than a t-test on raw length of stay.

Time to an event

  • Kaplan-Meier estimates the survival (or "still in care") curve over time and, crucially, handles censoring: animals still in the shelter at study end, or whose follow-up ends before the event, contribute the information they have without being dropped or treated as if the event occurred.
  • Log-rank test: compares two or more Kaplan-Meier curves.
  • Cox proportional hazards: the regression extension, giving hazard ratios while adjusting for covariates.

A plain mean length of stay is biased precisely because it ignores censored animals — the methodological point the Shelter link box above is making.

Repeated measurements on the same animals

Every test above assumes each row is a different animal. Once the same animal appears more than once, you need the paired or repeated version, or the whole test is invalid:

  • Paired t-test for two time points, because what is being tested is the mean of the within-animal differences, not the difference between two independent means. Pairing removes between-animal variability, which is why the paired test is usually more powerful for the same number of animals.
  • Repeated-measures analysis of variance for three or more time points. Its weakness is that it needs a complete set of measurements on every animal: one missing time point and the animal is dropped entirely.
  • Mixed-effects (multilevel) models are the general answer, and increasingly the expected one. A random effect for animal says each animal has its own baseline; fixed effects carry the predictors you actually want to test. They tolerate missing and unequally spaced time points, and the same machinery absorbs clustering of any kind, including littermates, households and kennel wards.

Shelter example: stress scores on days 1, 3, 7 and 14 in 40 cats housed under two enrichment protocols. The comparison of interest is the protocol, but the model has to know that the 160 rows come from 40 cats. See the study-design caveat at the foot of this section, which is the same point stated from the design side.

Exam framework

The simple test answers "do these groups differ?"; the regression answers "do they differ after accounting for everything else?"

Study-design caveat

Every test above assumes independent observations, and shelter data routinely violate this: littermates, animals from the same household, or repeat surrenders are correlated. When observations cluster, you move to mixed-effects (multilevel) models or, for matched case-control designs, conditional logistic regression. This is the link between study design and test choice that the table cannot show: the design determines whether the standard test is even valid, not just which one you reach for.

Association vs causation ref

The Bradford Hill considerations help judge causality: temporality, strength, dose-response, consistency, plausibility, coherence, experimental evidence, specificity, analogy.

Exam trap

Temporality (cause precedes effect) is the one essential criterion. The rest are supporting considerations, not a checklist that all must be met. Association is never proof of causation.

“Maybe we should think outside the box?” prioritisation of issues with UK not-for-profit canine health and welfare research funding using Delphi expert consensus and gap analysis
Skipper · PLOS ONE · 2024

Abstract. Over fifty participants, who together possessed broad research, veterinary and front-line expertise from across the canine health and welfare sector, contributed to a modified Delphi study to identify the highest priority research topics in UK canine health and welfare, the highest priorities for future research approaches, and the highest priorities for future reform in research processes and infrastructure, through group consensus. Further analysis also compared the prioritisation of selected research topics to the actual levels of research funding they previously received, through comparison with historical data. Most of the identified highest priority issues relating to canine health and welfare and its research concerned various aspects of the human-canine relationship, such as ownership or behavioural issues. Participants strongly emphasised the complexity of interrelated factors that impact the welfare of both dogs and people. Research topics identified as previously ‘most underfunded’ all concerned real-world canine welfare issues, particularly emphasising the breeding and supply of dogs. A supplementary analysis of historical research funding (2012–2022) for common chronic disorders in primary care practice, another identified highest priority topic, identified periodontal disease, anal sac disorders, overgrown nails and patellar luxation as the ‘most underfunded’ conditions. Most of the identified highest priority research approaches and methodologies concerned real-world design and execution aspects of canine health and welfare research, such as impact and engagement, with a strong focus on research investigating the human factors in canine welfare. Aspects of research funding infrastructure that were considered highest priority for future change mostly concerned increased transparency of funding processes and increased collaboration between stakeholder groups throughout the funding sector, which was strongly supported. Overall, these findings emphasise the importance of considering and including human factors and real-world impact, where appropriate, as key elements for optimising the relevance of canine health and welfare research.

Finding Yourself in the Arena of Scientific Writing The Journey From Idea to Publication in Shelter and Community Medicine
From Idea to Publication in Shelter and Community Medicine · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2023

Abstract. Irrespective of discipline, scientific study relies upon systematic reasoning from observations to formulate and refine hypotheses and develop theories that can explain documented phenomena. Our understanding of science and its influence on the world around us is ever evolving, yet science cannot advance without clear communication to disseminate findings that result from reproducible research. Veterinary and animal care team members can contribute significantly to the advancement of science by sharing ideas, experiences, perspectives, case management decisions, and patient outcomes. The uniqueness of shelter medicine, with recent emphasis on how to maintain animals within their homes, offers a wealth of observations and discoveries that are worthy of publication. For those in clinical practice in shelter and community medicine, the primary obstacles to publishing are time, confidence, and lack of familiarity or experience with the structure and style of scientific writing. Such barriers can be overcome through a combination of graduated exposure, mentoring, patience, and practice. This article explores the journey from idea to publication to encourage those in shelter and community medicine who generate and have access to vital data to come forward. Writing to publish is a teachable skill, one that the veterinary profession relies upon to translate scientific inquiry into print capable of broadening global perspectives and influencing the clinical practice of tomorrow.

A Measurement Equivalence Study of the Family Bondedness Scale: Measurement Equivalence Between Cat and Dog Owners
Nugent · Frontiers · 2022

Abstract. About 38.4% of U.S. households include a dog, and 25.4% a cat, as pets, and a recent poll suggested over 90% of pet owners feel their companion animal is a family member. Numerous studies have suggested pet ownership has physical, mental, and social health benefits, though much of this research has yielded mixed results. Results of a recent review suggested significant measurement problems in human-animal interaction (HAI) and human-animal bond (HAB) research, including the absence of validity evidence, overly long measures, lack of evidence for measurement equivalence across species of pets, and measures lacking a basis in important psychological, family, and attachment theories. This article describes the development and results of a measurement equivalence study of a new measure of the HAB called the family bondedness scale (FBS). This scale, and the research results, address multiple gaps in HAB measurement. Results of multi-group confirmatory factor analyses with multiple covariates indicated the scores on the FBS showed equivalence between cat and dog owners. The use of the FBS in both veterinary research and practice, as well as in research and practice in other disciplines, such as social work and psychology, are considered.

Pet ownership, feelings of loneliness, and mood in people affected by the first COVID-19 lockdown
Martinez-Caja · J of Vet Behavior · 2022

Abstract. The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic has caused dramatic changes to our lifestyle, particularly affecting our ability to interact “in person” with our social network. These changes have had a detrimental effect on the mental welfare of the global population. The international questionnaire “Pets in Lockdown” was designed to investigate whether feelings of loneliness were affecting the mood of people during the COVID-19 lockdown and whether pet ownership may have had a positive influence on both loneliness and general mood. As expected, higher loneliness scores were associated with higher negative and lower positive affective states. In addition, lower loneliness scores were associated with pet ownership and living with other people, but not with more frequent interactions with people from outside the household, suggesting that physical and close contact has an important role in decreasing feelings of loneliness. Besides the effects on the loneliness score, pet ownership was not associated with positive or negative affective states. The strength of the attachment to animals, measured as the amount of comfort that people obtain from their pets, was stronger in people with potentially limited access to affiliative physical human contact and was associated with both higher positive and negative affective states. Additionally, people obtained significantly more comfort from dogs and horses compared with other pet species. The results suggest that during the confinement period, pets may have benefited people with smaller social networks by alleviating loneliness and offering comfort and embodied close contact.

Dog behaviour classification with movement sensors placed on the harness and the collar
Kumpulainen, P. · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Dog owners’ understanding of the daily behaviour of their dogs may be enhanced by movement measurements that can detect repeatable dog behaviour, such as levels of daily activity and rest as well as their changes. The aim of this study was to evaluate the performance of supervised machine learning methods utilising accelerometer and gyroscope data provided by wearable movement sensors in classification of seven typical dog activities in a semi-controlled test situation. Forty-five middle to large sized dogs participated in the study. Two sensor devices were attached to each dog, one on the back of the dog in a harness and one on the neck collar. Altogether 54 features were extracted from the acceleration and gyroscope signals divided in two-second segments. The performance of four classifiers were compared using features derived from both sensor modalities. and from the acceleration data only. The results were promising; the movement sensor at the back yielded up to 91 % accuracy in classifying the dog activities and the sensor placed at the collar yielded 75 % accuracy at best. Including the gyroscope features improved the classification accuracy by 0.7–2.6 %, depending on the classifier and the sensor location. The most distinct activity was sniffing, whereas the static postures (lying on chest, sitting and standing) were the most challenging behaviours to classify, especially from the data of the neck collar sensor. The data used in this article as well as the signal processing scripts are openly available in Mendeley Data, https://doi.org/10.17632/vxhx934tbn.1.

Short-term storage conditions do not affect canine fecal cortisol and secretory immunoglobulin A concentrations
Lenz OC, Powell L, Reinhard CL, Watson B · Scientific Reports · 2026

Abstract. Fecal cortisol and secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) are frequently used biomarkers in animal welfare, wildlife conservation, and other fields. However, these research settings often lack immediate access to a -80 degrees C freezer or other ultra-low temperature storage equipment. This study aims to determine whether canine fecal cortisol and sIgA concentrations remain stable over 24 h at either ambient temperature or on wet ice. Fecal samples were collected from 10 adult dogs housed in two Philadelphia area animal shelters in May and June of 2024. After aliquoting, baseline samples were frozen on dry ice within 15 minutes of defecation. Remaining aliquots were stored at ambient temperature (15-21 degrees C) or on wet ice (4 degrees C) for 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, or 24 hours before freezing on dry ice. Cortisol and sIgA concentrations were quantified using ELISAs and analyzed with a linear mixed effects model. No significant differences were found between time points or storage conditions. These data show that concentrations of fecal canine cortisol and sIgA remain stable if stored unfrozen for 24 hours. Immediate freezing at -80 degrees C does not appear to be necessary for accurate quantification of these analytes. This knowledge removes logistical and regulatory barriers to conducting quality noninvasive research in animal shelter, clinical, and field settings.
