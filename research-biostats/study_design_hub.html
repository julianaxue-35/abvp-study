The big picture ref

How a study is set up decides what question it can answer and what number it produces. The easiest way to sort designs is by direction of reasoning:

  • Cohort: starts from the exposure and looks forward to see who gets sick.
  • Case-control: starts from the outcome (disease) and looks backward to see what they were exposed to.
  • Cross-sectional: a snapshot of everything at one moment in time.

Then there are three families:

  • Descriptive: just describes what is happening. No comparison group.
  • Analytic observational: compares groups, but the researcher only watches. Nothing is assigned.
  • Experimental: the researcher assigns the treatment, ideally at random. This is the only family that proves cause and effect directly.

1. Descriptive studies (just looking around) ref

These studies don't compare groups; they just describe. They are great for spotting brand-new problems, but they cannot prove cause and effect.

  • Case report / case series: one unusual patient, or a small cluster of sick animals. Example: "We saw 3 shelter cats with an unusual rash." Limitation: no comparison group, so you don't know whether it is normal or rare.
  • Cross-sectional survey: checks how much disease is present right now (prevalence). Example: testing every dog in the shelter for fleas today.
  • Ecological study: compares whole groups using existing data. Example: shelters in rainy states have higher rates of lungworm.
Exam trap: ecological fallacy

A trend in a group doesn't mean it applies to an individual animal. Rainy cities having more lungworm doesn't tell you about your dry-housed dog. Also, a case report or case series can never prove a risk factor, because it has no comparison group.

The researcher doesn't assign treatments; they watch what happens naturally. These are the highest-yield items in the subdomain.

3. Longitudinal designs (measuring the same thing over time) ref

When you track subjects across several points in time, the way you collect the data matters. Repeated measurements on the same animal are alike (correlated), so both the design and the analysis have to allow for that.

Panel study versus repeated cross-sectional survey

  • Panel study: measures the exact same animals or people over time. Example: tracking the stress scores of the same 20 foster cats every month. It shows true individual change. Weak point: animals drop out.
  • Repeated cross-sectional survey: surveys a brand new batch each round. It only shows change in the overall level.
Shelter example

If burnout looks the same every year in a repeated cross-sectional survey, you might think nobody changed. In reality, burnt-out workers might be quitting and being replaced by fresh, energetic ones. Surveying the same 200 carers each year (a panel) would show who actually changed.

Prospective, retrospective, ambidirectional

  • Prospective cohort: exposure is recorded now and outcomes happen in the future. Best data quality, but slow and expensive.
  • Retrospective cohort: built from past records, so exposure and outcome have already happened. Fast and cheap, but you only have what was written down.
  • Ambidirectional cohort: past records plus continued follow-up from today.
Exam trap: the retrospective cohort

A retrospective cohort uses old records but still reasons forward from exposure to disease. It is still a cohort study (it gives relative risk), not a case-control study. "Retrospective" is about when the researcher arrived; "case-control" is about the direction of reasoning. Examiners love this trick.

Repeated measures and pseudoreplication

An anxious cat scores high on every day you assess it, so its measurements are not independent. Treating them as independent is called pseudoreplication: it makes your sample look bigger than it is and creates significance that isn't real.

  • Two time points: paired t-test (Wilcoxon signed-rank if the data are skewed).
  • Three or more time points: repeated-measures ANOVA (Friedman test if skewed).
  • Best general answer: a mixed-effects model with a random effect for each animal. It copes with missing time points.
Shelter example

Stress scores on days 1, 3, 7 and 14 for 40 cats give 160 measurements but only 40 independent animals. Analysing them as 160 cats is the error.

Age, period and cohort effects

Why did something change over time?

  • Age: they got older.
  • Period: something happened this year that affected everyone alive right then (an outbreak year, a new law).
  • Cohort: a group that entered together carries a unique trait with them. Example: puppies adopted during the 2020 pandemic lockdown showing more separation-related behaviour.
Exam trap

Cohort = period − age, so the three are tangled together and cannot all be separated at once without an extra assumption.

Time series and interrupted time series

A time series measures a population at regular intervals. It has a trend (the overall direction), seasonality (a repeating yearly pattern, which in shelters means kitten season) and autocorrelation (this month looks like last month).

An interrupted time series is the way to judge a programme you couldn't randomise. You model the trend before the programme, project it forward, and test whether the level (an immediate step) or the slope (the direction afterwards) changed.

Interrupted time series. The pre-intervention trend is modelled and projected forward, so the effect is measured as the departure from that projection, in level and in slope. Original construction.
Exam trap

Comparing the average for the year before with the year after is not an interrupted time series. If intake was already falling, the programme gets credit for a decline that was happening anyway, and kitten season alone can create or hide an effect.

4. Experimental studies (intervening) ref

The gold standard, because the researcher assigns the treatment, ideally at random.

  • Randomised controlled trial (RCT): animals are randomly put into a treatment group or a control group. Random assignment balances out hidden differences, so it proves true cause and effect.
  • Control group: the comparison group (placebo or standard care).
  • Blinding: people who don't know who got what can't bias the result. Single-blind = one party unaware, double-blind = both participants and investigators unaware, triple-blind = the data analysts are unaware too.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis: if an animal drops out or is switched to a different treatment, you still analyse it in the group it was originally assigned to. This keeps the randomisation fair.

Cluster randomisation

Sometimes you can't randomise individual animals. You can't give half the dogs in one kennel room a special air filter and the other half nothing. Instead you randomise entire rooms, wards, foster homes or whole shelters. This is needed when:

  • the intervention is given to a whole group (a ward-wide sanitation protocol), or
  • treated and untreated animals would share air, staff or equipment, so the control group wouldn't be clean.

The cost: animals in the same cluster are alike, so you effectively have fewer independent animals than it looks. Inflate the sample size by the design effect = 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × ICC (intracluster correlation coefficient).

Stepped wedge: every cluster eventually gets the intervention, but the order is randomised. It suits a programme that is going to be rolled out to everyone anyway.

Exam trap: unit of analysis error

If you randomised 12 wards, you can't analyse as though you randomised 300 dogs. That makes results look more significant than they really are.

Types of review ref

Four different things are called "reviews". The first sentence of an abstract usually tells you which one it is.

Exam trap

A systematic review is not just a thorough narrative review. What makes it systematic is a plan written before the search that another team could follow to find the same studies. Not every systematic review includes a meta-analysis; studies are only pooled when they are similar enough.

Scoping review

A scoping review deliberately does not grade quality. Finding that only three small, uncontrolled studies exist is a useful result. In shelter medicine the evidence is thin, so scoping reviews are common.

Systematic review and meta-analysis machinery ref

1. PICO (setting up the question)

Before researchers even touch a computer to search for papers, they need a strict blueprint so they don't get lost. That blueprint is PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome):

  • Population: who are we looking at? Example: shelter puppies under 6 months old.
  • Intervention: what treatment are we testing? Example: intranasal bordetella vaccine.
  • Comparison: what are we comparing it against? Example: no vaccine, or a placebo.
  • Outcome: what are we actually measuring? Example: did they get kennel cough?

2. PRISMA (the paper trail flowchart)

When someone writes a review, people want to know: did you cherry-pick the studies that proved your point, or did you look at everything? PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) is the strict checklist and flowchart. It shows a map from start to finish, for example: "We typed our search into Google and found 1,200 papers. We threw away 1,100 junk ones, left out 50 duplicates, and ended up analysing 50 solid papers." It lets anyone check how papers were filtered out and why.

3. Heterogeneity and I² (the apples-to-oranges check)

When you combine 20 different studies, you have to ask: are these studies even talking about the same thing?

  • Heterogeneity means how much the studies genuinely disagree or differ from each other. Example: one study tested a vaccine on stressed shelter puppies in winter, another on pet poodles in summer.
  • I² (I-squared) is a percentage score telling you how much the results disagree because of real differences rather than random luck.
  • If I² is low, the studies agree nicely.
  • If I² is high, the studies are apples and oranges. Smashing them together into one single average number is dangerous and misleading.

4. The forest plot (the famous multi-study graph)

If you open a meta-analysis paper, you will see a graph called a forest plot. How to read it:

  • One row per study: every line across the page is a separate study.
  • The box: that study's result. Bigger box = bigger study = more weight in the final math.
  • The horizontal line: that study's confidence interval.
  • The "no effect" line: a straight vertical line down the middle. If a study's line crosses it, that study on its own found no real effect. It sits at 1.0 for relative risk or odds ratio, or 0 if the measure is a direct subtraction difference.
  • The diamond at the bottom: the final combined average answer of all the studies put together. If the diamond doesn't touch the "no effect" line, the treatment works overall.

5. Funnel plot and publication bias (the missing corner)

Scientists and journals love flashy, exciting news ("Miracle drug cures everything!"). They usually reject boring studies ("Drug did nothing, results were totally normal").

  • A funnel plot is a scatter graph that maps out all the studies. With a complete, honest picture, the dots form a neat, symmetrical, upside-down triangle (funnel shape).
  • Publication bias: if the bottom-right corner of the funnel is empty (small studies that found negative or boring results are missing), those studies were probably never published.

PRISMA and funnel plots help expose a review that is only showing you the sunny half of the truth.

Exam trap

Publication bias inflates a meta-analysis result, because the missing studies are mostly the ones that found nothing.

Critical appraisal and GRADE ref

Critical appraisal means judging a single study with three questions:

  • Are the results valid? Was it done properly (allocation, blinding, follow-up, confounding, analysis)?
  • What are the results? How big is the effect, and how precise (not just p < 0.05)?
  • Will they help my animals? Were the study animals and setting close enough to a shelter?

GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) rates a whole body of evidence, not one paper. RCTs start high and observational studies start low, then the rating moves:

  • Down for risk of bias, inconsistent results, wrong population or outcome (indirectness), imprecision, and suspected publication bias.
  • Up for a very large effect, a dose-response pattern, or confounding that would have worked against the effect that was still seen.
Why this matters here

Design is where a study starts, not where it ends. Good observational shelter data can still be rated up.

Statistical Inference

Is the result real or just chance? Hypothesis testing, confidence intervals, and the two ways a test can be wrong.

Hypothesis testing ref

1. The two hypotheses (H0 and H1)

When scientists test a new idea, they set up two competing theories before the study even starts:

  • Null hypothesis (H0): the "boring" default. It assumes nothing is happening and the treatment does absolutely nothing. Example: "This new kennel disinfectant has zero effect on ringworm rates; it's identical to plain water." (In numbers: relative risk = 1.)
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1): the exciting theory. It assumes there is a real difference or effect. Example: "No, the new disinfectant actually lowers ringworm rates."

2. The p-value (is it just random luck?)

When you run your study and get a result, you calculate a p-value.

  • What it asks: if the treatment really did nothing, how likely is it that you would still see results this extreme, just by random luck?
  • The magic threshold (0.05): science uses 0.05 (5%) as the standard cut-off line.
  • The conclusion (p < 0.05): if your p-value drops below 0.05, results like yours would be so unlikely under "nothing is happening" that you can throw out the boring assumption. You conclude your intervention had a real effect.
  • If p is 0.05 or higher: random luck could easily explain your result, so you cannot throw out the null hypothesis. That is not the same as proving there is no effect.
The big exam trap

A p-value is not the probability that your hypothesis is correct, and it is not the probability that the result happened by random luck. It only tells you how likely you would be to see these results if the treatment were a total dud.

3. Statistical significance vs clinical significance

A classic trap that examiners love to test:

  • Statistical significance (p < 0.05): the maths says the result probably wasn't random luck.
  • Clinical significance: the result actually matters in the real world.
Shelter example

A huge study tests a new cat food on 50,000 shelter cats. It finds the new food makes cats live 2 days longer over a 15-year lifespan, and because the sample is so big, p = 0.001.

Statistically significant? Yes (p < 0.05). Clinically significant? No. Nobody cares about 2 extra days in 15 years.

Golden rule: always look at how big the effect is, not just whether the authors squeezed a p-value below 0.05 by using a giant sample.

Confidence intervals ref

A study result is only an estimate, because you can never test every animal. A confidence interval (CI) is the range of plausible values for where the true number probably lies. Example: RR = 2.0 (95% CI: 1.2 to 3.4) means we are 95% confident the true risk multiplier for the whole population is between 1.2 and 3.4.

What "95%" means: if the same shelter study were repeated 100 times, about 95 of the ranges would contain the true number and about 5 would miss it.

Reading a CI for RR or OR: look for 1.0

1.0 means no effect. Check whether the range crosses 1.0.

95% CI for RR or ORWhat it meansReal or luck?
Includes 1.0 (for example 0.9 to 2.1)Might raise risk, lower it, or do nothingCould be luck (not significant)

  • Ratio measures (RR, OR): the null value is 1. Difference measures (mean or risk difference): the null value is 0.
  • A narrower interval means a more precise estimate. Larger samples narrow it.

Error types and power ref

The two ways an inference can be wrong. Original construction.
  • Type I (alpha): a false positive; concluding an effect exists when it does not.
  • Type II (beta): a false negative; missing a real effect.
  • Power = 1 minus beta; conventionally 0.80 or higher. It increases with larger sample size, larger true effect, higher alpha, and lower variability. Sample size is the main lever.

Mnemonic: Type I is the false alarm (cried wolf); Type II is the miss (missed the wolf).

Bias and Validity

Bias is a built-in error that pushes results one way, and a bigger sample does not fix it. Random error (chance) does shrink with a bigger sample.

Bias ref

BiasWhat happens
Selection biasThe animals you picked aren't a fair sample, which distorts the link between exposure and disease
Information (measurement) biasExposure or outcome is measured or recorded wrongly in a consistent way
MisclassificationAnimals put in the wrong category. Non-differential (same errors in every group) hides a real effect. Differential (errors differ by group) can hide or invent one

Exam trap

Random error (chance) shrinks with a larger sample. Bias does not.

Confounding and effect modification ref

A confounder must meet all three criteria:

  • associated with the exposure,
  • an independent risk factor for the outcome, and
  • not on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome.

Control it by design: randomise (this balances unknown confounders too), restrict, or match. Control it in the analysis: stratify (Mantel-Haenszel) or use multivariable regression.

Clarification

Effect modification is different from confounding. The effect is genuinely different in different groups (for example, a handling programme helps adults but not puppies). Report each group separately; don't adjust it away.

Validity, reliability and sampling ref

  • Internal validity: the study measures what it set out to, free of bias and confounding.
  • External validity (generalisability): the results apply beyond the study sample. A tightly controlled study can be high on internal but low on external validity.
  • Reliability (precision) = you get the same answer each time. Validity (accuracy) = the answer is close to the truth. A measure can be reliable but wrong. Kappa measures how well two observers agree, after allowing for chance.
  • Measurement equivalence: a 2022 study found the Family Bondedness Scale (a human-animal bond measure) scored equivalently for cat and dog owners, so scores can fairly be compared between them (Nugent, Frontiers, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Probability sampling (supports inference): simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster. Non-probability sampling (weaker): convenience, purposive. Convenience samples are a common source of selection bias.

Measures of association by design ref note

Full calculations live in your Data and Analysis material; here is only the design-to-measure mapping. Different study designs give you different kinds of answer.

  • Relative risk (RR): compares the risk in exposed animals with the risk in unexposed animals. Comes from cohort and experimental studies. Null = 1.
  • Odds ratio (OR): used in case-control studies. It approximates the relative risk when the disease is rare. Null = 1.
  • Prevalence ratio: comes from cross-sectional studies.
  • Attributable risk (risk difference): the extra risk in exposed animals, over and above the risk in unexposed animals. From cohort and experimental studies.

Test by test, with shelter examples ref

The table above is the quick lookup. This section says why, with a shelter example for each.

Comparing group means (continuous outcome)

  • Independent t-test: compares the average of a number (like length of stay) between exactly two separate groups. It assumes the data are roughly bell-shaped and the groups have similar spread. Welch's t-test doesn't need similar spread, so it is often the safer choice. Shelter example: average length of stay in single vs group housing.
  • Paired t-test: use this when the two measurements come from the same animals (for example weight before and after deworming).
  • One-way ANOVA: the same idea for three or more groups. Running lots of t-tests adds up the chance of a false positive, so ANOVA asks one question first: "do any of the group averages differ?" If yes, follow with post-hoc tests (Tukey HSD, Bonferroni) to see which pairs differ. Shelter example: average length of stay for owner-surrender, stray and transfer intake.

Comparing proportions (categorical outcome)

  • Chi-square: tests whether counts in categories differ from what you'd expect if the two things were unrelated. It needs at least 5 expected in each cell. Shelter example: adoption vs euthanasia outcome by feline leukaemia virus (FeLV) status.
  • Fisher's exact test: use this instead when the numbers in the cells are small.

Measuring association (two continuous variables)

  • Pearson correlation (r, from -1 to +1): how strong and in which direction a straight-line relationship is.
  • Linear regression: goes a step further. It gives a slope (how much the outcome changes per unit), lets you predict, and lets you adjust for confounders. Shelter example: weight against age, or surgery time against body weight.

Modelling a binary outcome with several predictors

Logistic regression is used when the outcome is yes/no (adopted or not, live release or not) and you want to adjust for several things at once. Its results come out as odds ratios.

Skewed or ordinal data (non-parametric)

These tests rank the values instead of using the raw numbers, so the data don't need to be bell-shaped. Each one replaces a regular test:

  • Mann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum) replaces the two-group t-test.
  • Kruskal-Wallis replaces one-way ANOVA.
  • Spearman's rho replaces Pearson (it finds a relationship that goes steadily up or down, not only a straight line).
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank replaces the paired t-test, and Friedman's test replaces repeated-measures ANOVA.

Length-of-stay data are usually right-skewed (lots of short stays, a few very long ones), so a Mann-Whitney is often a better choice than a t-test.

Time to an event

  • Kaplan-Meier is the go-to tool for how long animals stay in a shelter. It draws the "still in care" curve over time and handles censoring: animals still in the shelter at the end of the study are counted for the exact days they were there, rather than being unfairly thrown out.
  • Log-rank test: compares two or more Kaplan-Meier curves.
  • Cox proportional hazards: the regression extension, giving hazard ratios while adjusting for covariates.

A plain average length of stay is biased because it ignores animals still in care.

Repeated measurements on the same animals

Every test above assumes each row is a different animal. If the same animal appears more than once, use the paired or repeated version:

  • Paired t-test for two time points. It tests the change within each animal, which removes animal-to-animal differences and makes it more powerful.
  • Repeated-measures ANOVA for three or more time points. Weakness: one missing time point and the animal is dropped.
  • Mixed-effects (multilevel) models are the best general answer. A random effect gives each animal its own baseline, and the fixed effects are what you want to test. They cope with missing time points and with clustering (littermates, households, kennel wards).

Shelter example: stress scores on days 1, 3, 7 and 14 in 40 cats under two enrichment protocols. You want to compare the protocols, but the model must know the 160 rows come from 40 cats.

Exam framework

Simple tests just ask "do these groups differ?" Regression asks "do they still differ after we control for everything else?"

Study-design caveat

Every test above assumes each animal is independent. Shelter data often break this: littermates, animals from one household and repeat surrenders are alike. When animals cluster, use a mixed-effects model, or conditional logistic regression for matched case-control studies.

Association vs causation ref

Association is never proof of causation. To judge whether something really causes an outcome, use the Bradford Hill considerations: temporality, strength, dose-response, consistency, biological plausibility, coherence, experimental evidence, specificity, analogy.

Exam trap

Temporality (the cause must happen before the effect) is the only absolute, essential requirement. All the other criteria are helpful supporting clues, not a mandatory checklist.

“Maybe we should think outside the box?” prioritisation of issues with UK not-for-profit canine health and welfare research funding using Delphi expert consensus and gap analysis
Skipper · PLOS ONE · 2024

Abstract. Over fifty participants, who together possessed broad research, veterinary and front-line expertise from across the canine health and welfare sector, contributed to a modified Delphi study to identify the highest priority research topics in UK canine health and welfare, the highest priorities for future research approaches, and the highest priorities for future reform in research processes and infrastructure, through group consensus. Further analysis also compared the prioritisation of selected research topics to the actual levels of research funding they previously received, through comparison with historical data. Most of the identified highest priority issues relating to canine health and welfare and its research concerned various aspects of the human-canine relationship, such as ownership or behavioural issues. Participants strongly emphasised the complexity of interrelated factors that impact the welfare of both dogs and people. Research topics identified as previously ‘most underfunded’ all concerned real-world canine welfare issues, particularly emphasising the breeding and supply of dogs. A supplementary analysis of historical research funding (2012–2022) for common chronic disorders in primary care practice, another identified highest priority topic, identified periodontal disease, anal sac disorders, overgrown nails and patellar luxation as the ‘most underfunded’ conditions. Most of the identified highest priority research approaches and methodologies concerned real-world design and execution aspects of canine health and welfare research, such as impact and engagement, with a strong focus on research investigating the human factors in canine welfare. Aspects of research funding infrastructure that were considered highest priority for future change mostly concerned increased transparency of funding processes and increased collaboration between stakeholder groups throughout the funding sector, which was strongly supported. Overall, these findings emphasise the importance of considering and including human factors and real-world impact, where appropriate, as key elements for optimising the relevance of canine health and welfare research.

Finding Yourself in the Arena of Scientific Writing The Journey From Idea to Publication in Shelter and Community Medicine
From Idea to Publication in Shelter and Community Medicine · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2023

Abstract. Irrespective of discipline, scientific study relies upon systematic reasoning from observations to formulate and refine hypotheses and develop theories that can explain documented phenomena. Our understanding of science and its influence on the world around us is ever evolving, yet science cannot advance without clear communication to disseminate findings that result from reproducible research. Veterinary and animal care team members can contribute significantly to the advancement of science by sharing ideas, experiences, perspectives, case management decisions, and patient outcomes. The uniqueness of shelter medicine, with recent emphasis on how to maintain animals within their homes, offers a wealth of observations and discoveries that are worthy of publication. For those in clinical practice in shelter and community medicine, the primary obstacles to publishing are time, confidence, and lack of familiarity or experience with the structure and style of scientific writing. Such barriers can be overcome through a combination of graduated exposure, mentoring, patience, and practice. This article explores the journey from idea to publication to encourage those in shelter and community medicine who generate and have access to vital data to come forward. A Measurement Equivalence Study of the Family Bondedness Scale: Measurement Equivalence Between Cat and Dog Owners
Nugent · Frontiers · 2022

Abstract. About 38.4% of U.S. households include a dog, and 25.4% a cat, as pets, and a recent poll suggested over 90% of pet owners feel their companion animal is a family member. Numerous studies have suggested pet ownership has physical, mental, and social health benefits, though much of this research has yielded mixed results. Results of a recent review suggested significant measurement problems in human-animal interaction (HAI) and human-animal bond (HAB) research, including the absence of validity evidence, overly long measures, lack of evidence for measurement equivalence across species of pets, and measures lacking a basis in important psychological, family, and attachment theories. This article describes the development and results of a measurement equivalence study of a new measure of the HAB called the family bondedness scale (FBS). This scale, and the research results, address multiple gaps in HAB measurement. Results of multi-group confirmatory factor analyses with multiple covariates indicated the scores on the FBS showed equivalence between cat and dog owners. The use of the FBS in both veterinary research and practice, as well as in research and practice in other disciplines, such as social work and psychology, are considered.

Pet ownership, feelings of loneliness, and mood in people affected by the first COVID-19 lockdown
Martinez-Caja · J of Vet Behavior · 2022

Abstract. The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic has caused dramatic changes to our lifestyle, particularly affecting our ability to interact “in person” with our social network. These changes have had a detrimental effect on the mental welfare of the global population. The international questionnaire “Pets in Lockdown” was designed to investigate whether feelings of loneliness were affecting the mood of people during the COVID-19 lockdown and whether pet ownership may have had a positive influence on both loneliness and general mood. As expected, higher loneliness scores were associated with higher negative and lower positive affective states. In addition, lower loneliness scores were associated with pet ownership and living with other people, but not with more frequent interactions with people from outside the household, suggesting that physical and close contact has an important role in decreasing feelings of loneliness. Besides the effects on the loneliness score, pet ownership was not associated with positive or negative affective states. The strength of the attachment to animals, measured as the amount of comfort that people obtain from their pets, was stronger in people with potentially limited access to affiliative physical human contact and was associated with both higher positive and negative affective states. Additionally, people obtained significantly more comfort from dogs and horses compared with other pet species. The results suggest that during the confinement period, pets may have benefited people with smaller social networks by alleviating loneliness and offering comfort and embodied close contact.

Dog behaviour classification with movement sensors placed on the harness and the collar
Kumpulainen, P. · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Dog owners’ understanding of the daily behaviour of their dogs may be enhanced by movement measurements that can detect repeatable dog behaviour, such as levels of daily activity and rest as well as their changes. The aim of this study was to evaluate the performance of supervised machine learning methods utilising accelerometer and gyroscope data provided by wearable movement sensors in classification of seven typical dog activities in a semi-controlled test situation. Forty-five middle to large sized dogs participated in the study. Two sensor devices were attached to each dog, one on the back of the dog in a harness and one on the neck collar. Altogether 54 features were extracted from the acceleration and gyroscope signals divided in two-second segments. The performance of four classifiers were compared using features derived from both sensor modalities. and from the acceleration data only. The results were promising; the movement sensor at the back yielded up to 91 % accuracy in classifying the dog activities and the sensor placed at the collar yielded 75 % accuracy at best. Including the gyroscope features improved the classification accuracy by 0.7–2.6 %, depending on the classifier and the sensor location. The most distinct activity was sniffing, whereas the static postures (lying on chest, sitting and standing) were the most challenging behaviours to classify, especially from the data of the neck collar sensor. The data used in this article as well as the signal processing scripts are openly available in Mendeley Data, https://doi.org/10.17632/vxhx934tbn.1.

Short-term storage conditions do not affect canine fecal cortisol and secretory immunoglobulin A concentrations
Lenz OC, Powell L, Reinhard CL, Watson B · Scientific Reports · 2026

Abstract. Fecal cortisol and secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) are frequently used biomarkers in animal welfare, wildlife conservation, and other fields. However, these research settings often lack immediate access to a -80 degrees C freezer or other ultra-low temperature storage equipment. This study aims to determine whether canine fecal cortisol and sIgA concentrations remain stable over 24 h at either ambient temperature or on wet ice. Fecal samples were collected from 10 adult dogs housed in two Philadelphia area animal shelters in May and June of 2024. After aliquoting, baseline samples were frozen on dry ice within 15 minutes of defecation. Remaining aliquots were stored at ambient temperature (15-21 degrees C) or on wet ice (4 degrees C) for 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, or 24 hours before freezing on dry ice. Cortisol and sIgA concentrations were quantified using ELISAs and analyzed with a linear mixed effects model. No significant differences were found between time points or storage conditions. These data show that concentrations of fecal canine cortisol and sIgA remain stable if stored unfrozen for 24 hours. Immediate freezing at -80 degrees C does not appear to be necessary for accurate quantification of these analytes. This knowledge removes logistical and regulatory barriers to conducting quality noninvasive research in animal shelter, clinical, and field settings.
