Epidemiology & Biostatistics

Research & Biostats · 6% of exam · a teaching walk-through of your notes, formula by formula

Almost every biostats question is really one of five moves: count disease (incidence/prevalence) → compare two groups (RR/OR) → judge a test (Se/Sp/PPV) → pick the right study → decide if a result is real (p-value/CI). Everything below is built on one tiny table of four numbers — learn that table first and the formulas fall out of it.

1 · Counting disease — incidence vs prevalence

Incidence = NEW cases

Incidence counts new disease events appearing over a period. It measures risk / how fast disease is arriving. Two flavours:

CI = new cases in the periodpopulation at risk at the start

Cumulative incidence (CI) — a simple proportion; you must state the time window. Answers "what's a dog's risk of getting this over the next month?"

IR = new casestotal animal-time at risk

Incidence rate (IR) — uses animal-time (e.g. dog-days) as the denominator, so it handles animals that stay for different lengths of time. Use IR when your population is churning — a shelter where animals come and go.+

Example. 200 dogs enter clean; over 2 weeks, 30 develop CIRDC. CI = 30/200 = 15% in 2 weeks. If instead you logged 2,000 dog-days at risk: IR = 30/2,000 = 0.015 cases per dog-day.

Prevalence = EXISTING cases

Prevalence is the existing burden — the slice of the population that has the disease right now. It's a snapshot, not a rate.

Point prev. = cases at one instantpopulation at that instant
Period prev. = cases during an intervalpopulation over that interval

Point = a single day's census; period = anyone who had it across a span of time.

Example. On 1 July, 25 of 180 cats in the building have URI → point prevalence = 14%.
Prevalence ≈ Incidence × average duration

The key relationship. A pool fills from incidence and drains as animals recover or die. Prevalence is high when disease either arrives fast (high incidence) or sticks around (long duration).

⚠ Trap: a chronic disease (e.g. FIV) can have high prevalence but low incidence — it lingers. A quick, deadly, or quickly-cured disease can have high incidence but low prevalence.

Attack rate — incidence for an outbreak

Attack rate = number illnumber ill + number well (all at risk)

Just a cumulative incidence measured over the short window of one outbreak. You compute it separately for exposed vs unexposed groups, then compare the two to fish out the risk factor. This is exactly how you'd investigate a parvo cluster in a kennel run.+

Example. Of 50 puppies in a room, 40 develop parvo → attack rate = 40/50 = 80%. If the attack rate in vaccinated pups was 10% and unvaccinated 80%, vaccination status is your lead.
⚠ Denominator rule (population at risk): exclude animals who can't become a new case — those already sick, currently infected, or immune. Putting them in the denominator dilutes your measure.

2 · The one table everything comes from

The exposure 2×2

Learn these four letters once. RR, OR and attributable risk are all just arithmetic on a, b, c, d.

Disease +Disease −
Exposedab
Unexposedcd
  • a = exposed & got disease · b = exposed, stayed well
  • c = unexposed & got disease · d = unexposed, stayed well
  • Risk in exposed = a/(a+b) · Risk in unexposed = c/(c+d)

"Exposed" just means has the risk factor

Exposure is whatever you're testing as a cause: unvaccinated, overcrowded, intact, imported, a certain diet. "Disease" is your outcome of interest.

Memory hook: read the table left-to-right, top row first — a before b, c before d — diseased column always first.+

Later (section 5) the same 2×2 is re-labelled TP/FP/FN/TN to judge a diagnostic test. Same skeleton, different question.

3 · Comparing two groups — measures of association

Relative Risk (RR) — the "risk ratio"

RR = risk in exposedrisk in unexposed = a / (a+b)c / (c+d)

In plain terms: how many times more likely is the exposed group to get sick than the unexposed group? RR of 6 = "six times the risk."

Example. Unvaccinated dogs: 30 sick / 100 → risk 0.30. Vaccinated: 5 sick / 100 → risk 0.05. RR = 0.30 / 0.05 = 6.0 → unvaccinated dogs are 6× as likely to get parvo.
RR > 1 — exposure raises risk (harmful) RR = 1 — no association (null value) RR < 1 — exposure lowers risk (protective)
⚠ Where it's valid: RR needs real incidence, so it can only come from a cohort or experimental study — designs that follow animals forward from exposure to outcome.

Odds Ratio (OR) — for case-control studies

OR = a × db × c

In plain terms: the odds ratio compares the odds of exposure in the diseased vs the well. You reach for it when you started from the outcome and worked backwards (case-control), so you can't measure incidence and therefore can't do RR.

Example. Cross-multiply the diagonal. With a=30, b=70, c=5, d=95: OR = (30×95)/(70×5) = 2850/350 = 8.1. Read it like RR: about 8× the odds.
OR > 1 — positive association OR = 1 — no association OR < 1 — protective
⚠ Rare-disease rule: OR ≈ RR only when the disease is rare. When the disease is common, the OR exaggerates the RR (pushes it further from 1). Great for rare / emerging outbreak work; read with caution when the condition is common.

Attributable risk (risk difference)

AR = riskexposed − riskunexposed

The excess risk carried because of the exposure — a subtraction, not a ratio. Answers "how much of the risk would vanish if we removed the exposure?"

Example. 0.30 − 0.05 = 0.25 → 25 extra cases per 100 dogs are attributable to being unvaccinated.

Prevalence ratio

The cross-sectional cousin of RR: prevalence in exposed ÷ prevalence in unexposed. Used when a snapshot study measured existing cases rather than following animals forward.

Same >1 / =1 / <1 reading as RR, but built on prevalence, so it can't prove the exposure came first.

4 · Death metrics — three different denominators

Crude mortality

deaths from ANY causewhole population at risk of dying

Overall death rate of the group, all causes together.

Cause-specific mortality

deaths from ONE diseasewhole population at risk

How much a single disease contributes to death in the whole population.

Case-fatality rate (CFR)

deaths from a diseaseconfirmed CASES of that disease

Of the animals who actually got it, what fraction died — a measure of the disease's virulence / severity.

Example. 12 parvo deaths among 40 parvo cases → CFR = 30%.
⚠ The classic mix-up: case-fatality's denominator is only the sick animals (how deadly is it if you catch it); cause-specific mortality's denominator is the entire population (how much does it kill overall). Same numerator, very different meaning.

5 · Judging a diagnostic test

The test 2×2 — same table, new labels

Disease PRESENTDisease ABSENT
Test +TPFP→ PPV
Test −FNTN→ NPV
read ↓SensitivitySpecificity

Columns = the truth (disease or not). Rows = what the test said. Sensitivity & specificity read down the truth columns; PPV & NPV read across the test rows.

Sensitivity — "truly diseased"

Sn = TPTP + FN

Of all the genuinely sick animals, what fraction the test catches. High sensitivity = few false negatives.

SnNOUT: a highly Snsitive test that comes back Negative is good for ruling a disease OUT — great for screening.+

Specificity — "truly healthy"

Sp = TNTN + FP

Of all the genuinely healthy animals, what fraction the test correctly clears. High specificity = few false positives.

SpPIN: a highly Specific test that comes back Positive is good for ruling a disease IN — great for confirmation.+

PPV & NPV — what a result means

PPV = TPTP + FP NPV = TNTN + FN

PPV: if the test is positive, what's the chance the animal is truly diseased? NPV: if negative, truly healthy? Unlike Sn/Sp, these depend on prevalence.

The prevalence trap

⚠ High-yield: as prevalence falls, PPV falls — even with the same Sn & Sp. In a low-prevalence population, most positives are false positives, so you must confirm a positive with a second test.

This is the engine behind the FeLV/FIV "confirm the positive" rule and the parvo SNAP interpretation — same statistics, different disease.+

6 · Study design — which study answers the question

Descriptive — generate a hypothesis

Describe what's happening; cannot test a hypothesis, only raise one. No comparison group.

  • Case report — one animal
  • Case series — several similar cases
  • Cross-sectional — snapshot of a population
  • Ecological — correlation at the group level

Analytic observational — test a hypothesis

You observe (don't assign) exposure, and you have a comparison group. The key question is which end you start from:

  • Cohort — start with exposure, follow forward, measure outcome. → gives incidence & RR. Good for common diseases & multiple outcomes.
  • Case-control — start with the outcome, look back for exposure. → gives OR. Good for rare / slow disease; prone to recall & selection bias.
  • Cross-sectional — measure exposure and outcome at once → gives prevalence; cannot establish temporality (which came first).

Experimental — the investigator assigns

Randomised controlled trial (RCT) — the gold standard. Animals are randomly put into an intervention group vs a control group. Randomisation balances confounders, so it's the strongest design for proving causation.

Hierarchy of evidence (strongest → weakest)

  • 1. Systematic review / meta-analysis
  • 2. Randomised controlled trial (RCT)
  • 3. Cohort study
  • 4. Case-control study
  • 5. Case series / case reports (primary observation, no comparison group)
  • 6. Expert opinion / narrative review (no primary data)
Off the ladder. Laboratory, in vitro and challenge studies in purpose-bred animals are not a rung. They answer mechanism or efficacy under controlled conditions, trading external validity for internal validity — the uniform dose, uniform age and clean housing are exactly what a shelter is not.
Ranks designs, not studies. A large, well-conducted cohort can beat a small unblinded RCT with heavy loss to follow-up. GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) formalises this: rate down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias; rate up for a large effect, a dose-response gradient, or confounding that would have worked against the effect still observed.
Recall-bias example (case-control). Owners of a sick dog think harder about past exposures and over-report them — their worry colours the memory, skewing the exposure history. That's why case-control is bias-prone.

7 · Surveillance & outbreak curves

Four ways to watch for disease

  • Passive — the system waits for cases/reports to come to it. Cheap, but misses cases.
  • Active — the team deliberately seeks out cases. More complete, more work.
  • Syndromic — tracks clinical syndromes (e.g. "coughing dogs") for an early signal, before a diagnosis is confirmed.
  • Sentinel — monitor a defined subset as an early indicator for the wider population.
⚠ Case definition is critical. Decide up front what counts as a case, applied consistently by time, place & animal — otherwise your counts are meaningless.

Reading the epidemic curve

Shape of the "cases over time" plot tells you the source.

Point source One brief, shared exposure. Sharp rise to a single peak, then tapers; all cases inside one incubation period.
Continuous / common source One ongoing source. Rises then holds a plateau until the source is removed.
Propagated Animal-to-animal spread. Successive peaks, each about one incubation period apart, often growing.

8 · Bias, confounding & effect modification

Random error vs bias

Random error is truly random noise — it isn't aimed anywhere and shrinks with a bigger sample. Bias is a systematic error that pushes results in a particular direction and does not improve with sample size.

Types of bias: selection, information/measurement, misclassification (wrong category), recall, observer.

Which way does misclassification push?

  • Non-differential (random, affects all groups equally) → biases toward the null, diluting a real difference (you under-estimate the effect).
  • Differential (linked to exposure or outcome) → can bias in either direction, creating or hiding an effect.
Rule of thumb: sloppy-but-even errors hide effects; lopsided errors can invent them.

Confounding — a lurking third variable

A confounder distorts the exposure–outcome link. To qualify it must be all three:

  • 1. Associated with the exposure
  • 2. An independent risk factor for the outcome
  • 3. Not on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome

Effect modification — a real difference

Not an error. The exposure's true effect genuinely differs across levels of a third variable (e.g. a treatment helps puppies but not adults). You report each subgroup separately rather than "adjusting it away."

Confounding is a nuisance to remove; effect modification is a finding to describe.

9 · Is the result real? — p-values, errors & confidence intervals

Null hypothesis & p-value

The null hypothesis (H₀) says "no difference, no association." Its null value is 1 for ratios (RR, OR) and 0 for differences.

The p-value = the probability of seeing data this extreme if H₀ were true. A low p means the data would be surprising under "no effect," so you reject H₀ (conventionally p < 0.05).

How to interpret the number
p ≤ 0.05 — statistically significantYou reject the null hypothesis. There is less than a 5% probability your results happened purely by chance → you have evidence of a true effect or difference.
p > 0.05 — not statistically significantYou fail to reject the null hypothesis. Random chance could reasonably explain what you observed → no strong evidence of a real difference.
p does not tell you the size of the effect or the chance the null is true — only how surprising the data are.+

Type I / Type II errors & power

  • Type I error (α) = false positive — you claim an effect that isn't there. (α = 0.05 is the usual line.)
  • Type II error (β) = false negative — you miss a real effect.
  • Power = 1 − β — the chance of detecting a true effect. Bigger samples → more power.
Mnemonic: Type 1 = you saw 1 thing that wasn't there; Type 2 = you missed it.+

Statistical vs clinical significance

Statistical significance (a small p) only says the result is unlikely to be chance — with a big enough sample, a trivial difference can be "significant." Clinical significance asks whether the effect is big enough to matter to the animal.

Use the confidence interval (CI) to judge both at once: it shows the plausible range of the true effect. If the 95% CI crosses the null value (1 for ratios, 0 for differences), the result is not statistically significant. A narrow CI = a precise estimate; a wide CI = lots of uncertainty (often too small a sample).

Example. RR = 6.0, 95% CI 2.1–17 → doesn't cross 1, so it's significant, but the wide range warns the exact size is uncertain. RR = 1.2, 95% CI 0.9–1.6 → crosses 1 → not significant.
