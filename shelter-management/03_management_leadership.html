Personnel Management

Effective shelters are built on communication, careful hiring, and consistent performance management. Rushing any stage creates compounding problems that cost far more time and credibility than the original shortcut saved.

Management and Leadership

Speed

Communication principles Ch. 5

  • Active listening: essential for two-way communication. Staff respect and trust are non-negotiable for effective functioning.
  • Clarification loop: ask for clarification of what the other person is saying, then repeat it back and ask for confirmation. Do not prejudge the message being received.
  • Gray zones cause conflict: shelters frequently have "gray zones" around euthanasia and intake criteria that cause conflict and bad feelings. Consistent application of criteria removes the gray zone.
  • Positive reinforcement: applies to training staff just as it applies to animals. Use it explicitly.
  • Fair pay: a baseline requirement for staff retention and organizational trust.

The 9 hiring principles Ch. 5

  1. Thorough over fast: it is worth working short-staffed for a longer period to find the right person. Pressure to fill a slot quickly leads to short-sighted decisions that will be regretted.
  2. Check references, including past employers. Past employers rarely disclose poor performance, but will enthusiastically describe excellent employees. Key question: "If given the chance, would you hire this person again?"
  3. The interview must include a facility tour. Topics to cover: euthanasia and adoption policies, handling of animals with infectious diseases. The astute interviewer observes how the applicant interacts with the animals and the depth of questions the tour generates.
  4. Probationary basis only. If things are not feeling right during the probationary period, terminate promptly, even before the period ends.
  5. Gut feelings are worth listening to.
  6. Common sense cannot be overrated. It is one of those assets a person either has or does not have. It cannot be taught.
  7. Team players are invaluable. Skills can be honed with proper training. Personality cannot.
  8. Allow adequate time for training.
  9. Quality evaluations must be standard practice. For good employees: highlight appreciation and valued qualities. For poor employees: document specifically where improvement is needed and what is expected going forward.
Exam Trap: Skills vs. personality Technical skills can be trained. Common sense and personality cannot. When an exam scenario presents a candidate with good personality/common sense but limited technical skills versus a technically strong candidate with a poor attitude, the correct answer prioritizes the former, because the organization can train skills but cannot fix personality.

Performance evaluations Ch. 5

Employee typePrimary purpose of evaluationKey content
Good employee Appreciation and retention Highlight the specific qualities that are valuable to the organization and the animals. Make the employee feel seen and valued.
Poor employee Documentation and accountability Specify exactly where improvement is needed and what is expected going forward. This documentation forms the legal foundation for termination.

Termination Ch. 5

  • If termination becomes necessary, the case must be carefully and professionally built in advance.
  • The pattern of poor performance previously documented in evaluations greatly reduces the likelihood of a lawsuit or union intervention.
  • An impulsive, undocumented termination creates legal vulnerability. A documented, progressive performance record does not.
Exam Trap: Probationary period is not a protection for the employee The probationary period exists to protect the organization, not the employee. An employee showing problematic behavior during probation should be terminated promptly, even if the probationary period is not yet complete. Waiting is the incorrect action.

Volunteer Management ASV 2022

Volunteers are a critical operational resource in most shelters but occupy a fundamentally different legal and supervisory relationship than paid employees. Managing volunteers well requires explicit systems for screening, onboarding, scope limitation, and exit.

Volunteer screening and onboarding

  • Background check: Anyone working directly with animals or accessing shelter areas should undergo a background screening appropriate to the level of access. This is a risk management requirement, not an optional best practice.
  • Orientation: All volunteers must complete a formal orientation covering shelter policies, animal handling protocols, biosecurity requirements, and emergency procedures before beginning unsupervised work.
  • Documented training: Training documentation for volunteers must be maintained in the same manner as for paid staff. If a trained volunteer causes an incident and training cannot be documented, the shelter faces the same legal vulnerability as with an untrained volunteer.
  • Health disclosure: Volunteers who are immunocompromised, pregnant, or have animal allergies should be counseled on zoonotic exposure risks before beginning work with animals.

Chain of command and scope limitations

  • Volunteers report to designated staff supervisors, not directly to the Executive Director or Board. The chain of command for volunteers must be explicit and communicated at orientation.
  • Scope of practice: Volunteers are permitted to perform only the tasks they have been trained and authorized for by licensed or credentialed staff. Tasks requiring veterinary judgment (medication administration, triage assessment, euthanasia) cannot be delegated to volunteers regardless of their enthusiasm or prior experience outside the shelter.
  • State practice acts may specifically restrict which tasks unlicensed individuals can perform, including volunteers. The shelterʼs internal volunteer scope definitions cannot exceed state law limits.

Volunteer retention

  • Match task complexity to skill level. Volunteers who feel competent but appropriately challenged show higher retention than those who are overwhelmed or bored.
  • Provide visible growth pathways: level systems, mentorship roles, or specialized training opportunities give long-term volunteers a reason to stay engaged.
  • Apply the WIIFM ("what's in it for me?") principle: volunteers, like private practitioners, evaluate their continued participation by whether it meets their personal goals. Understanding each volunteerʼs motivation (animal love, skill development, social connection) allows leadership to structure their experience accordingly.
  • Regular acknowledgment of volunteer contributions is essential. Unlike paid staff, volunteers have no financial incentive — recognition is their primary compensation.
  • What actually drives satisfaction: a 651-volunteer survey found internal shelter policies and procedures — not volunteer demographics or personal traits — are the strongest predictor of satisfaction (Reese, JAAWS, 2023 — see journal abstracts below). A companion survey of the same population found volunteers who feel they lack "voice" in shelter decisions are significantly less satisfied, and that limited-intake shelters with higher save rates report better volunteer satisfaction with their voice and overall experience (Reese, JAAWS, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Foster volunteers specifically: a national survey of 611 dog-foster volunteers found they are generally satisfied with organizational support but want more communication, more training beyond the basics, and stronger peer/social support networks — gaps linked to why fosters consider quitting (Reese, JAAWS, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). Emotional attachment to a foster dog is comparable to attachment to an owned pet, and higher emotional stress from fostering predicts thoughts of quitting — organizational support directed at the volunteer (not just the animal) can offset this (Reese, Anthrozoös, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Volunteer exit process

  • A volunteer who violates shelter protocols (boundary violations with animals, sharing confidential case information, unauthorized medical interventions) should be removed from the program using a documented, progressive process — not an impulsive verbal dismissal.
  • Exit documentation protects the shelter against disputes. The same principle that makes performance documentation critical for paid employee terminations applies to volunteer exits.
Exam Trap: Volunteers are not a lower-risk category A volunteer who administers medication incorrectly or handles an aggressive animal outside their training creates the same liability for the shelter as a paid employee. Volunteer status does not reduce the shelter's duty of care or its legal exposure. Training documentation, scope definitions, and supervision protocols apply equally.

Governance and Authority

Organizational effectiveness depends on clear role boundaries. The Board governs strategy; the Executive Director manages operations; staff never bypass the chain of command.

Board of Directors: role and limits Ch. 5

The Board DOESThe Board does NOT do
Define the organizational direction and mission Manage day-to-day shelter operations
Oversee finances and ensure adequate resources Direct animal care staff on specific tasks
Long-range planning Alter feeding schedules, cleaning rotations, or intake decisions
Review and approve operating budgets Communicate with staff except through the ED
Ensure the organization's policies reflect the mission Sign off on individual animal euthanasia decisions
Exam Trap: Board involvement in operations is a governance failure A board member who directs kennel staff to change feeding schedules or instructs the intake team to accept a specific animal has overstepped their role. This is an institutional boundary violation regardless of their intent or position. Day-to-day operations are entirely outside board authority.

The chain of command Ch. 5

  • The Executive Director (ED) is the staff's exclusive interface with the Board.
  • It is never appropriate for staff members to go over the head of the ED directly to the Board, even if they disagree with an ED decision.
  • Big problems develop when the Board and the ED do not have a strong and cooperative relationship.

Government / municipal shelter bureaucracy Ch. 5

  • Some animal control facilities operate under the auspices of a police department or other municipal agency whose leadership are not necessarily "animal people."
  • The main problems in this structure center on money, bureaucracy, and ignorance of animal welfare and sheltering issues.
  • Those in positions of authority over the shelter need to be educated on important animal welfare issues and, critically, on "what is in it for them" (i.e., how animal welfare outcomes serve their organizational and political goals).

Authority, credentials, and the veterinarian's role ASV 2022

  • Decisions involving resource allocation (organizational, population, or individual animal level) must be made by personnel who understand the shelter's priorities and its Capacity for Care.
  • Authority and responsibility must only be given to those with appropriate knowledge, training, and (where applicable) credentials. Non-credentialed staff cannot make medical decisions.
  • The shelter veterinarian must be consulted on all policies and protocols related to the maintenance of medical and behavioral animal health. This is not optional.
  • Where friction actually comes from: a 179-respondent survey found non-veterinarian leaders rated the veterinarian-leader relationship most positively (M=4.0/5), veterinarian-leaders next (M=3.7), and veterinarian non-leaders least positively (M=3.1) — vet non-leaders' top complaint was "lack of effective communication" (70%), vet-leaders' was "differences in priorities/conflicting goals" (66%), and non-vet leaders' was "managing expectations and demands" (55%); communication was the single factor all three groups agreed mattered most for a successful relationship (Mauck, J Shelter Med Community Animal Health, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Veterinarian-of-record relationships (VoRRs) — the nonregulated arrangement letting a shelter buy wholesale medical supplies through a supervising veterinarian — show a documented expectations gap: veterinarians are significantly more concerned than shelter administrators about meeting VCPR legal requirements, and expect significantly higher compensation than administrators expect to pay, helping explain why shelters report difficulty retaining a VoR (Almcrantz, J Shelter Med Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Compensation benchmarks (2024 US survey, 299 respondents): full-time clinical shelter veterinarians earned a median $122,500 (+25% since 2018); part-time clinical vets earned $85/h (+77%) for a median 24 h/week; academic shelter faculty earned a median $130,000 (+20%, ranging Instructor $103,000 to Professor $150,000); leadership/consulting roles earned the most at a median $168,000 (+40%). Despite the increases, clinical shelter-vet pay remained 5-7% below general and companion-animal private-practice medians (Kreisler, JAVMA, 2025; Midwestern, J Shelter Med Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Media Relations

Shelter veterinarians are most likely to be approached by the media during cruelty cases, hoarding investigations, or operational failures. Every media interaction carries reputational risk and opportunity.

The designated spokesperson rule Ch. 5

  • In any sensitive case (cruelty, hoarding, operational failure), there must be one designated media point person for the agency.
  • All media inquiries must be referred to this person.
  • The shelter veterinarian should only make public statements if explicitly authorized by the designated point person.
  • Assume that most reporters want to present accurate information. Truthfulness enhances credibility.
Exam Trap: The vet speaks only if authorized A shelter vet approached by a reporter during a cruelty case must refer the reporter to the designated media point person and decline to comment unless explicitly authorized. Giving an off-the-record interview or inviting a reporter into a sensitive area are both wrong answers on the boards.

Interview rules Ch. 5

RuleRationale
Never walk in cold, upset, or unprepared. Obtain the questions in advance.Preparation allows you to control the message rather than react to it.
Assume the interview is being recorded.Anything said informally can appear on air or in print.

Never say "no comment." If you cannot discuss something, say so and explain why if possible."No comment" reads as evasion or guilt. A clear explanation of a legal restriction is credible.
Do not repeat the interviewer's negative words. Pivot to a positive frame.Repeating negative language reinforces it in print or on video. Always reframe.

Exam Trap: "No comment" vs. "I can't discuss that" "No comment" is explicitly flagged as the wrong response. The correct alternative when information cannot be shared is to explain that the information cannot be disclosed and, where possible, why (e.g., "This is part of an ongoing investigation and I am not in a position to share details at this time").
Exam Trap: Do NOT repeat negative words If a reporter asks: "Why is your shelter failing so badly?" the wrong answer begins with "We are not failing badly because..." Repeating the negative language reinforces it. The correct response pivots directly to a positive message about what the shelter is doing.

Community Engagement

Building relationships with local general practitioners and the broader veterinary community is imperative. These partnerships directly benefit shelter animals and strengthen the organization's community standing.

The "What's in it for me?" principle Ch. 5

  • Every decision a person makes is based on the underlying question: "What's in it for me?" This is the guiding principle for building relationships with private practitioners.
  • Working together on small projects and coming to consensus on small issues marks the beginning of affinity and rapport.
  • Every small "yes" makes future yeses more likely. Start with achievable asks; build toward larger commitments.
  • The more time an individual donates to an organization, the more invested and loyal they become. Investment creates ownership and loyalty.

Engagement strategies for private practitioners Ch. 5

StrategyDetails and exam-relevant nuance
Newsletter / mailing list Local vets should be on every shelter's mailing list. Leave newsletters in their waiting rooms. The shelter vet can write columns on shelter-relevant topics: vaccination protocols, rabies quarantine policies, pediatric spay/neuter. Invite local colleagues to write a column, which further deepens the relationship.
After-hours facility tour An evening or weekend "behind the scenes" tour of shelter facilities can have surprising benefits. Providing food significantly increases attendance when people are being asked to come after a full workday. Can be extended to family members and clinic staff.
Invite to teach at the shelter Invite local practitioners to give a seminar for the public or shelter staff on topics such as geriatric pet care, emergency first aid, vaccination importance, or choosing the right companion animal. Teaching increases personal investment and loyalty.
Special event invitations Add practitioners to the shelter's event mailing list. A personal follow-up call, where appropriate, amplifies the invitation.
Exam Trap: Food at after-hours events is explicitly recommended The notes specifically call out that providing food increases attendance when practitioners are being asked to attend after work. This is a testable detail.

Liaison committees Ch. 5

Composition

  • A few local Veterinary Medical Association (VMA) members plus relevant shelter staff.
  • Experience with such committees is typically positive and benefits both organizations.

Protocol consultation

  • It is always wise to solicit the opinions of colleagues who have experience with proposed protocol changes or can evaluate a program's effectiveness.
  • Critical warning: veterinarians may become resentful if their advice is solicited and then seemingly ignored. If feedback is sought, it must be genuinely considered, or the request for input will damage rather than strengthen the relationship.

Working with outside specialists Ch. 5

  • Fee discussion upfront: whenever an outside colleague will provide a service for a shelter case, fees must be discussed before the service is rendered. Options include full price, reduced fee, at cost, or pro bono. Ambiguity about fees after the fact damages relationships.
  • Recognize generous donations of time: it is vital that generous donations of specialist time and expertise are recognized. A thank-you note signed by shelter staff or the veterinary team is highly valued by recipients, even if it "does not seem like much."
  • High-value recognition tokens: cookies, flowers, or a framed photograph of the patient with their new adoptive family. The photo is specifically noted as deeply meaningful to recipients.
Exam Trap: Fees must be settled BEFORE services are rendered Discussing financial terms after the fact creates awkwardness and potential conflict. The correct sequence is: agree on fee structure first (full/reduced/at cost/pro bono), then proceed with the clinical service. This applies even for specialist colleagues with whom you have an existing relationship.

Strategic Planning and Protocols

Administrative infrastructure, not just clinical skill, determines shelter outcomes. Strategic plans set direction; policies set boundaries; protocols deliver consistency. Training ensures every staff member can execute both safely.

Strategic planning Ch. 5ASV 2022

  • Definition: the organizational process used to define the shelter's essential programs and goals, then purposefully allocate resources toward achieving those goals.
  • Review frequency: strategic plans are most effective when reviewed regularly, often quarterly, to ensure progress is being made and goals remain relevant.
  • External guidance: when developing organizational policies and protocols, administrators are encouraged to consult industry-specific professional organizations (e.g., ASV, NACA) for guidance and to learn from the experience of others in the field.

Policies vs. Protocols Ch. 5

Exam Trap: Policies safeguard C4C Policies serve an explicit population-management function: they prevent animal needs from overwhelming the resources available to meet those needs. Operating beyond C4C is unacceptable; policies are the administrative mechanism that prevents it. The exam may frame a policy question as a C4C question.

Consistency in intake and euthanasia criteria Ch. 5

  • The intake and euthanasia areas are the most common sources of staff conflict and bad feelings in shelters.
  • The root cause is inconsistent or ambiguous application of criteria, which creates a "gray zone."
  • The solution is consistent, clearly documented criteria applied uniformly. Removing the gray zone removes the emotional burden from individual staff members who otherwise feel responsible for case-by-case life-or-death decisions.

Training requirements ASV 2022

Mandatory training topics

  • Zoonotic disease information: must be provided to all staff members. This is an absolute obligation, not optional.
  • Proper animal handling skills
  • Animal body language
  • Bite prevention strategies

Broader training curriculum

  • Communication techniques
  • Data management
  • Animal husbandry (beyond mandatory zoonotic/safety content)
  • Staff well-being and resilience
  • Diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI)

Documentation requirement

  • Documentation of all completed training must be maintained and reviewed regularly as part of professional development and performance reviews.
  • Undocumented training creates the same administrative vulnerability as no training at all.

Legal compliance ASV 2022

  • Shelters are legally obligated to comply with all local, state, and national regulations.
  • These regulations must be reviewed regularly, as they change.
  • Non-compliance is not mitigated by organizational mission or financial constraints.

Zoonotic Disease Training: Management Obligation ASV 2022

Providing comprehensive zoonotic disease information to all staff and volunteers is an absolute administrative requirement — not an optional health promotion activity. This obligation extends to paid staff, credentialed team members, and volunteers alike.

Required content

  • Identification of the major zoonotic diseases present in the shelter environment: ringworm (dermatophytosis), rabies exposure protocols, leptospirosis, pasteurellosis (bite wound), salmonella, and MRSP/MRSA.
  • Personal protective equipment requirements for each category of zoonotic risk (gloves, masks, eye protection for high-risk handling).
  • Specific high-risk populations who require enhanced briefing: pregnant staff or volunteers (toxoplasmosis, ringworm, salmonella), immunocompromised individuals (cryptosporidiosis, salmonella, campylobacter), and those with animal bite histories (pasteurellosis, rabies exposure cascade).
  • What to do after a potential zoonotic exposure: bite/scratch wound care protocol, when to seek immediate medical attention, who to notify within the shelter hierarchy, and which exposures require mandatory reporting to occupational health or public health authorities.

Documentation mandate

  • Zoonotic disease training must be formally documented for every staff member and volunteer. Documentation date, training content, and trainer identity should all be recorded.
  • New hire orientation must include this training before independent work with animals begins — not after a trial period.
  • Annual refresher training is recommended; required when a new zoonotic risk is identified in the shelter population (e.g., first ringworm outbreak of the season, confirmed Brucella case).
Exam Trap: Undocumented training = untrained If a manager provides verbal zoonotic disease guidance but does not document it, the shelter has the same legal exposure as if no training occurred. Documentation transforms the training from an informal conversation into a defensible occupational health record. This principle is identical to any other training documentation requirement.

2024 analysis of compensation, job duties, and benefits among US shelter medicine veterinarians
Rachael E. Kreisler, Miranda E. Spindel, Mark Rishniw · Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association · 2025

Abstract. OBJECTIVE: To understand the salary, benefits, hours, and job duties of shelter medicine veterinarians. METHODS: Similar to a 2018 survey, an anonymous survey on compensation and duties of shelter medicine veterinarians was conducted via a commercial platform from January 4 to 31, 2024. The survey was distributed through social media, message boards, and specialty listservs. Participants were categorized into part-time clinical veterinarians, full-time clinical veterinarians, academic faculty, and leadership/consultants. RESULTS: 299 US shelter veterinarians responded: 258 were engaged in clinical work (198 working full time and 60 part time), 18 worked in academia, and 23 held leadership/consulting roles. Full-time clinical veterinarians reported a median salary of $122,500, reflecting a 25% increase since 2018. Part-time clinicians earned $85/h-a 77% increase-while working a median of 24 h/wk. Faculty had a median salary of $130,000, a 20% increase. Those in leadership or consulting roles earned a median of $168,000, a 40% increase. CONCLUSIONS: Salaries increased across all categories, with the highest increase for leadership/consulting roles and the lowest for academic positions. Part-time veterinarians saw a large rise in hourly rates, while working fewer hours. Leadership positions yielded the highest incomes, followed by faculty. Clinical salaries were 5% lower than the AVMA-reported 2022 salaries for private practice veterinarians and 7% lower for companion animal veterinarians. CLINICAL RELEVANCE: Understanding these compensation trends assists shelter veterinarians in making informed career decisions and negotiations. Despite salary increases, shelter veterinarians' earnings remain lower than those in private practice.

A new future for dog breeding
Proschowsky · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.1016/j.ygeno.2006.07.007

Abstract. The modern idea of purebred dogs has come under increasing critical scrutiny over recent decades. In light of this critical focus and other developments in society, some new trends in how companion dogs are bred and acquired have emerged. This means a diminishing influence from traditional kennel clubs with more dogs being sold without a pedigree, stricter legal restrictions on dog breeding, growing popularity of deliberate crosses of established breeds (i.e. so-called designer breeds) and growing hype around the benefits of mixed-breed dogs. We give an overview of these trends and discuss to what extent they will serve to promote dogs that are innately healthy, have good welfare and function well in their various roles in today's world. We argue that newly invented designer breeds and mixed breeds also have worrying health and behavioural problems, and that the predictability of purebred dogs with respect to body size, basic behaviours, known need for grooming, disorder profiles and other attributes may well offer some benefits for a satisfying human-dog relationship seen from both sides. The optimal future seems to lie in the middle ground, where the future organised dog world (i.e. kennel and breed clubs or their successor organisations) will need to re-open the breed registries, remove wording from breed standards that currently promotes extreme conformation, support selection against disease-predisposing genotypes and phenotypes and refocus dog showing and breeding to promote health and appropriate behaviour.

Cross-cultural variation in understanding of animal welfare principles and animal management practices among veterinary and animal welfare professionals in the UK and Japan
Otani · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.1080/10871209.2023.2234389

Abstract. The World Organisation for Animal Health describes animal welfare as a "complex and multi-faceted subject with scientific, ethical, economic, cultural, social, religious and political dimensions." In this study, an online survey in English and Japanese was developed based on the Five Freedoms, with the aim of investigating attitudes of veterinarians and behaviour/welfare scientists in the United Kingdom (UK) and Japan toward management of companion, farmed, experimental, zoo and wildlife animals. Respondents from the UK (n = 212) were more familiar with the Five Freedoms than those from Japan (n = 321) but both countries tended to prioritise 'survival-related' attributes (health and nutrition) over 'situation-related' attributes (behaviour) and the environmental impacts (discomfort). In Japan, however, fewer respondents recognised the 'Freedom to express normal behaviour' as important for domesticated animals compared to UK respondents. When considering vignettes with practical situations of cat management and dog euthanasia, UK respondents considered the provision of outdoor access to represent better management for cat welfare while most Japanese respondents thought cats should be managed entirely indoors, although the benefits and risks of going outdoors were similarly recognised in both countries. For the vignette of dog pain relating to an incurable tumour, severe pain and the dog's mental stress motivated respondents from both countries to consider euthanasia. However, for Japanese respondents, the data suggested a perception that mental stress did not have an association with the dog's inabilities to express normal behaviour. These data highlighted the importance of understanding the manner in which people perceive animals in different contexts and the value of considering different cultural approaches.

Exploring buyer's preferences to propose strategies to improve dog welfare in Brazil
Dias Rodrigues · Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · 2025 · 10.1080/10888705.2023.2282972

Abstract. This study aimed to investigate trends and concerns related to the commercialization of dogs in Brazil. The authors analyzed 1,500 ads for the sale of Miniature Schnauzer, Chihuahua, and German Spitz breeds using the methodology of hedonic price analysis. Marginal and relative values of attributes were calculated for puppy sex, coat, age, pedigree, vaccination and deworming, seller type and region, using ordinary least squares. The most valued attributes for each breed were identified as selling by kennels, pedigree, and coat color. The results suggest that this market niche is willing to pay for guarantees of better dog breeding, which represents an opportunity for the development of certification labels for origin and good practices for kennels. This study also provides valuable insights for the development of guidelines and public policies aimed at improving animal welfare and reducing information asymmetry in the pet market.

Life expectancy and survival analysis for companion dogs and cats in Seoul, South Korea
Yang · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2025 · 10.1136/vr.145.22.625

Abstract. BACKGROUND: Investigating life expectancy and mortality is crucial for the development of evidence-based health strategies for companion animals. However, relevant studies are lacking in South Korea, possibly because of challenges in collecting mortality data. In this regard, preliminary analyses were conducted to obtain life tables for companion animals in South Korea. METHODS: The electronic records of six veterinary hospitals in Seoul, South Korea were examined. The data collected included breed, sex, spay/neuter status, date of birth, and date of death for all dogs and cats with a verifiable date of death since November 1, 2004 until December 31, 2022. After data preprocessing, descriptive statistical analysis was performed to summarize the demographics, and life tables and survival curves were created for dogs and cats. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to analyze the effects of demographic factors on survival. RESULTS: The mean age of dogs at death was 3427.49 days. Spayed or neutered dogs had a significantly higher life expectancy than intact dogs. Mixed-breed dogs had a higher life expectancy than purebred dogs. For cats, the mean age at death was 1965.49 days, with spayed or neutered cats living significantly longer than intact cats. Purebred cats had a higher median survival than Mixed-breed cats. Spaying or neutering and breed significantly affected survival probabilities in both species. CONCLUSION: Our study provides insights into the longevity of companion animals in South Korea, and reveals that neutering and breed significantly influence life expectancy.

No one cares about the animal like me.’ Indian veterinarians’ experiences of improving animal welfare through Continuing Professional Development
Rayner E, et al · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.1017/awf.2025.3

Abstract. Veterinarians are custodians of animal welfare, ensuring practices remain current and effective in the face of the ever-changing demands of the profession. Continuing Professional Development (CPD) is essential for protecting animal welfare, however access to quality CPD is a challenge in many countries. India has one of the fastest growing veterinary industries globally, emphasising the importance of accessible CPD opportunities that are relevant to this setting. This study used focus groups to explore how Indian veterinarians identify relevant CPD, barriers they encounter, and their experiences with CPD. We describe three themes: (1) ‘career vs calling’, where veterinarians’ extrinsic and intrinsic motivational factors were identified, such as their desire to protect animal welfare; (2) being ‘willing to learn but can’t’, with context-specific barriers, such as accessing reliable CPD information; and (3) ‘real interactions matter’, where participants described how pedagogical design influenced their choices, e.g. being able to observe animal welfare improvements through practical teaching. We have three recommendations: firstly, to improve CPD learning opportunities informed by evidence-based methods, to meet knowledge and skills gaps such as the high demand for practically focused training; secondly, the development of a unified accreditation and quality assurance framework to assess content, relevance and delivery standards of available CPD options to veterinarians; and, lastly, improved support from employers to address current barriers and facilitate attendance. These findings contribute to the current knowledge gap of factors that influence Indian veterinarians’ experiences of attaining relevant, accessible CPD and makes suggestions to improve standards of veterinary care and, ultimately, patient welfare.

One welfare: bibliometric review of world literature
Platto · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.ijvsm.2018.07.003

Abstract. The One Welfare framework emphasizes the interconnection between animal, human, and environmental well-being, extending One Health principles to address broader welfare dimensions. Despite its relevance, One Welfare remains underexplored. This study investigates global research trends and thematic priorities in One Welfare literature published from 2013 to 2024. A bibliometric review was conducted using PubMed, Elsevier, Springer, Web of Science, Scopus, and CABI databases. A literature search was conducted using keywords translated into five of the world's most widely spoken languages: Hindi, Chinese, Spanish, English, and French. A total of 111 publications were identified and categorized into four main domains-Policy, Governance, Economy (PGE); Applied Human-Animal Sciences (AHAS); Societal, Economic, Environmental Dimensions (SEED); and Human-Animal Bond and Mental Health (HAB-MH)-and eight subcategories: Legal Framework and Economy (LFE); Education and Philosophy (EP); Sustainable Resource Management (SRM); Traditional Knowledge and Societal Impact (TKSI); Animal Management (AM); Human-Animal Diseases (HAD); Human-Animal Interaction (HAI); and Psychology (PSY). The analysis also considered animal types-companion animals (CA), production animals (PA), wild animals (WA), working/sport animals (WS), and general (GE)-and divided data into two time periods (2013-2018, 2019-2024).
