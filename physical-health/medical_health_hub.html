Foundations & Capacity

Medical care in a shelter is proactive and population-aware: it starts at or before intake, balances the individual against the group, and is bounded by a defined capacity to provide care. These foundations frame every decision in the rest of the subdomain.

Medical (Non-Infectious) Health

Speed

The health mandate and care timeline ASV

  • Health is defined (after the World Health Organization) as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.
  • Shelter medical care must begin at or before intake and continue throughout the stay. When a shelter admits an animal, it becomes responsible for all needed medical and wellness care, or for promptly finding an outcome that meets those needs.
  • Preventive pillars: vaccination, parasite control, good nutrition, and appropriate handling and housing location. (These have their own subdomains; here they are named as the framework, not detailed.)

The Five Freedoms standard ref

The original welfare benchmark for assessing quality of life across animal-care settings (FAWC; origin Brambell 1965). It is high-yield because the ASV wrote its Guidelines for Standards of Care in Animal Shelters with the Five Freedoms as its explicit basis, so every shelter medical and husbandry practice should support them.

FreedomProvided by
1 · From hunger & thirstReady access to fresh water and a diet that maintains full health and vigor.
2 · From discomfortAn appropriate environment, including shelter and a comfortable resting area.
3 · From pain, injury & diseasePrevention, or rapid diagnosis and treatment.
4 · From fear & distressConditions and treatment that avoid mental suffering.
5 · To express normal behaviorSufficient space, proper facilities, and the company of the animal's own kind.
Why it mattersLinking each protocol back to a welfare goal is the point of the framework. Note that four of the five are "freedoms from" unpleasant states; good welfare also requires the presence of positive experiences, not merely the absence of negative ones.

Capacity to provide medical care ASV

Population versus individualIndividual care is balanced against population health. Population health suffers when disease spread is likely, when long lengths of stay push the shelter over its capacity for care, and when one animal's treatment costs drain the resources available to others.

Oversight, Records & Stewardship

A shelter's medical program runs on a formal veterinary relationship, written evidence-based protocols, disciplined prescribing, and complete records. This tab also covers the supervision definitions and telemedicine, which determine who may do what, and where.

Veterinary supervision ASV PS

  • Every shelter should have a formal relationship with a veterinarian who has direct knowledge of the organization, its population, and its facilities.
  • Preventive protocols are developed in consultation with this veterinarian and may be implemented by trained staff under indirect supervision. Treatment of common conditions by trained staff may also occur under indirect supervision per written protocol, or by telephonic or written instruction.
TermDefinition
Direct supervisionA licensed veterinarian is readily available on the premises and has assumed responsibility for the care given.
Indirect supervisionThe veterinarian need not be on the premises, has given written or oral instructions, is readily available by phone or immediate communication, and has assumed responsibility.
Direct knowledgeThe veterinarian regularly visits and examines the shelter population in person.
Written protocolA document detailing standard care for a condition or procedure (policy, disease recognition, dose and route, diagnostics, environmental risk, adverse-effect response, storage and handling, documentation).
Where this comes fromThese definitions track the 2019 AVMA Model Practice Act, cited by the ASV Veterinary Supervision position statement (2021). Most preventive and common-condition care in shelters runs under indirect supervision.

Telemedicine and the VCPR ASV PS

  • Telemedicine is the use of remote communication technology to provide medical examination and treatment. It broadens access, removes geographic barriers, reduces travel stress, and supports biosecurity.
  • Veterinarians use professional discretion to decide whether telemedicine is appropriate for a given diagnosis or judgment.
  • The ASV supports legislation allowing a veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) to be created through telephone, digital, or electronic communication, and allowing prescribing through such a VCPR.
  • Keep medical records for each encounter, ensure follow-up, and respect the jurisdictional limits of veterinary licenses.

Evidence-based protocols and the prescribing standard ASV

  • Evidence-based protocols are essential and must be developed in consultation with the shelter's veterinarian. Ensuring compliance, at population and individual level, is part of veterinary oversight.
  • Protocols include six areas: diagnosis, treatment, animal housing, sanitation, decision-making, and communication. When a concern falls outside standard protocols or does not respond as expected, the veterinarian must be consulted.
  • Prescribing standard: medications only by prescription or written protocol, requiring a reasonable presumptive diagnosis, the ability to administer as directed, and a plan to monitor so success or failure can be judged.
  • Antibiotic stewardship: giving medications when not needed, such as antibiotics to prevent a viral infection, causes harmful side effects and promotes antibiotic resistance.
  • Drug use and dispensing must follow federal and state regulations, which may limit off-label and compounded drugs.

Records and the prescription label ASVnotes

  • Medical records include identifying information; signalment (age, sex, species, reproductive status); and a dated list of exam findings, vaccinations, diagnostic results, procedures, and treatments (with drug dose and route). A copy must be provided when the animal leaves the shelter's care.
  • Prescription drug label, seven components: name of the prescribing veterinarian; clinic or shelter name, phone, and address; patient ID and species; date dispensed and expiration date; drug name, form, and amount; directions for use; and cautionary statements.

Program quality and surveillance ASVcorrected

  • Track disease rates and outcomes to measure population health. Deficiency indicators: a decline in animal health and welfare after intake; sick or injured animals held without prompt treatment; wide-scale disease outbreaks; animals dying or euthanized from shelter-acquired disease or injury; and chronically high disease rates.
  • Population health surveillance: track morbidity and mortality trends and develop targeted strategies; individual and population health monitoring cannot exist without each other.
CorrectionYour notes list "side scale disease outbreaks." The ASV term is wide-scale disease outbreaks. It is a typo, but a key term, so it is corrected here.

Monitoring & Examination

From intake onward, every animal is assessed on a defined cadence. Knowing the exact intervals (intake, daily, monthly, six-monthly) and the FeLV/FIV testing logic is high-yield.

The problem-oriented approach standard ref

The framework widely accepted as the gold standard for small-animal patient care and assessment. A problem is any abnormality requiring medical or surgical management, or one that interferes with quality of life (Lorenz); in a shelter, problems also include public-health and safety concerns. The clinical reasoning runs through four steps.

StepWhat happens
1 · Database collectionObtain a minimum database on every patient: a complete history plus a complete physical exam, including behavioral observation.
2 · Problem identificationList the historical, physical, behavioral, welfare, and public-health/safety problems revealed by the database, stated at their current level of understanding.
3 · Plan formulationFor each problem, plan the necessary diagnostic testing, initial therapy, and staff/adopter education, within the shelter's mission, resources, and legal holding periods.
4 · Assessment & follow-upMake clinical appraisals, document progression over time, and reassess regularly (critical for long-stay animals) to update known problems and catch new ones.
Minimum database & geriatric add-onsAt intake the minimum database is history, physical exam (including scanning for a microchip), and behavioral observation; prior to adoption add FeLV/FIV testing (cats), heartworm testing (dogs in endemic areas), and a fecal exam if diarrhea is present. For geriatric animals offered for adoption, add PCV/TS and urine specific gravity + dipstick, and, when time and resources allow, FNA cytology of all lumps and bumps.

The monitoring cadence ASVnotes

  • Intake: every animal gets at least a cursory health assessment by trained personnel (confirm age, sex, description, identification, microchip); a comprehensive physical exam is ideally performed within 24 hours of intake. Animals with signs of infectious disease are isolated until determined low-risk.
  • Daily: trained personnel must visually observe every animal at least once every 24 hours, ideally before cleaning, noting food intake and enclosure condition (feces, urine, vomit).
  • Monthly: reweigh and repeat body condition score.
  • Every 6 months: a comprehensive exam for long-term residents, including those in foster.

Exam trapThe ASV does not recommend euthanasia of a clinically healthy cat based on a single positive point-of-care retroviral test. Confirmatory testing follows the current AAFP guidelines, and complete history (test date and type) goes with the cat at adoption or transfer.

Quarantine versus isolation ASV

  • Isolation separates animals with suspected or confirmed infectious disease until diagnosis or treatment determines them low-risk.
  • Quarantine of healthy animals at intake is not generally recommended; it is appropriate only for animals with a history of direct, high-risk infectious-disease exposure. Unnecessary holds increase length of stay and harm welfare.
Exam trapBlanket intake quarantine of healthy animals is a distractor. It lengthens stays without benefit. Reserve quarantine for documented high-risk exposure.

Pain Management

Pain control is mandatory, multimodal, and pre-emptive where possible. Failure to treat pain is explicitly unacceptable, and euthanasia is a legitimate part of treatment when suffering cannot be relieved.

Recognizing and treating pain ASV

  • Pain must be recognized and treated to alleviate suffering. Failure to provide treatment for pain is unacceptable. Treatment of pain can include providing euthanasia.
  • Assess pain through behavior and knowledge of causes; if a condition is painful in humans, assume it is painful in animals. Several validated pain scales exist.
  • Unrelieved pain is a significant welfare concern and can cause weight loss, muscle breakdown, increased blood pressure, prolonged recovery, and mental and emotional distress.

Pre-emptive, multimodal, and reassessed ASVnotes

  • Pain control must be of an appropriate strength and duration to preempt or relieve pain. When pain can be anticipated, as with surgery, provide control before the painful event.
  • The use of controlled drugs must be supervised by a veterinarian as required by statute.
  • Non-pharmacological methods that supplement drugs: the presence of littermates, a quiet environment, massage, physical therapy, heat, and deep bedding. These increase comfort and reduce anxiety.
  • Reassess animals frequently to judge the efficacy of pain relief. When relief is inadequate, emergency medical care must be provided.
NoteNon-pharmacological methods supplement, not replace, analgesia. They are adjuncts to a veterinary pain-control protocol.

Emergency & Health-Concern Response

Every shelter needs a written plan for timely emergency care, a structured triage method, command of the core emergency drugs, a clear rule for when euthanasia relieves suffering, and the principle that legal status never blocks treatment. Infectious-disease and outbreak response round out the framework.

The emergency medical plan ASVnotes

  • Every shelter must have an established, written emergency medical plan to provide appropriate, timely veterinary care for any animal that is injured, in distress, or showing signs of significant illness.
  • The plan must indicate how staff recognize and report conditions needing emergency care, and whether services are on-site or at an outside clinic. Animals in foster or off-site are covered by the same guidance, with clear after-hours instructions.
  • Three execution paths guarantee prompt care: on-site stabilization and immediate treatment; immediate transfer to a partnered 24-hour emergency or specialty facility; or prompt, humane euthanasia by qualified personnel (as local law permits) when suffering cannot be alleviated.
  • Staff-training mandate: a shelter cannot rely on veterinary presence alone. Non-veterinary care staff must be trained to actively recognize critical conditions so they can trigger the emergency protocol without delay.

Triage: the ABC primary survey standard ref

Triage is the rapid assessment of animals entering or residing in the facility to determine physiological criticality and prioritize treatment. Work the primary survey in order: Airway, Breathing, Circulation.

Minimum database and operational controls standard refASV

  • The "big five" minimum database for a critical case: PCV and total protein (hidden hemorrhage, anemia, dehydration); blood glucose (pediatric and neonatal triage); BUN and creatinine (renal perfusion baseline); urine specific gravity or dipstick (separates pre-renal dehydration from primary renal failure before aggressive fluids); and a thorough physical exam.
  • Expired-drug rule: avoid using expired pharmaceuticals. They compromise efficacy and patient safety and undermine the shelter legally by raising questions about the baseline quality of care.
  • Equipment acquisition: shelters may legally accept donated capital equipment (fluid pumps, anesthesia machines, forced-air warmers) and unexpired drugs from private practices or human hospitals; verify calibration and log items in inventory.
  • Written medical-record mandate: each animal has a distinct, unalterable record with its unique shelter ID and signalment, objective exam findings, a working diagnosis or problem list, and prescribed treatments (exact dose, route, frequency, and administrator initials).

Legal status does not bar treatment ASV PS

  • The legal status of an animal must never interfere with necessary preventive care or treatment of painful injuries, significant illness, debilitating conditions, or urgent behavioral needs.
  • When a shelter is legally required to hold such an animal (stray, evidence, boarder), it must enact a plan to provide adequate treatment or seek alternative placement.
  • If suffering cannot be alleviated, the shelter should seek permission to euthanize. Agreements with relevant parties can clarify emergency-care expectations in advance.

Infectious disease and outbreak response ASV

  • Animals with suspected infectious disease must be isolated until a veterinarian determines them low-risk. Allowing animals with severe infectious disease to remain in the general population is unacceptable.
  • An outbreak is more than the usual number of affected animals, or an increase in case severity. Response: risk assessment to identify exposed animals, physical separation of sick, exposed, at-risk, and unexposed groups, and possibly halting animal movement or intake.
  • Monitor at-risk animals for disease at least once a day; limit animal handling and foot traffic; avoid returning recovered or exposed animals to the general population while transmission risk remains.
  • Follow reportable-disease laws. Depopulation is a last resort for extraordinary circumstances, not an initial outbreak response (see the Euthanasia hub).
Exam trapFootbaths often become contaminated and aid transmission rather than control it. Do not treat a footbath as a primary outbreak control.

An Interview-Based Retrospective Study on Long-Term Outcomes of Shelter Cats with Feline Lower Urinary Tract Disease (FLUTD) and a History of Urethral Obstruction (UO)
Mendoza K, Ferrell E, Rekers W · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2026 · 10.56771/jsmcah.v5.169

Abstract. Historical shelter practices have sometimes involved euthanasia for cats with FLUTD and urethral obstruction due to concerns about adoptability and prognosis. While recurrent urinary issues occur in up to 51% of affected cats and obstruction recurs in 15-47%, this retrospective study examined actual adoption outcomes. Researchers surveyed adopters of 78 shelter cats with prior obstruction, achieving a 39.7% response rate. Inclusion required at least one documented obstruction event requiring 24+ hours of catheterization, absence of other conditions, successful adoption, and minimum three-month residence post-event. All cats were alive and remained in their adopted homes with only 32.3% showing recurrent symptoms and 6.5% experiencing re-obstruction. Housing environment, household composition, and diet showed no statistical association with recurrence. All respondents expressed willingness to adopt similar cats again, indicating positive experiences and favorable long-term outcomes despite study limitations.

Impact of Plasma Supplementation on Survival and Clinical Illness in Orphaned Neonatal Kittens in a Retrospective Shelter Cohort
Chapman J, Piraino A, Min K, Diniz PPVP, Noble J, Hedge Z · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2026 · 10.56771/jsmcah.v5.143

Abstract. The San Diego Humane Society manages numerous orphaned kittens annually who face elevated illness and mortality risks. This retrospective cohort study examined whether subcutaneous plasma administration at intake affected outcomes in 544 orphaned kittens (193 receiving plasma, 351 controls) between February and August 2015. The investigation focused on two primary outcomes: mortality within 30 days and time until onset of clinical signs including diarrhea, upper respiratory infection, hypothermia, anorexia, weight loss, or neurological symptoms. Plasma supplementation was not statistically associated with decreased mortality risk at any time during the first 30 days despite early survival curve differences. However, plasma administration did delay onset of diarrhea and weight loss. The research identified intake weight and weight gain as the most important survival predictors, suggesting nutritional support's critical role in kitten outcomes within shelter settings.

International Renal Interest Society best practice consensus guidelines for the diagnosis and management of acute kidney injury in cats and dogs
Segev · Vet Journal · 2024

Abstract. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as an injury to the renal parenchyma, with or without a decrease in kidney function, as reflected by accumulation of uremic toxins or altered urine production (i.e., increased or decreased). AKI might result from any of several factors, including ischemia, inflammation, nephrotoxins, and infectious diseases. AKI can be community- or hospital-acquired. The latter was not previously considered a common cause for AKI in animals; however, recent evidence suggests that the prevalence of hospital-acquired AKI is increasing in veterinary medicine. This is likely due to a combination of increased recognition and awareness of AKI, as well as increased treatment intensity (e.g., ventilation and prolonged hospitalization) in some veterinary patients and increased management of geriatric veterinary patients with multiple comorbidities.

Advancements in the management of AKI, including the increased availability of renal replacement therapies, have been made; however, the overall mortality of animals with AKI remains high. Despite the high prevalence of AKI and the high mortality rate, the body of evidence regarding the diagnosis and the management of AKI in veterinary medicine is very limited. Consequently, the International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) constructed a working group to provide guidelines for animals with AKI. Recommendations are based on the available literature and the clinical experience of the members of the working group and reflect consensus of opinion. Fifty statements were generated and were voted on in all aspects of AKI and explanatory text can be found either before or after each statement

Malassezia dermatitis in dogs and cats
Hobi · Vet Journal · 2024

Abstract. Malassezia are members of the mycobiome of dogs and cats. In the presence of an underlying disease, these yeasts can proliferate, attach to the skin or mucosa to induce a secondary Malassezia dermatitis, otitis externa or paronychia. Since allergic dermatitis is one of the most common underlying causes, diagnostic investigation for allergy is often indicated. Cats may suffer from various other underlying problems, especially where Malassezia dermatitis is generalised. Malassezia dermatitis in dogs and cats is chronic, relapsing and pruritic. Direct cytology from dermatological lesions and the ear canal, showing “peanut-shaped” budding yeasts, facilitates a rapid and reliable diagnosis. Topical treatment includes antiseptic and antifungal azole-based products. Systemic treatment with oral antifungals is indicated only in severe or refractory disease. Identification and treatment of the underlying cause is essential for an optimal response. In this evidence-based narrative review, we discuss the clinical presentation of Malassezia dermatitis in dogs and cats, underlying comorbidities, and diagnostic considerations. Treatment is discussed in light of emerging evidence of antifungal resistance and the authors’ clinical experience.

Neurological adverse effects of isoxazoline exposure in cats and dogs
Bates · Vet Record · 2024

Abstract. Background

Isoxazolines are rarely reported to be associated with neurological adverse events in cats and dogs, but information about the onset and duration of neurological signs is lacking in the summary of product characteristics of these medicines. Methods

The Veterinary Poisons Information Service and the Dutch Poisons Information Center databases were searched using the Veterinary Dictionary for Drug-Related Affairs terms for ataxia, muscle tremor, convulsions or hyperesthesia in cats and dogs exposed to isoxazolines. Results

There were 22 cases with and 57 cases without outcome information, mostly involving fluralaner or sarolaner. In both groups, muscle tremors and convulsions were the most common signs. In dogs, neurological signs occurred with oral therapeutic dose and overdosage. In cats, most fluralaner cases involved therapeutic topical exposure, and all sarolaner cases involved oral exposure. In all cases with outcome information, the animals recovered. Limitations

Cases discussed with poison centres tend to involve more severe signs. Conclusion

The true incidence of neurological adverse effects from isoxazolines remains unclear. The delay between the administration and onset of signs can be long, and the association may be missed. A lack of timing information in the summary of product characteristics could also contribute to missed attribution of adverse effects.

Prevalence of osteoarthritis in the shoulder, elbow, hip and stifle joints of dogs older than 8 years
Roitner · Vet Journal · 2024

Abstract. Osteoarthritis is one of the most common diseases in veterinary medicine. There are various causes for joints developing OA, with some of them being well investigated, while others are still a matter of speculation. In this retrospective study we examined the prevalence of OA in the shoulder, elbow, hip and stifle joints in a clinic population of dogs older than 8 years, which were presented mostly due to orthopaedic complaints. Dogs were included in the study if one or more of the aforementioned joints was included in the radiographs. Radiographs were reviewed by three different observers and graded by severity. Prevalence of OA was 39.2%, 57.4%, 35.9% and 36.4% for the shoulder, elbow, hip and stifle, respectively. There was no correlation between higher grades of OA and weight as well as age, but significantly higher prevalence of OA in heavier groups when grouped for weight. A deterioration in the score in at least one of the indicators during the stay in the shelter was recorded in 52 (41.6%) of 125 cats that were assessed at least twice. The effect of the LOS, sex and age on the scores for each health indicator was examined via a linear mixed model analysis, as this method allows for handling of dependencies in the data of repeated measurements. The effect of predictors on the third eye visibility scores was not found. The age of cats predicted the coat condition and body condition scores. The LOS predicted scores of the abnormal posture and lameness indicator and scores of a composite index composed of indicators related to upper respiratory tract disease. The results suggest that despite the fact that some improvement of health was documented during the cats' stay in the shelter, there were a non-negligible number of animals experiencing a permanent or long-term deterioration in health. Efforts to minimize the undesirable factors contributing to the deterioration of well-being of cats during their stay in a shelter should be made.

Clinical features, treatment and outcome of discospondylitis in cats
Gomes · Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery · 2021

Abstract. Objectives: There is a paucity of information on feline discospondylitis. This study aimed to describe the signalment, clinical and laboratory findings, aetiological agents, treatment and outcome in cats affected by discospondylitis. Methods: This was a retrospective review of the medical records of cats diagnosed with discospondylitis at four referral institutions. Results: A total of 17 cats were identified. Most were domestic shorthair cats (76.5%) and male (58.8%), with a median age of 9 years (range 0.9-14) and a median duration of clinical signs of 3 weeks (range 0.3-16). All cats presented with spinal hyperaesthesia; 3/17 had pyrexia. Neurological dysfunction was found in 64.7% of cats, which was indicative of a T3-L3 or L4-S2 spinal segment, associated nerve root or associated nerve neurolocalisation. Haematology, serum biochemistry and urinalysis revealed occasional inconsistent non-specific changes. All cats underwent urine culture; 9/17 cats also had a distinct tissue cultured. Positive bacterial cultures were obtained in two cats (11.8%) for Staphylococcus species (urine, blood and intradiscal fine-needle aspirate) and Escherichia coli (urine); both presented with multifocal discospondylitis. Treatment was non-surgical in all cats, with sustained antibiotic therapy for a median of 3 months (range 1-9). Analgesia provided included non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, alone or in combination with gabapentin. Restricted exercise was advised for a minimum of 4 weeks. Outcome information available in 12 cats was excellent in terms of pain control and neurological function in 10 cats (83.3%) at the time of stopping antibiotics. Recurrence occurred in one case, which had received a single antibiotic for 6 weeks, and relapsed 4 months after presentation. One other case failed to improve and was euthanased during the course of hospitalisation. Conclusions and relevance: Feline discospondylitis is uncommon and no obvious signalment predisposition was found in this study. Spinal hyperaesthesia was universally present, with neurological dysfunction also highly prevalent. Bacterial culture was unrewarding in most cases. Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid or cephalosporins are reasonable choices for first-line antibiotics. Prognosis was favourable, with no long-term evidence of recurrence in cats on sustained antibiotic therapy, for a mean duration of 3 months.

Early maternal separation is not associated with changes in telomere length in domestic kittens ( Felis catus)
Delgado · PeerJ · 2021

Abstract. Objective: Studies of multiple species have found that adverse early life experiences, including childhood trauma and maternal separation, can result in accelerated telomere shortening. The objective of this study was to determine if premature separation from the mother affected telomere length in domestic kittens (Felis catus). Subjects were 42 orphaned kittens and 10 mother-reared kittens from local animal rescue groups and shelters. DNA was extracted from whole blood collected from kittens at approximately 1 week and 2 months of age. Telomere length was assessed by qPCR (quantitative polymerase chain reaction) from a total of 86 samples and expressed as a ratio of telomere PCR relative to a single copy gene PCR (T/S). Results: A generalized linear mixed model found there were no detectable differences in telomere length based on survival (F 1, 76.2 = 3.35, p = 0.07), orphan status (F 1, 56.5 = 0.44, p = 0.51), time point (F 1, 43.5 = 0.19, p = 0.67), or the interaction between orphan status and time (F 1, 43.5 = 0.86, p = 0.36). Although in other species telomere shortening is commonly associated with aging, even early in life, we did not find evidence for telomere shortening by two months of age. Our results suggest that the experience of early maternal separation in domestic cats who are subsequently hand-reared by humans does not accelerate telomere shortening compared to mother-reared kittens, at least in the first few months of life.

Pre-mortem risk factors for mortality in kittens less than 8 weeks old at a dedicated kitten nursery
Dolan, Doyle, Tran, Slater · Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery · 2021

Abstract. Objectives: Kittens have unique requirements for care in a shelter setting given their higher susceptibility to infectious disease and socialization needs. Significant time and resources are necessary to care for this vulnerable population and dedicated kitten nurseries are one way to meet the requirements of kittens too young for neutering and rehoming. However, young kittens remain at a higher risk of dying relative to adult cats, even in specialized settings. Efforts to investigate kitten mortality have focused on post-mortem findings and little is known about pre-mortem clinical signs that may be associated with death. The purpose of this study was to elucidate predictors of mortality in underage kittens. Methods: The medical records of kittens aged <8 weeks reared in a kitten nursery in New York City during 2017 were examined. The data collected included signalment (estimated age and weight at intake, sex), physical findings (body condition score [BCS]), clinical signs (weight loss, anorexia, diarrhea, upper respiratory tract infection [URI]), diagnoses (panleukopenia, trauma), how early in the feline breeding season the kitten entered (April-November), and whether the kitten had died or was euthanized. The data were analyzed using Cox proportional hazard modeling with 1353 kittens to identify factors associated with any death or euthanasia. Results: Elevated risk of dying was found for kittens in the lightest weight group (13 times greater), diagnosed with panleukopenia (13 times greater), exhibiting weight loss (>9 times greater), diagnosed with URI (almost four times greater), exhibiting anorexia (three times greater), identified with a low BCS at intake (two times greater) and experiencing diarrhea (almost two times greater). Conclusions and relevance: These findings identify clinical signs and diagnoses that can serve as prognostic indicators for underage kitten survival in a shelter/rescue setting and can aid in enhancing protocols for monitoring, intervention and euthanasia decision-making.

Value of Probiotics in Canine and Feline Gastroenterology
Schmitz · Veterinary Clinics of North America: Small Animal Practice · 2021

Abstract. Probiotics/or synbiotics products for small animals do not fulfill the criteria required to qualify as a probiotic. Studies explaining modes of action are lacking. Outcome measures are inconsistent, with some trials assessing only nonspecific routine diagnostic parameters or fecal scores. Preliminary evidence shows that specific preparations are beneficial in parvovirus infections and acute hemorrhagic diarrhea syndrome in dogs and in Tritrichomonas fetus infection in cats. In dogs, inflammatory bowel disease specific probiotics can decrease clinical severity. More studies focusing on functional outcomes in dogs and cats with well-defined diseases to allow evidence-based clinical use of probiotics and synbiotics are needed.
