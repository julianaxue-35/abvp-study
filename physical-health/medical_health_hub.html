Foundations & Capacity

Medical care in a shelter is proactive and population-aware: it starts at or before intake, balances the individual against the group, and is bounded by a defined capacity to provide care. These foundations frame every decision in the rest of the subdomain.

Medical (Non-Infectious) Health

Speed

The health mandate and care timeline ASV

  • Health is defined (after the World Health Organization) as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.
  • Shelter medical care must begin at or before intake and continue throughout the stay. When a shelter admits an animal, it becomes responsible for all needed medical and wellness care, or for promptly finding an outcome that meets those needs.
  • Preventive pillars: vaccination, parasite control, good nutrition, and appropriate handling and housing location. (These have their own subdomains; here they are named as the framework, not detailed.)
  • Puppies and kittens are defined by the ASV Medical Health chapter as animals under 5 months of age.
  • At intake, the risk of rabies exposure is low, so rabies vaccination is not considered essential at that visit (full vaccine timing is in the Vaccination subdomain).
  • Every animal must be dewormed for roundworms and hookworms before leaving the shelter (full parasite-control protocol is in the Parasites subdomain).

The Five Freedoms standard ref

The original welfare benchmark for assessing quality of life across animal-care settings (FAWC; origin Brambell 1965). It is high-yield because the ASV wrote its Guidelines for Standards of Care in Animal Shelters with the Five Freedoms as its explicit basis, so every shelter medical and husbandry practice should support them.

FreedomProvided by
1 · From hunger & thirstReady access to fresh water and a diet that maintains full health and vigor.
2 · From discomfortAn appropriate environment, including shelter and a comfortable resting area.
3 · From pain, injury & diseasePrevention, or rapid diagnosis and treatment.
4 · From fear & distressConditions and treatment that avoid mental suffering.
5 · To express normal behaviorSufficient space, proper facilities, and the company of the animal's own kind.
Why it mattersLinking each protocol back to a welfare goal is the point of the framework. Note that four of the five are "freedoms from" unpleasant states; good welfare also requires the presence of positive experiences, not merely the absence of negative ones.

Capacity to provide medical care ASV

Population versus individualIndividual care is balanced against population health. Population health suffers when disease spread is likely, when long lengths of stay push the shelter over its capacity for care, and when one animal's treatment costs drain the resources available to others.
  • "Likelihood of recovery" in practice — kitten nurseries: a Cox proportional-hazards analysis of 1,353 kittens <8 weeks old in a dedicated kitten nursery found mortality risk rose sharply with: lightest weight class (13× greater), panleukopenia diagnosis (13×), weight loss (>9×), URI diagnosis (~4×), anorexia (3×), low body condition score at intake (2×), and diarrhea (~2×) — a concrete, ranked set of prognostic factors to fold into capacity-to-treat decisions for underage kittens (Dolan, Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Oversight, Records & Stewardship

A shelter's medical program runs on a formal veterinary relationship, written evidence-based protocols, disciplined prescribing, and complete records. This tab also covers the supervision definitions and telemedicine, which determine who may do what, and where.

Veterinary supervision ASV PS

  • Every shelter should have a formal relationship with a veterinarian who has direct knowledge of the organization, its population, and its facilities.
  • Preventive protocols are developed in consultation with this veterinarian and may be implemented by trained staff under indirect supervision. Treatment of common conditions by trained staff may also occur under indirect supervision per written protocol, or by telephonic or written instruction.
TermDefinition
Direct supervisionA licensed veterinarian is readily available on the premises and has assumed responsibility for the care given.
Indirect supervisionThe veterinarian need not be on the premises, has given written or oral instructions, is readily available by phone or immediate communication, and has assumed responsibility.
Direct knowledgeThe veterinarian regularly visits and examines the shelter population in person.
Written protocolA document detailing standard care for a condition or procedure (policy, disease recognition, dose and route, diagnostics, environmental risk, adverse-effect response, storage and handling, documentation).
Where this comes fromThese definitions track the 2019 AVMA Model Practice Act, cited by the ASV Veterinary Supervision position statement (2021). Most preventive and common-condition care in shelters runs under indirect supervision.

Telemedicine and the VCPR ASV PS

  • Telemedicine is the use of remote communication technology to provide medical examination and treatment. It broadens access, removes geographic barriers, reduces travel stress, and supports biosecurity.
  • Veterinarians use professional discretion to decide whether telemedicine is appropriate for a given diagnosis or judgment.
  • The ASV supports legislation allowing a veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) to be created through telephone, digital, or electronic communication, and allowing prescribing through such a VCPR.
  • Keep medical records for each encounter, ensure follow-up, and respect the jurisdictional limits of veterinary licenses.

Evidence-based protocols and the prescribing standard ASV

  • Evidence-based protocols are essential and must be developed in consultation with the shelter's veterinarian. Ensuring compliance, at population and individual level, is part of veterinary oversight.
  • Protocols include six areas: diagnosis, treatment, animal housing, sanitation, decision-making, and communication. When a concern falls outside standard protocols or does not respond as expected, the veterinarian must be consulted.
  • Prescribing standard: medications only by prescription or written protocol, requiring a reasonable presumptive diagnosis, the ability to administer as directed, and a plan to monitor so success or failure can be judged.
  • Antibiotic stewardship: giving medications when not needed, such as antibiotics to prevent a viral infection, causes harmful side effects and promotes antibiotic resistance.
  • Drug use and dispensing must follow federal and state regulations, which may limit off-label and compounded drugs.
  • Culture and susceptibility testing is not optional evidence-gathering — it changes outcomes: a 10-year US review of 343 hepatobiliary bacterial isolates from dogs and cats found 40% were multidrug-resistant, and MDR was significantly more common in animals with prior antibiotic exposure (81%) than without (30%) — resistance to first-line drugs (amoxicillin-clavulanate, aminopenicillins, fluoroquinolones) also rose significantly from 2010–2014 to 2015–2019 (Schlachet, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). A parallel US analysis of >344,000 urinary E. coli isolates from dogs and cats (2019–2022) found the highest resistance was to amoxicillin (~28%), with a seasonal summer peak in testing volume but no seasonal pattern in resistance itself (Ojasanya, Preventive Veterinary Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below); a UK cystocentesis-sample study similarly found high resistance in feline urinary isolates, especially amoxicillin and cephalexin resistance in Enterobacterales (>50%) (D'Aout, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Canine pyoderma antimicrobial guidelines (ISCAID): cytology before antibiotics in every case; topical therapy alone is first-line for surface/superficial pyoderma; systemic antibiotics are reserved for deep pyoderma or antibiotic-refractory superficial disease, given for 2 weeks (superficial) or 3 weeks (deep) before re-examination (Loeffler, Veterinary Dermatology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Antibiotic harm goes beyond resistance: a controlled trial giving growing Beagle puppies a 5-day course of combined metronidazole and sulfadimethoxine (15 mg/kg of each, q 24 h) found it reduced diet digestibility (dry matter, organic matter, crude protein), lowered fecal butyrate, raised intestinal permeability and serum nuclear factor κB, and reduced fecal immunoglobulin A and bacterial diversity — a concrete illustration of the collateral harm the stewardship principle above is meant to prevent (Melotto, JAVMA, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Records and the prescription label ASVnotes

  • Medical records include identifying information; signalment (age, sex, species, reproductive status); and a dated list of exam findings, vaccinations, diagnostic results, procedures, and treatments (with drug dose and route). A copy must be provided when the animal leaves the shelter's care.
  • Prescription drug label, seven components: name of the prescribing veterinarian; clinic or shelter name, phone, and address; patient ID and species; date dispensed and expiration date; drug name, form, and amount; directions for use; and cautionary statements.

Program quality and surveillance ASVcorrected

  • Track disease rates and outcomes to measure population health. Deficiency indicators: a decline in animal health and welfare after intake; sick or injured animals held without prompt treatment; wide-scale disease outbreaks; animals dying or euthanized from shelter-acquired disease or injury; and chronically high disease rates.
  • Population health surveillance: track morbidity and mortality trends and develop targeted strategies; individual and population health monitoring cannot exist without each other.
  • What shelter surveillance studies actually find: a Czech no-kill shelter review of 1,884 cats found more than half had ≥1 health record, kittens <6 months generated the most records, and antibiotics (mostly for respiratory/GI disease) were the most-used drug class (Vojtkovská, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below). A companion study of 220 group-housed shelter cats tracked over a year found 41.6% of cats assessed twice showed a deterioration in at least one welfare indicator, and length of stay predicted worsening lameness/posture and respiratory scores — longer stays are not automatically benign (Vojtkovska, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below). A Brazilian shelter's clinical-epidemiological profile (639 dogs) found common findings of enlarged lymph nodes (38.7%), skin lesions (31.5%), and thrombocytopenia (36.3%) despite 100% vaccination/deworming/desexing rates — reinforcing that routine preventive care does not eliminate the need for individual monitoring (Colocho, JAAWS, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Monitoring & Examination

From intake onward, every animal is assessed on a defined cadence. Knowing the exact intervals (intake, daily, monthly, six-monthly) and the FeLV/FIV testing logic is high-yield.

The problem-oriented approach standard ref

The framework widely accepted as the gold standard for small-animal patient care and assessment. A problem is any abnormality requiring medical or surgical management, or one that interferes with quality of life (Lorenz); in a shelter, problems also include public-health and safety concerns. The clinical reasoning runs through four steps.

StepWhat happens
1 · Database collectionObtain a minimum database on every patient: a complete history plus a complete physical exam, including behavioral observation.
2 · Problem identificationList the historical, physical, behavioral, welfare, and public-health/safety problems revealed by the database, stated at their current level of understanding.
3 · Plan formulationFor each problem, plan the necessary diagnostic testing, initial therapy, and staff/adopter education, within the shelter's mission, resources, and legal holding periods.
4 · Assessment & follow-upMake clinical appraisals, document progression over time, and reassess regularly (critical for long-stay animals) to update known problems and catch new ones.
Minimum database & geriatric add-onsAt intake the minimum database is history, physical exam (including scanning for a microchip), and behavioral observation; prior to adoption add FeLV/FIV testing (cats), heartworm testing (dogs in endemic areas), and a fecal exam if diarrhea is present. For geriatric animals offered for adoption, add PCV/TS and urine specific gravity + dipstick, and, when time and resources allow, FNA cytology of all lumps and bumps.

The monitoring cadence ASVnotes

  • Intake: every animal gets at least a cursory health assessment by trained personnel (confirm age, sex, description, identification, microchip); a comprehensive physical exam is ideally performed within 24 hours of intake. Animals with signs of infectious disease are isolated until determined low-risk.
  • Daily: trained personnel must visually observe every animal at least once every 24 hours, ideally before cleaning, noting food intake and enclosure condition (feces, urine, vomit).
  • Monthly: reweigh and repeat body condition score.
  • Every 6 months: a comprehensive exam for long-term residents, including those in foster.
  • Why repeat screening matters even in "healthy" animals: a prospective 2-year study of 122 apparently healthy senior/geriatric dogs found 20% already had ≥1 undiagnosed disease at the very first screening; among the confirmed-healthy remainder, the most common diseases to emerge over the next 2 years were neoplasia (12%), azotemic chronic kidney disease (8%), neurologic disease (11%), and orthopedic disease (5%) — nearly half of malignant neoplasia was detectable on physical exam alone (rectal, skin, mammary palpation) (Marynissen, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Exam trapThe ASV does not recommend euthanasia of a clinically healthy cat based on a single positive point-of-care retroviral test. Confirmatory testing follows the current AAFP guidelines, and complete history (test date and type) goes with the cat at adoption or transfer.
  • Why FeLV status matters long-term: a single-institution necropsy study of 3,108 cats (1989–2019) found FeLV-positive status was significantly associated with decreased longevity, while FIV status was not — supporting FeLV (not FIV) as the retrovirus with the clearer survival impact when counselling on positive results. The same study found neutered cats of both sexes had significantly longer lifespans than intact cats, and cancer was the leading cause of death overall (35.8%) (Kent, PLOS ONE, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Quarantine versus isolation ASV

  • Isolation separates animals with suspected or confirmed infectious disease until diagnosis or treatment determines them low-risk.
  • Quarantine of healthy animals at intake is not generally recommended; it is appropriate only for animals with a history of direct, high-risk infectious-disease exposure. Unnecessary holds increase length of stay and harm welfare.
Exam trapBlanket intake quarantine of healthy animals is a distractor. It lengthens stays without benefit. Reserve quarantine for documented high-risk exposure.

Pain Management

Pain control is mandatory, multimodal, and pre-emptive where possible. Failure to treat pain is explicitly unacceptable, and euthanasia is a legitimate part of treatment when suffering cannot be relieved.

Recognizing and treating pain ASV

  • Pain must be recognized and treated to alleviate suffering. Failure to provide treatment for pain is unacceptable. Treatment of pain can include providing euthanasia.
  • Assess pain through behavior and knowledge of causes; if a condition is painful in humans, assume it is painful in animals. Several validated pain scales exist.
  • Unrelieved pain is a significant welfare concern and can cause weight loss, muscle breakdown, increased blood pressure, prolonged recovery, and mental and emotional distress.
  • Chronic pain is under-recognised, not just under-treated: a retrospective radiographic review of dogs over 8 years old found osteoarthritis in 39–57% of shoulders, elbows, hips, and stifles depending on joint — meaning most such joints are OA-free, so radiographic OA should not be assumed as a normal ageing finding without confirmation (Roitner, Veterinary Journal, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Evidence for feline chronic pain therapy: robenacoxib (a feline NSAID) showed no significant increase in adverse events over placebo across a 449-cat pooled safety analysis, including cats with chronic kidney disease (King, JVIM, 2021 — see journal abstracts below), and produced significant reductions in owner-assessed disability plus improved objective activity in a 109-cat blinded trial (Adrian, Scientific Reports, 2021 — see journal abstracts below). Frunevetmab (an anti-nerve growth factor monoclonal antibody) improved owner-rated mobility and objectively measured activity over placebo in a 126-cat trial, with a well-tolerated safety profile (Gruen, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Nutraceuticals are not interchangeable with NSAIDs: in a blinded, placebo-controlled trial of dogs with hip OA pain, a green-lipped-mussel-plus-krill-oil extract performed as well as meloxicam and significantly better than placebo, but a different marketed joint supplement (Biota orientalis extract) was statistically indistinguishable from placebo — product choice within the "joint supplement" category matters (Kampa, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Pain physiology, scoring & pharmacologic principles AAHA/AAFP Pain Management Guidelines

  • Pain has two components: sensory (nociception — neural processing of the stimulus) and affective (pain perception — the unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with the damage).
  • Acute pain refers to up to 3 months into a healing process; chronic pain is beyond that.
  • Pain management algorithm: PLATTER — Plan, Anticipate, Treat, Evaluate, Return.
  • Keys to pain assessment: maintenance of normal behavior, loss of normal behavior, and development of new behavior. Pain score is considered the fourth vital sign, after temperature, pulse, and respiration.
  • Opioids are the most effective drug class for managing acute pain.
  • NSAID washout of 5–7 days (or longer for chronic use) is recommended before switching agents; if a washout isn't possible, cover with a gastroprotectant (a proton-pump inhibitor, an H2 antagonist, or sucralfate).
  • Alpha-2 adrenergic agonists act on receptors co-located with opioid receptors, making the combination highly synergistic for sedation and analgesia — a concern in cardiac patients.
  • Thermal modalities: use cold for acute pain, heat for chronic pain.

Pre-emptive, multimodal, and reassessed ASVnotes

  • Pain control must be of an appropriate strength and duration to preempt or relieve pain. When pain can be anticipated, as with surgery, provide control before the painful event.
  • The use of controlled drugs must be supervised by a veterinarian as required by statute.
  • Non-pharmacological methods that supplement drugs: the presence of littermates, a quiet environment, massage, physical therapy, heat, and deep bedding. These increase comfort and reduce anxiety.
  • Reassess animals frequently to judge the efficacy of pain relief. When relief is inadequate, emergency medical care must be provided.
NoteNon-pharmacological methods supplement, not replace, analgesia. They are adjuncts to a veterinary pain-control protocol.

Emergency & Health-Concern Response

Every shelter needs a written plan for timely emergency care, a structured triage method, command of the core emergency drugs, a clear rule for when euthanasia relieves suffering, and the principle that legal status never blocks treatment. Infectious-disease and outbreak response round out the framework.

The emergency medical plan ASVnotes

  • Every shelter must have an established, written emergency medical plan to provide appropriate, timely veterinary care for any animal that is injured, in distress, or showing signs of significant illness.
  • The plan must indicate how staff recognize and report conditions needing emergency care, and whether services are on-site or at an outside clinic. Animals in foster or off-site are covered by the same guidance, with clear after-hours instructions.
  • Three execution paths guarantee prompt care: on-site stabilization and immediate treatment; immediate transfer to a partnered 24-hour emergency or specialty facility; or prompt, humane euthanasia by qualified personnel (as local law permits) when suffering cannot be alleviated.
  • Staff-training mandate: a shelter cannot rely on veterinary presence alone. Non-veterinary care staff must be trained to actively recognize critical conditions so they can trigger the emergency protocol without delay.

  • Correcting a volume deficit: kg × % dehydration = liters to correct. Add this volume to the maintenance volume and replace over 24 hours.
  • Maintenance rate: cats, 80 × kg over 24 hours (≈2–3 mL/kg/hr); dogs, 132 × kg over 24 hours (≈2–6 mL/kg/hr). A maintenance rate is indicated for patients not eating or drinking but with no other ongoing losses.
  • Under anesthesia: keep fluids under 10 mL/kg/hr; a reasonable starting rate is 3 mL/kg/hr in cats and 5 mL/kg/hr in dogs.
  • Three fluid disturbances to consider in sick patients: a change in volume (dehydration, blood loss, heart disease) is corrected as above; a change in content (hyperkalemia, diabetes mellitus, renal disease) calls for an isotonic fluid; a change in distribution (pleural effusion, edema) means fluids should be stopped.
  • Shock volume is essentially one complete blood volume: dogs 80–90 mL/kg IV, cats 50–55 mL/kg IV. For anesthetic hypotension, give a 3–10 mL/kg LRS bolus and repeat once if needed; colloids are more likely than crystalloids to raise blood pressure.
  • Hypovolemia is expected once total protein is under 3.5 g/dL or albumin is under 1.5 g/dL.
  • Potassium supplementation: long-term lactated Ringer's predisposes to hypernatremia and hypokalemia; supplement with 13–20 mmol/L potassium chloride, not exceeding 0.5 mmol/kg/hr of potassium in total.
  • Hetastarch: up to 20 mL/kg/day in dogs, 10–20 mL/kg/day in cats.
  • Subcutaneous fluids are not adequate to treat hyperthermia.

Minimum database and operational controls standard refASV

  • The "big five" minimum database for a critical case: PCV and total protein (hidden hemorrhage, anemia, dehydration); blood glucose (pediatric and neonatal triage); BUN and creatinine (renal perfusion baseline); urine specific gravity or dipstick (separates pre-renal dehydration from primary renal failure before aggressive fluids); and a thorough physical exam.
  • Expired-drug rule: avoid using expired pharmaceuticals. They compromise efficacy and patient safety and undermine the shelter legally by raising questions about the baseline quality of care.
  • Equipment acquisition: shelters may legally accept donated capital equipment (fluid pumps, anesthesia machines, forced-air warmers) and unexpired drugs from private practices or human hospitals; verify calibration and log items in inventory.
  • Written medical-record mandate: each animal has a distinct, unalterable record with its unique shelter ID and signalment, objective exam findings, a working diagnosis or problem list, and prescribed treatments (exact dose, route, frequency, and administrator initials).

Legal status does not bar treatment ASV PS

  • The legal status of an animal must never interfere with necessary preventive care or treatment of painful injuries, significant illness, debilitating conditions, or urgent behavioral needs.
  • When a shelter is legally required to hold such an animal (stray, evidence, boarder), it must enact a plan to provide adequate treatment or seek alternative placement.
  • If suffering cannot be alleviated, the shelter should seek permission to euthanize. Agreements with relevant parties can clarify emergency-care expectations in advance.

Infectious disease and outbreak response ASV

  • Animals with suspected infectious disease must be isolated until a veterinarian determines them low-risk. Allowing animals with severe infectious disease to remain in the general population is unacceptable.
  • An outbreak is more than the usual number of affected animals, or an increase in case severity. Response: risk assessment to identify exposed animals, physical separation of sick, exposed, at-risk, and unexposed groups, and possibly halting animal movement or intake.
  • Monitor at-risk animals for disease at least once a day; limit animal handling and foot traffic; avoid returning recovered or exposed animals to the general population while transmission risk remains.
  • Follow reportable-disease laws. Depopulation is a last resort for extraordinary circumstances, not an initial outbreak response (see the Euthanasia hub).
Exam trapFootbaths often become contaminated and aid transmission rather than control it. Do not treat a footbath as a primary outbreak control.

A New, Easy-to-Learn, Fear-Free Method to Stop Purring During Cardiac Auscultation in Cats
Vliegenthart T, Szatmári V · Animals · 2025 · 10.3390/ani15020236

Abstract. Background: Purring in cats can interfere with cardiac auscultation. If the produced noise is loud enough, purring makes it impossible to perform a meaningful auscultation as it is much louder than heart sounds and murmurs. Our study introduced and tested a new, simple, fear-free, cat-friendly method to stop purring during auscultation. Methods: The technique involves grasping the cat's larynx from ventral with one hand, while simultaneously holding the stethoscope in the other hand to perform the auscultation. Results: The incidence of purring was evaluated in 582 cats, in a veterinary teaching hospital and in a cat-friendly private practice. Fifty-one (8.8%) cats were purring during their physical examination. The tested method had a success rate of 89% in terminating purring. A comparison between investigators (a veterinary student versus an experienced veterinary cardiology specialist) showed no significant difference in the effectiveness of the method (p = 0.57). The incidence of purring was not significantly different between the teaching hospital and the cat-friendly practice (p = 1.00). Sick and older cats purred more often than healthy and younger cats. Conclusions: This new, simple, easy-to-master method is an improvement over previously reported techniques and supports the need for stress-free, cat-friendly handling in veterinary practice.

A prospective study evaluating the impact of chronic otitis on the quality-of-life of dogs and their owners and assessment of the short-term impact of specialist intervention
Dembele V, et al · Veterinary Dermatology · 2025 · 10.1111/vde.13347

Abstract. Background A prospective assessment of the impact of chronic otitis externa (COE) on the wellbeing of affected dogs and their owners has not been thoroughly investigated.

Hypothesis/Objectives To evaluate the quality-of-life (QoL) of dogs with COE and their owners before and after specialist treatment. Secondary objectives included the determination of the influence of patient factors, duration of disease, microbial culture results and 0–3 Otitis Index Scores (OTIS3) on the final treatment outcomes.

Animals Thirty-one client-owned dogs with COE of ≥3 months duration, scheduled for video-otoscopy and ear flush.

Materials and Methods Dogs were evaluated at pre-visit, video-otoscopy (Day 0) and subsequent visits (V1, V2). At each time point, QoL, OTIS3 and pruritus Visual Analog Scale (pVAS) scores were collected. Additional data included breed, age of onset of otitis and cytological and microbial culture results.

Results COE was successfully treated in 79.6% ears with a mean time to resolution of 6 weeks after initial consultation. For dogs, a significant reduction in QoL was recorded between pre-visit and V1: p < 0.001, and V2: p < 0.001. For owners, a significant reduction was seen between pre-visit and D0 (p = 0.008), D0 and V1 (p < 0.001) and pre-visit and V2 (p < 0.001). Isolation of Pseudomonas spp. was strongly associated with treatment failure. Late age of onset and longer episodes were associated with worse outcomes.

Conclusions and Clinical Relevance COE imposes a burden on both the QoL of affected dogs and their owners. Timely referral to a specialist significantly enhances clinical outcomes and improves QoL.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Patterns of Bacteria Associated With Hepatobiliary Disease in Dogs and Cats (2010–2019)
Schlachet E, et al · Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine · 2025 · 10.1111/jvim.70007

Abstract. Background Description of antibiotic susceptibility of isolates from dogs and cats with hepatobiliary disease is limited. Objectives To describe antibiotic susceptibility patterns of bacteria associated with hepatobiliary disease in dogs and cats over a 10 year-period. Animals Three hundred nine dogs and cats. Methods Bacterial species and antibiotic susceptibility data from positive bile and liver tissue cultures were reviewed from both a Veterinary Teaching Hospital and a private laboratory. Prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria was assessed, along with its association with previous antibiotic administration. Results A total of 343 bacterial isolates were included from 310 cultures. Monobacterial cultures were more frequent (91%, 283/310). Gram-negative bacteria were predominant (67%, 227/340), with Escherichia coli (49%, 136/340), Staphylococcus spp. (14%, 47/340), and Enterococcus spp. (10%, 34/340) being the most prevalent isolates. Resistance of gram-negative bacteria were: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (33%, 70/214), aminopenicillins (47%, 96/205), and fluoroquinolones (16%, 67/417); for gram-positive bacteria: amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (12%, 5/41), aminopenicillins (16%, 11/67), and fluoroquinolones (17%, 35/111).
