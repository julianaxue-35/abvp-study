Control Principles & Transmission

Parasite management in shelters protects the population, the public, and the adoptability of each animal. The core levers are a default intake deworming protocol, understanding which parasites can establish in a congregate setting, and rigorous sanitation against environmentally resistant stages.

The three shelter concerns and the intake mandate notesASV

  • Three concerns: protect the individual animal and the population (and limit environmental contamination); protect staff, visitors, and adopters from zoonotic agents; and ensure untreated parasites do not reduce adoptability or burden new owners.
  • ASV intake mandate: all dogs and cats must be treated for roundworms and hookworms at intake, starting at 2 weeks of age, because of the zoonotic risk. Whipworm and tapeworm treatment at intake is often cost-prohibitive and targeted instead.
  • At minimum, every dog should leave the shelter having received a product effective against hookworms and roundworms.
  • Broad-spectrum heartworm and flea spot-ons should not replace a primary dewormer. Choose products by availability, price, and required spectrum, as determined by the attending veterinarian.
  • Intake deworming reduces but doesn't eliminate risk: a shelter study found 54% of dogs parasitised at admission versus 35% still positive before discharge — a real reduction, but far from zero. Of dogs that left the shelter, 31% were still infected and half of those carried a zoonotic parasite (mostly Ancylostoma); some dogs that tested negative at admission tested positive later, showing shelter-acquired infection is a real risk too (J Shelter Med Community Animal Health, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Direct versus indirect transmission notes

Exam trapGiardia and Cryptosporidium are shed in an immediately infective form, so daily sanitation must be impeccable. Ascarids, hookworms, whipworms, and coccidia are shed in a non-infective form that needs environmental development, so infected animals do not need isolation, but post-treatment fecal output is heavily contaminating and demands prompt cleanup.

Environmental persistence notes

  • Ascarid (roundworm) eggs are extremely resistant; on hard surfaces, heat and disinfectants such as bleach or ammonia work mainly by disrupting the outer coat to increase desiccation. Once in soil, they are nearly impossible to destroy.
  • Hookworm eggs are less hardy and are killed fairly rapidly by desiccation on dry surfaces. Whipworm eggs are hardy but their polar plugs may make them slightly more susceptible to bleach than ascarid eggs.
  • Because eggs are not immediately infective, there is no need to isolate or quarantine infected animals; the priority is prompt, thorough sanitation after treatment.

Intestinal Endoparasites

The shelter-relevant intestinal worms (hookworms, ascarids, whipworms, tapeworms) and protozoa each have a characteristic transmission route, prepatent period, and zoonotic profile. Diagnosis centers on zinc-sulfate centrifugation.

Hookworm and roundworm specifics notes

  • Transmammary A. caninum: larvae in the bitch's milk infect nursing puppies, and with a roughly 2-week prepatent period, puppies can shed eggs and develop life-threatening regenerative anemia and melena by about 14 days of age. This milk route is unique to A. caninum among the hookworms.
  • A. braziliense spends more time in the dermis, which is why it causes the majority of human cutaneous larva migrans cases.
  • T. canis can be minimized in puppies by treating the pregnant bitch with ivermectin or doramectin around day 40 to 50 of gestation, which greatly reduces, but does not eliminate, environmental contamination.
  • Recommended regimen: deworm puppies every 2 weeks from week 2 of life and kittens from week 3, then monthly to 6 months; treat pregnant and nursing animals too.
  • Ollulanus tricuspis — the feline "vomit worm", a tiny gastric (stomach) nematode of cats. Transmitted via vomitus, not feces (direct, host-to-host; favoured by crowding such as catteries/colonies), so it is diagnosed by examining vomit (or gastric washings), not by a routine fecal float. Causes chronic vomiting and gastritis; treat with fenbendazole.
  • Multiple anthelmintic drug resistance (MADR) — an emerging exam-relevant threat: Ancylostoma caninum resistant to every anthelmintic class registered in the US (benzimidazoles, pyrantel, macrocyclic lactones) was first reported in 2019 and has since spread well beyond its origin in greyhound kennels/farms — any breed, age, or sex of dog anywhere in the US can now be infected with a resistant or MADR isolate. This is a zoonotic parasite, adding urgency (Geary, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Product choice matters — a head-to-head field trial in a high-hookworm-burden community found oral oxibendazole performed poorly against A. caninum (only 9% cleared at 7–11 days), while moxidectin (56%) and off-label ivermectin (90%) performed much better; for ectoparasites, afoxolaner and imidacloprid/flumethrin collars cleared infestation well, while ivermectin was ineffective — no single product covers both endo- and ectoparasites equally well (Raw, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Environmental contamination is not hypothetical: a UK/Ireland soil survey of 142 public parks and recreational areas found viable, potentially infective Toxocara canis eggs in 86.6% of parks (average 2.1 eggs per 50 g of topsoil), with contamination heavier where dog fouling was more visible — reinforcing that public spaces, not just individual dogs, are the exposure risk for visceral/ocular larva migrans (Airs, Veterinary Record, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • A. caninum vs. U. stenocephala — species ID changes drug choice: in a southern Italy survey of naturally infected dogs, Uncinaria stenocephala was actually the more prevalent hookworm on coproculture (85.1% of positive samples vs. 26.4% for A. caninum, with 11.5% coinfection), even though the two species are nearly identical in larval body size and require molecular confirmation (18S rRNA/ITS-1, 98–99% identity) to separate reliably. This matters clinically because milbemycin oxime is ineffective against U. stenocephala, so accurate species ID in co-endemic areas directly changes the anthelmintic choice (Fagundes-Moreira, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Why prenatal timing works — the hormonal mechanism behind T. canis reactivation: dormant T. canis larvae in a pregnant bitch are reactivated by a hormone cascade — progesterone in early gestation directly stimulates larval reactivation and downregulates the protective granulomatous immune response, then as progesterone falls, prolactin and estrogen maintain that stimulation through to whelping. This explains the biology behind the transplacental/transmammary spread to puppies and why treating the dam in mid-to-late gestation targets larval reactivation directly, rather than just reducing adult worm burden (Muñoz-Guzmán, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • New DNA-based tests are not automatically better than flotation for Toxocara: comparing standard sedimentation-flotation, a sequential-sieving flotation variant, and two automated DNA-extraction/PCR methods head-to-head in cats and dogs, the parasitological (flotation) methods had higher estimated sensitivity than the DNA-based methods in every comparison, and specificity was above 90% for all methods except the sieving variant. Fecal flotation remains the primary diagnostic tool for Toxocara spp.; DNA methods have not yet demonstrated a diagnostic edge in practice (Winterfeld, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Whipworm specifics (Trichuris vulpis) notes

  • Location & biology: adults live in the cecum and colon (large intestine) — the thin anterior "whip" end threads into the mucosa while the thick posterior end carries the eggs. Direct life cycle, no intermediate host: the dog ingests embryonated eggs from contaminated soil.
  • Long prepatent period (~3 months / 11–12 weeks): the longest of the common intestinal nematodes, so a dog can harbour adult worms for months before eggs appear — a single negative fecal float never rules whipworm out. Shedding is also intermittent and low-number.
  • Eggs are exceptionally hardy: barrel / lemon-shaped with bipolar plugs, resistant to desiccation, freezing and most disinfectants, and survive years in soil — the environment becomes a long-term reservoir, so contaminated outdoor runs are very hard to clear and reinfection is common.
  • Clinical signs: often subclinical, but heavier burdens cause large-bowel diarrhea — mucus, fresh (bright-red) blood, tenesmus (straining) — with weight loss; can be life-threatening in young or debilitated dogs.
  • Diagnosis: zinc-sulfate centrifugation fecal flotation (the characteristic bipolar-plugged egg); because of the long prepatent period and intermittent shedding, repeat floats or a fecal antigen test / empiric treatment may be needed in a compatible case.
  • Treatment: fenbendazole (50 mg/kg PO daily ×3 days), febantel, milbemycin oxime or moxidectin. Because of the long prepatent period and environmental reinfection, retreat at 3 weeks and again at 3 months; monthly milbemycin- or moxidectin-based heartworm preventives also control whipworm.
Exam trap — pyrantel misses it, and the pseudo-Addison picture(1) Pyrantel pamoate does NOT cover whipworm (it treats only hookworms and roundworms) — use fenbendazole, milbemycin or moxidectin. (2) Heavy Trichuris infection can cause hyponatremia + hyperkalemia that mimics hypoadrenocorticism ("pseudo-Addison's"); a normal ACTH-stimulation test distinguishes it.

Strongyloides stercoralis std ref

  • Zoonotic threadworm: a small-intestinal nematode of dogs and humans that develops equally well in both species, so it is a genuine direct zoonosis — not a host-adapted look-alike.
  • Transmission: skin-penetrating (percutaneous) larvae that first mature to the infective stage in dirt and debris around pens, plus a transmammary route when the nursing dam acquires an acute infection. The larvae thrive in swampy, damp, heavily soiled cage areas, so wet runs and poor drainage are the key risk.
  • Diagnosis — larvae, not eggs: unusually, the stage passed in feces is the first-stage larva, so infections are easily missed on a routine float and turn up sporadically. Recover larvae with the Baermann funnel technique (preferred) or zinc-sulfate flotation.
  • Treatment: ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg SC (repeat in 1 week) or fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO SID for 5–14 days (fenbendazole favored for ivermectin-sensitive breeds). Dogs respond well but rarely self-cure; isolate during treatment and for 1–2 weeks after, and treat all dogs sharing the same area at the same time.
  • Shelter relevance: once a constant problem in well-run kennels, it is now rare because animals on monthly macrocyclic-lactone preventives are unlikely to become infected.

Cestodes (tapeworms) — the four to separate notes

  • Dipylidium caninum (flea tapeworm): intermediate host = the flea (and chewing louse) — the dog/cat swallows an infected flea while grooming. Motile cucumber-seed / rice-grain proglottids around the anus or in bedding; on flotation the diagnostic stage is egg packets, not single eggs. Control = flea control — deworming without clearing the flea burden just lets it recur. Low zoonotic risk (a child must swallow a flea).
  • Taenia spp. (e.g. T. pisiformis, T. taeniaeformis): intermediate host = prey (rabbits, rodents); the dog/cat is infected by eating the cysticercus in prey tissue, so it is unlikely to cycle inside a shelter (no hunting). Single thick-shelled, radially-striated eggs on float; low zoonotic risk (the human pork/beef tapeworms are separate species). Cleared by praziquantel and by fenbendazole.
  • Echinococcus spp. (E. granulosus, E. multilocularis): the dog/canid is the definitive host of a tiny (2–6 mm) tapeworm, so segments are NOT seen and infection is usually silent. Major zoonosis — humans are accidental intermediate hosts: E. granulosus → hydatid cysts (liver/lung); E. multilocularis → invasive, tumour-like alveolar echinococcosis (emerging/spreading in North America). Eggs are immediately infective and zoonotic — glove up and handle feces with care. Praziquantel (fenbendazole does NOT reliably clear it); reportable in some jurisdictions.
  • Spirometra spp. ("zipper tapeworm"; mostly warm/subtropical regions): a two-intermediate-host cycle — 1st = a water copepod, 2nd = amphibians/reptiles/rodents carrying the sparganum (plerocercoid) larva; the cat/dog is infected by eating an infected frog, snake or rodent. Zoonotic sparganosis in people from drinking copepod-contaminated water or eating raw intermediate hosts (larva migrates in tissue). Less praziquantel-susceptible — needs a higher praziquantel dose (~4× routine) ± repeat.
  • Diagnostic limitation — flotation and PCR both underperform: a necropsy-gold-standard study found routine coproscopy and PCR agreed only moderately with true infection status, and PCR detected zero of the Dipylidium caninum cases confirmed at necropsy despite reasonable sensitivity for Taenia/Hydatigera — current non-invasive tests are particularly poor for Dipylidium, and a commercially available coproantigen test is still needed (Deak, Preventive Veterinary Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). A separate US study confirmed this gap in practice: standard fecal flotation detected essentially no D. caninum infections, while coproantigen testing (± perianal swab PCR) found infection in 5.6–7.3% of dogs overall — and shelter dogs were ~5-fold more likely to be infected than owned pet dogs (Little, Veterinary Parasitology, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trapThe tapeworm drug is praziquantel. Fenbendazole covers hookworms, roundworms, whipworms, Giardia and most Taenia, but NOT Dipylidium, Echinococcus or Spirometra. Match each tapeworm to its intermediate host: Dipylidium → flea; Taenia → prey; Echinococcus → livestock/rodents (+ the big human hazard); Spirometra → copepod, then frog/reptile/rodent.

Intestinal protozoa overview notes

  • Protozoa: Giardia, Cystoisospora (coccidia), Cryptosporidium, and Tritrichomonas cause diarrhea. Work up feline diarrhea with fecal flotation, direct smear and cytology, and a Giardia ELISA as needed; if negative, treat empirically with a broad-spectrum anthelmintic.
  • International prevalence context: in a multicity Kazakhstan survey of 1,301 urban cats, 17.7% carried at least one intestinal parasite (Cystoisospora felis 7.2%, Toxocara cati 6.2%, Giardia 6.4%), with kittens under 6 months roughly twice as likely to test positive as adults (Lider, Veterinary World, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). A retrospective Irish antigen-panel survey of stray dogs (n=789) and cats (n=241) found Giardia the most common parasite in dogs (26%) and ascarids the most common in cats (23.2%), with the youngest age classes in both species carrying the highest burden — the same age-risk pattern seen worldwide (de Waal, Animals, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Spurious parasites are a diagnostic clue, not a false alarm: in a large US laboratory dataset of 48,509 canine fecal exams, the most common "spurious" (non-pathogenic, passed-through) finding was Eimeria oocysts (4.14%) — from coprophagy or eating prey/livestock feces, not true canine coccidiosis (dogs are not the host) — but dogs shedding any spurious parasite had a significantly higher risk of also having a true parasitic infection (P<0.0001). Do not treat an incidental Eimeria or other spurious finding as canine disease, but do treat it as a prompt to look harder for a genuine parasite (Nagamori, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • US baseline prevalence and regional variability: in a companion dataset of 48,510 US canine fecal exams from the same year, the five most common true canine parasites were Giardia intestinalis (5.27%), Ancylostoma spp. (3.14%), Toxocara canis (2.07%), Cystoisospora spp. (1.95%), and Trichuris vulpis (0.88%). Regional and monthly (seasonal) differences each significantly affected the prevalence of every one of these — but only overall parasitism, Ancylostoma, and T. canis showed a significant region-by-season interaction; Giardia, Cystoisospora, and T. vulpis prevalence did not vary by season within a given region. Intake fecal-screening expectations should be weighted by geography and time of year, not treated as uniform nationwide (Nagamori, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Feline lungworm std ref

  • Aelurostrongylus abstrusus — the most prevalent feline lungworm; adults live in the bronchioles and alveolar ducts. Indirect life cycle: eggs are coughed up, swallowed and passed in feces → larvae infect a slug/snail (intermediate host) → ingested by a paratenic host (rodent, bird) → eaten by the cat (vertical transmission also occurs). Diagnosis: Baermann fecal larval migration test (test of choice).
  • Capillaria aerophila (Eucoleus aerophilus) — direct life cycle; low host specificity (also dogs and wildlife, and can infect humans). Diagnosis: standard fecal flotation.
  • Paragonimus (lung fluke) — two intermediate hosts (aquatic snail, then crab/crayfish); the cat eats the second host and flukes migrate to the lungs. Diagnosis: fecal sedimentation.
  • Clinical signs: lower-respiratory disease — cough, dyspnea, fever, weight loss; can mimic feline asthma; worse in kittens; secondary bacterial bronchopneumonia, pleural effusion or pneumothorax may follow. Can be a cause of anesthetic death.
  • Treatment: fenbendazole PO, or topical macrocyclic lactones — Advantage Multi (moxidectin), Profender (emodepside), Revolution (selamectin), milbemycin.
Exam trap — match the lungworm to its fecal testAelurostrongylus → Baermann (larvae); Capillaria → flotation (eggs); Paragonimus → sedimentation (heavy fluke eggs). Choosing the wrong concentration technique misses the diagnosis.

Giardia duodenalis notesstd ref

  • Organism: Giardia duodenalis (= G. intestinalis / G. lamblia) — a flagellated protozoan and one of the most common intestinal parasites in shelter dogs and cats. Two forms: the trophozoite (motile feeding stage — "falling leaf" tumbling motility, two nuclei giving an "owl-eyes" look, a concave ventral adhesive disc, four pairs of flagella) and the cyst (hardy, environmentally resistant, the transmissible stage).
  • Assemblages: genotypes A–H. Dogs carry mainly host-adapted assemblages C and D; cats mainly F. Assemblages A and B are the potentially zoonotic ones, but because pets mostly carry host-adapted assemblages the zoonotic risk is generally low — not zero. Use PCR/assemblage typing when a zoonotic question is raised.
  • Transmission & prepatent period: fecal-oral ingestion of cysts; cyst shedding starts ~5–16 days after infection and is intermittent. Cysts are immediately infective when shed — no environmental maturation needed (unlike ascarid, hookworm, whipworm, and coccidia stages), so spread through a ward and via fomites is rapid and impeccable daily sanitation is essential. Cysts survive well in cool, moist environments and standing water; reinfection from cysts trapped in the coat is common.
  • Clinical signs: small-bowel (sometimes mixed-bowel) diarrhea — soft, pale, malodorous, often fatty or mucoid. Frequently self-limiting in immunocompetent adults; worse in the young and in dense housing. Many infected animals are asymptomatic shedders. Pups and kittens may show weight loss and poor growth.
  • Chronic sequelae after acute giardiasis (a new finding): juvenile dogs with confirmed acute Giardia gastroenteritis had significantly higher rates of chronic GI signs (29% vs 10% in matched controls) and pruritus (33% vs 8%) on long-term follow-up — mirroring post-infectious IBS in humans. A high acute diarrhoea severity score combined with metronidazole treatment further increased the risk of chronic GI signs (Walz, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Zoonotic assemblage detection in stray cat colonies: in an Italian survey, 35.3% of stray colony cats were Giardia-positive, and — notably — all positive cats carried the zoonotic Assemblage A (2 with a mixed A+B infection), positioning stray cat colonies as a potential human giardiasis source (Guadano Procesi, Zoonoses and Public Health, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Diagnosis:
    1. Direct saline wet-mount smear of fresh feces → motile trophozoites with falling-leaf motility.
    2. Fecal flotation (zinc sulfate centrifugation preferred) → cysts. Shedding is intermittent, so examine multiple samples (e.g. 3 over several days) before calling negative.
    3. Giardia antigen (Ag) ELISA / point-of-care SNAP test.
    4. PCR / assemblage typing — for species/zoonotic questions.
    AAFP/ASV minimum diagnostic plan for acute diarrhea = fecal flotation + a Giardia antigen assay. Note Giardia is one of the three GI protozoa of ferrets (with coccidia and Cryptosporidium).
  • Treatment (shelter, evidence-based): Fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h × 5 days plus environmental control is adequate — no other drug is required. Treat clinical disease, not shedding — asymptomatic, formed-stool shedders do not need treatment. Bathe on day 1 and at the end of the course to remove cysts from the coat (Jacobson). If signs persist, repeat the 5-day fenbendazole cycle (re-treat ~d7/14/21) rather than switching drugs. If still loose at ~day 21, Giardia is unlikely the cause — work it up as chronic diarrhea (most canine chronic GI is food-responsive).
  • Confirming cure — flotation, not antigen: a treated animal may keep testing antigen-positive even after cyst shedding has stopped, so verify treatment success with centrifugal (zinc sulfate) flotation only — do not use an antigen test to confirm clearance.
  • Avoid metronidazole as first-line: a 2021 RCT found no significant efficacy difference between fenbendazole and metronidazole (P=0.686), but metronidazole significantly disrupts the gut microbiome, and juvenile Giardia treated with metronidazole is linked to increased chronic intestinal signs. Reach for repeat fenbendazole, not metronidazole.
  • Clinical severity is not explained by dysbiosis: a study comparing diarrheic and non-diarrheic Giardia-infected kennel dogs found no significant difference in fecal microbial diversity or a validated dysbiosis index between the two groups — the microbiome does not appear to determine whether giardiasis causes diarrhea, so other host/parasite factors are driving clinical variability (Kuzi, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Diagnostic method comparison: a four-method comparison found DFA and zinc-sulfate flotation both perform well for detecting Giardia, a rapid diagnostic test is a reasonable alternative/complement, but PCR sensitivity was comparatively low — its value is in assemblage typing, where it identified the zoonotic Assemblage B in 25% of PCR-positive dogs (Gabrielli, Veterinary Parasitology, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). A separate comparison of a beta-giardin qPCR assay against standard multilocus genotyping for assemblage typing found 92.1% agreement for the zoonotic A/B assemblages (Cohen's kappa 0.83) — supporting qPCR as a faster, cheaper option for zoonotic-assemblage screening, though multilocus genotyping is still needed to detect non-zoonotic assemblages and mixed infections (Scorza, Parasites & Vectors, 2026 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Disinfection & environment (the control point): Giardia cysts resist quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs). They are killed by thorough drying/desiccation, steam/heat (>60–70 °C (140–158°F)), accelerated hydrogen peroxide (e.g. Oxivir/Rescue), and bleach on hard surfaces. Eliminate standing water and fix poor run drainage (cysts persist in moisture); remove feces promptly and clean and fully dry runs.
Exam trap — Giardia control is environmental, not just pharmacologicThree high-yield points: (1) Giardia cysts are immediately infective on shedding, unlike ascarid/hookworm/whipworm/coccidia stages that need environmental development — so infected animals demand isolation-level sanitation. (2) QACs do NOT kill Giardia cysts — a shelter relying on a quat as its primary disinfectant will not decontaminate; use drying, steam, or accelerated H₂O₂. (3) Fenbendazole is first-line, not metronidazole — equivalent efficacy but metronidazole causes dysbiosis and chronicity (especially in juveniles); persistent cases get a repeat fenbendazole cycle plus bathing and environmental clean-up, not a drug switch.

Tritrichomonas foetus in cats notesstd ref

  • Organism: Tritrichomonas foetus — a protozoan flagellate; the same species causes bovine trichomoniasis but in cats it colonizes the large bowel.
  • Epidemiology: young cats (typically under 2 years) in congregate settings — shelters and catteries. Fecal-oral transmission via contaminated litter boxes. A 5-year Polish surveillance study found 10.6% overall PCR positivity among diarrheic cats, rising to 14.9% in cats under 1 year old — reinforcing age as the key risk factor (Janczak, Pathogens, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Clinical signs: chronic, intermittent large bowel diarrhea — fresh blood, mucus, semi-formed to liquid feces, tenesmus. Normal appetite, body weight, and activity. No systemic signs. Cats often look clinically well between episodes. It self-resolves over months to years in many cats.
  • Diagnosis:
    1. Direct fecal smear of very fresh stool (within minutes) — look for motile, pear-shaped organisms with a characteristic forward rolling movement and visible axostyle (stiff rod).
    2. InPouch TF culture — incubated 48–72 hours; gold standard for sensitivity.
    3. PCR — most specific; can detect T. foetus when other methods are negative.
  • Best sample: a colonic saline flush via red-rubber catheter is highest yield, then a fecal loop, then fresh diarrhoeic feces. Use only diarrhoeic stool; do not refrigerate or ship (the trophozoite survives poorly). About 22–54% of infected cats are co-infected with Giardia, so a positive Giardia result does not rule out Tritrichomonas.
  • Treatment: Ronidazole 30 mg/kg PO once daily × 14 days. Ronidazole is nitroimidazole-based but NOT interchangeable with metronidazole for Tritrichomonas. Metronidazole has POOR efficacy against T. foetus and will not resolve the infection. Caution: ronidazole can cause reversible neurotoxicity (lethargy, ataxia, tremors) — use the lowest effective dose and stop if neurologic signs appear.
  • Shelter management: segregate affected cats from kittens; rigorous litter box hygiene (prompt removal of feces); avoid shared litter boxes wherever possible; fomite transmission via hands, equipment, and litter scoops is possible. Environmental survival is poor (~2–3 days), so host-to-host and fomite control matters more than heavy environmental decontamination.
Exam trap — Tritrichomonas vs. Giardia on smearBoth are flagellated protozoa seen on direct fecal smear. Distinguish them by movement and morphology:
T. foetus: forward rolling movement; single axostyle (stiff posterior rod); one nucleus; three anterior flagella and one undulating membrane.
Giardia: "falling leaf" tumbling motility; two nuclei (owl-eyes); concave ventral disc.
Critical treatment trap: metronidazole is effective against Giardia but NOT against T. foetus. Using metronidazole for suspected Tritrichomonas is a high-frequency error.

Cryptosporidium spp. correctednotesstd ref

  • Organisms: dogs primarily harbor the host-adapted C. canis; cats harbor C. felis. C. parvum is chiefly a ruminant/human pathogen — dogs and cats are not important reservoirs of it (nor of C. hominis). All are coccidian apicomplexans, closely related to Toxoplasma and Cystoisospora.
  • Transmission & prepatent period: fecal-oral; prepatent period ~2–14 days. Oocysts are immediately infective when shed — there is NO required period of sporulation outside the host, so environmental contamination occurs faster than with most other coccidia. Thin-walled oocysts also cause internal autoinfection, so infection can persist and amplify without re-exposure.
  • Zoonotic risk (limited from companion animals): the major human pathogens are C. hominis and C. parvum (the latter largely ruminant-acquired); the host-adapted C. canis/C. felis that dogs and cats carry only occasionally infect people, so the shelter ID textbook rates dog/cat→human risk as limited. Still treat feces as potentially zoonotic and use handwashing — the single most important protective measure — especially around immunocompromised people (HIV/AIDS, organ transplant).
  • Clinical signs: small intestinal diarrhea, watery to mucoid, often self-limiting in immunocompetent adult animals. Young, neonatal, or immunocompromised animals can develop severe, life-threatening diarrhea.
  • Diagnosis:
    1. Routine fecal flotation: poor sensitivity — oocysts are very small (4–5 μm) and can be missed.
    2. Modified Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) stain: oocysts stain pink to red against a blue-green background; the reference staining technique for Cryptosporidium.
    3. ELISA or direct IFA: better sensitivity for C. parvum; useful when ZN stain is negative and suspicion remains high.
    4. PCR: most specific; also specifies the Cryptosporidium species.
  • Treatment: no consistently effective treatment exists for immunocompetent dogs and cats. Supportive care (fluids, nutritional support, bland diet). Azithromycin has been used empirically. Paromomycin was historically used but is nephrotoxic in cats and no longer recommended. Focus on strict hygiene and reducing exposure rather than drug treatment in most shelter cases.
  • Disinfection — critical exam trap: Cryptosporidium oocysts are highly resistant to chlorine disinfectants including bleach at standard dilutions. Do not rely on bleach alone.
    • Effective: 6% hydrogen peroxide (30 min), steam cleaning (over 60°C (140°F) is required), desiccation (dry surfaces, direct sunlight)
    • Ineffective or unreliable: standard bleach dilutions (1:32), quaternary ammonium compounds, most commonly used shelter disinfectants
  • Shelter protocol: isolate affected animals; restrict to dedicated staff where possible; thorough handwashing after any contact; use steam cleaning or 6% H₂O₂ for environment; counsel adopters on zoonotic risk.
Exam trap — bleach fails against CryptosporidiumUnlike CPV or Giardia, Cryptosporidium oocysts have an extremely tough outer wall that resists standard chlorine disinfection. A shelter using bleach as its primary disinfectant during a Cryptosporidium outbreak will NOT adequately decontaminate the environment. Steam cleaning or high-concentration hydrogen peroxide must be used.

Cystoisospora (coccidia) std ref

  • Host-specific coccidia: the Cystoisospora of cats and the Cystoisospora of dogs are distinct, host-specific species — cat coccidia do not infect dogs or humans, and vice versa. Clinical coccidiosis is mainly a problem of naive puppies and kittens in a heavily contaminated environment.
  • Species & naming: the dog coccidia are Cystoisospora canis and C. ohioensis; the cat coccidia are C. felis and C. rivolta (all formerly called Isospora). Small, oval, thin-walled oocysts on flotation — larger than Cryptosporidium and lacking the internal structure of a Toxoplasma/Neospora-type oocyst.
  • Prepatent period: in dogs about 3 weeks (kittens similar) — so a puppy/kitten can already be sick with coccidiosis while still not shedding, and a single fecal float can be negative early in disease.
  • Life cycle & sporulation (key exam point): direct and fecal–oral, but oocysts are passed UNSPORULATED and must sporulate in the environment before they are infective — as fast as ~6 hours in warm, moist, oxygenated conditions (often longer). Because fresh feces are not yet infective, removing feces at least once daily, before sporulation, breaks transmission — unlike Cryptosporidium and Giardia, which are shed already infective. An indirect route also exists: paratenic/transport hosts (e.g. rodents) carry tissue cystozoites that reactivate in stressed or immunocompromised animals. Sporulated oocysts resist most routine disinfectants — rely on daily mechanical removal, steam or ammonia.
  • Clinical & who gets sick: disease is mainly a problem of young, recently weaned, crowded or stressed puppies and kittens — watery to mucoid, sometimes bloody, small-bowel diarrhea with dehydration and weight loss in heavy infections. Immunocompetent adults are usually subclinical carriers; the stress of intake, weaning and overcrowding tips carriers into clinical disease.
  • Treatment of choice — ponazuril: ponazuril (toltrazuril sulfone) 20–50 mg/kg PO SID for 1–3 days is coccidiocidal and the treatment of choice; it can suppress oocyst shedding below detectable limits.
  • Only labeled option — sulfadimethoxine: sulfadimethoxine 50 mg/kg PO day 1 then 25 mg/kg SID for 1–3 weeks is the only labeled product, but it is merely coccidiostatic (it suppresses, rather than kills, the parasite). Other options: trimethoprim-sulfa 30–60 mg/kg SID ×6 days, or diclazuril 5 mg/kg once (cats).
  • Species-specific pathogenicity in diarrhea cases: among young dogs and cats presenting with diarrhea, overall coccidia positivity was far more common in cats (33.9%, mostly C. felis at 28%) than dogs (6.8% C. canis, 3.4% C. ohioensis-like), and clinical scoring pointed to C. canis as the more pathogenic species in dogs and C. rivolta as the more pathogenic species in cats — dogs with coccidiosis uniformly had very soft feces, and in cats, positivity for hookworm-family eggs (Ancylostomatidae) was significantly correlated with vomiting, dehydration, and watery/hemorrhagic diarrhea (Morelli, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap — signs before sheddingFor coccidia, the cellular (enterocyte) destruction that causes clinical signs occurs BEFORE oocyst production. An animal can therefore be sick with coccidiosis while its fecal float is still negative, and a drug aimed at the early stages may do little once signs are already present. A single negative float does not rule out coccidiosis.

Toxoplasma gondii ⚠ zoonotic · pregnancystd ref

  • ⚠ ZOONOSIS — the big one: a significant zoonosis, greatest risk to pregnant women (transplacental → congenital toxoplasmosis: hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, CNS calcification, abortion/stillbirth) and the immunocompromised. The main HUMAN route is eating undercooked meat (tissue cysts), NOT cat contact — so daily litter removal (before oocysts sporulate), gloves/handwashing, and not feeding/eating raw meat are the key preventives; pregnant women should avoid changing litter. A Tehran study of stray/household cats and their owners supports this counseling point directly: despite over half of owners testing seropositive, cat seropositivity was comparatively low (15–18%) and not strongly correlated with owner infection — undercooked meat and environmental exposure explain more human cases than direct cat contact. The same study found stray cats had a striking 47% Toxocara cati prevalence, making them a bigger environmental-contamination concern than a direct toxoplasmosis risk (Karimi, Frontiers, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Cats are the ONLY definitive host: domestic and wild felids are the sole source of oocysts, shed in feces mainly during acute infection, for only about 2 weeks. Oocyst shedding begins ~3–10 days after a cat eats tissue cysts (the shortest, most efficient route) and takes longer after ingesting oocysts. No other species sheds oocysts.
  • Fresh cat feces are low-risk: shed oocysts need more than 24 hours to sporulate to the infective stage, so daily litter removal eliminates them before they become infective, and contact with cats (meticulous groomers) is not a serious risk factor for human transmission.
  • Seropositivity means LESS shedding: a serologically positive cat has already been infected and is at decreased risk of shedding oocysts — counterintuitively, the antibody-positive cat is the safer one in this setting.
  • Two routes of infection for any host: (1) ingesting sporulated oocysts from the environment, or (2) eating raw or undercooked meat containing tissue cysts.
  • Fecal diagnosis is unreliable: Toxoplasma-like oocysts are found in 1% or fewer cat fecal samples and cannot be distinguished by morphology from Hammondia or Besnoitia.
  • Environmental shedding is not random — it tracks people and climate: a systematic review of free-ranging domestic and wild felids found oocyst-shedding prevalence rose with human population density at the sampling site, and with greater diurnal temperature range in domestic cats specifically — meaning managing free-roaming cat populations in and around human settlements can meaningfully lower environmental T. gondii contamination (Zhu, PLOS ONE, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Large-scale European risk-factor data: across 45,523 cats tested, seropositivity was significantly higher with age >5 years, in European domestic shorthairs, and in FeLV- or FIV-positive cats; PCR-positive (actively shedding) cats were also more likely in these groups — reinforcing that older, retrovirus-positive cats carry the highest shedding risk and warrant closer counseling (Schafer, JFMS, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). A separate study of 150 cats specifically living with women of childbearing age found neurological or ocular signs were strongly associated with acute (IgM-positive) infection — useful for triaging which symptomatic household cats need urgent testing (Traversa, Veterinary Parasitology, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Diagnosis of clinical disease (fecal exam is NOT the way): oocysts are shed for only ~2 weeks, in <1% of cats, and can't be told from Hammondia/Besnoitia — so diagnose clinical toxoplasmosis by:
    • Serology — the mainstay: IgM rises early (~1–2 wk) and signals active/recent infection — it correlates best with clinical disease. IgG rises later and persists for years, so a single positive IgG only proves prior exposure; you need a 4-fold rise in paired IgG titres (2–3 wk apart) to confirm active infection. Many healthy cats are IgG-positive, so serology must be read with the clinical picture.
    • PCR — detects T. gondii DNA in blood, aqueous humour, CSF, or affected tissue/BAL fluid; confirms the organism.
    • Cytology / histopathology — demonstrate tachyzoites in tissue aspirates, effusions, aqueous humour, CSF or bronchoalveolar lavage.
    • Ocular / CNS forms — compare antibody in aqueous humour or CSF with serum (local production) to support intra-ocular / intra-thecal toxoplasmosis.
    • Supportive — compatible signs + a positive response to clindamycin (a therapeutic trial).
  • Disseminated disease (immunosuppressed animals): fever, anorexia, and ocular, pulmonary, or CNS signs. Treat with clindamycin 10–12 mg/kg BID for 2–4 weeks, or pyrimethamine plus a sulfonamide; ponazuril can reduce oocyst shedding in identified shedders.
  • Exam-relevant differential trap: a 7-week-old shelter kitten with progressive neurological decline was strongly suspected to have non-effusive FIP, with toxoplasmosis considered but lower on the list — necropsy and histopathology instead confirmed disseminated toxoplasmal meningoencephalitis with no evidence of feline coronavirus. Overwhelming toxoplasmosis can mimic the neurological presentation of FIP closely enough to be missed antemortem (Abdu, Veterinary Parasitology: Regional Studies and Reports, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • An in-house test can substitute for the commercial assay: an in-house modified agglutination test (MAT) developed to detect IgG against T. gondii showed excellent agreement with a commercial MAT, providing a reliable and economical serological screening tool. In Australian feral and stray cats tested this way, overall seroprevalence was 40.4%; body weight, geographic location, season, and feral-versus-stray status were all associated with seropositivity, but sex was not (see journal abstracts below).

Neospora caninum std ref

  • Dog is the definitive host: the life cycle mirrors Toxoplasma but cycles dog ↔ cattle rather than cat-and-prey; dogs are usually infected by eating raw beef or venison and pass oocysts in feces on initial infection.
  • NOT zoonotic: despite numerous investigations, there is no evidence Neospora infects people — a key contrast with Toxoplasma.
  • Classic puppy presentation: congenitally infected pups develop ascending flaccid hind-limb (posterior) paralysis at about 3 weeks of age; adults show neurologic signs, nodular dermatitis, myocarditis, and myositis.
  • Adult-onset disease is more variable than the classic pup picture: a case series of 21 adult dogs found presentations ranging from generalized myopathy to multifocal intracranial disease, myelopathy, and polyneuropathy, with markedly elevated CK in most. Treatment response was complete in only 8/21, and relapse occurred in 4 dogs even after apparently complete initial recovery — adult neosporosis needs longer-term monitoring than the diagnosis might suggest (Kennedy, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Treatment: clindamycin or trimethoprim-sulfa, each combined with pyrimethamine, for about 4 weeks. Treat all littermates of an affected pup, even the clinically normal ones.
  • Diagnosis: serology (IFAT / ELISA, ideally paired or rising titres), PCR of CSF or affected tissue, and histopathology; supportive chemistry shows markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) and AST from the myositis/myocarditis.

Heartworm Disease

Dirofilaria immitis is largely clinically invisible, slow, and expensive to treat, which makes shelter protocols a balance of testing, prevention, and selective treatment. The ASV endorses the AHS guidelines, adapted for shelter resources.

Life cycle notesAHS

  • Exact larval-development timeline (the stage timings that get tested):
    • In the MOSQUITO (intermediate host): microfilariae (L1) → L2 → infective L3 over ~10–14 days — temperature-dependent (development stalls below ~14 °C / 57 °F, faster in warm weather).
    • In the DOG (definitive host): the mosquito deposits L3 at the bite; L3 → L4 in ~4.5–6.5 days in subcutaneous / muscle tissue (the "tissue larval" stage); L4 → L5 (immature adult) over the next ~2 months, then the developing adults migrate to the pulmonary arteries and mature over ~4–5 months.
    • Prepatent period (infection → circulating microfilariae): ~6–7 months in dogs — which is why antigen / microfilaria testing is unreliable in dogs <6–7 months old or <6 months after exposure.
  • Longevity & reservoir: adult worms live ~5–7 years in dogs (a dog can be its own reservoir). In cats, worms live ~2–4 years, circulating microfilariae are rare/transient, and cats often self-cure over ~18–48 months.
  • Geographic scope & seasonality: heartworm has been reported in 49 of the 50 US states plus US territories — only Alaska has not yet documented autochthonous transmission, though conditions there are considered permissive. Transmission decreases in winter in many regions but never truly reaches zero, and urban heat islands let larvae keep maturing through colder months, lengthening the local transmission season. Even within known endemic areas, only about 2–19% of local mosquitoes are actually infected.
  • Unowned/shelter dogs as a community reservoir: in northern Italy's historically hyperendemic Po River Valley, screening of 510 shelter-entering stray dogs still found 33.9% microfilaria-positive (D. immitis, D. repens, or mixed) despite widespread preventive availability — unowned dog populations sustain local transmission and elevate exposure risk for every owned dog nearby (Genchi, Parasites & Vectors, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Prevalence is not uniform, even within one guideline area: a systematic review/meta-analysis in Australia — where heartworm preventive guidelines are not climate-specific — found pooled prevalence in humid summer zones (18.7%) nearly 9-fold that of cool winter zones (2.1%), showing real geographic risk variation that a single blanket recommendation obscures (Atkinson, Preventive Veterinary Medicine, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). Climate-driven range expansion also affects historically non-endemic countries: the UK's first autochthonous-adjacent case (a Scotland-resident dog with D. repens) illustrates how climate change and invasive mosquito species could establish filarial infection outside its traditional endemic range (Panarese, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • D. immitis is not the only filarioid worth knowing: a review of cutaneous filarioid nematodes in US dogs (e.g. Onchocerca lupi, which is zoonotic) argues these skin-dwelling filarioids may be more common than previously recognised, simply because clinical and diagnostic attention has focused almost exclusively on D. immitis (Gruntmeir, Frontiers, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • A second thrombocytopenia mechanism, beyond mechanical worm damage: in 169 canine samples, dogs positive for D. immitis had significantly higher anti-phosphatidylserine (anti-PS) IgM and IgG autoantibodies than negative dogs, and anti-PS levels correlated with low platelet counts — suggesting autoantibody-mediated platelet destruction contributes to heartworm-associated thrombocytopenia alongside the mechanical vascular damage from the worms themselves (Montenegro, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Diagnosis correctedAHS

  • Antigen tests detect circulating antigen of adult female worms. They are the primary screening test. Microfilariae are detected separately by concentration tests (Modified Knott).
  • Run both antigen and microfilaria tests, because a small fraction of infected dogs are antigen-negative and microfilaria-positive. Do not test before about 7 months of age, since the prepatent period is roughly 6 months.
  • Dogs on preventives may have a false-negative antigen test from antigen-antibody complex formation; heat treatment of serum can unmask antigen by breaking those complexes.
  • A "no antigen detected" result does not confirm freedom from infection; all positive antigen tests should be confirmed before any adulticidal therapy.
  • No test can identify a male-only worm burden: there is currently no diagnostic that specifically detects the presence of male worms alone, which is part of why antigen tests can be falsely negative in early or single-sex infections.
  • Roughly 20% of antigen-positive dogs are not microfilaremic on concurrent testing — and this proportion is even higher in dogs already receiving heartworm prevention — so a negative microfilaria test never rules out infection on its own.
  • Beware a look-alike microfilaria — false positive: the non-pathogenic subcutaneous worm Acanthocheilonema (Dipetalonema) reconditum also circulates microfilariae; if these are misidentified as D. immitis, a dog is wrongly called heartworm-positive. A concentration test (Modified Knott) with careful morphologic assessment distinguishes the two.

Radiographic staging AHS

  • Most objective severity measure: thoracic radiography is the most objective method of assessing the severity of heartworm-associated cardiopulmonary disease and is the basis for staging the dog.
  • Nearly pathognomonic vascular signs: enlarged, tortuous, and truncated peripheral (intralobar and interlobar) branches of the pulmonary arteries, with variable pulmonary parenchymal disease.
  • Severe disease: marked right-heart enlargement produces the classic "reverse D" cardiac silhouette.
  • Echocardiography confirms infection when adult worms are seen, but is an inefficient screening test, especially with light worm burdens.

Treatment: the AHS protocol AHSnotes

  • Melarsomine is the only FDA-approved adulticide, effective against worms aged 120 days and older, given by deep IM injection into the epaxial lumbar muscles (L3 to L5).
  • Injection schedule & supportive drugs: melarsomine 2.5 mg/kg deep IM as the AHS 3-dose (split) protocol — one injection, then two more 30 days later given 24 hours apart (about Days 60, 90 and 91 when the full protocol is started at diagnosis). Give an anti-inflammatory prednisone taper after each injection: 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h (week 1), 0.5 mg/kg q24h (week 2), then 0.5 mg/kg every other day (weeks 3–4). Strict cage rest / leash-only throughout and for at least 6–8 weeks after the last injection — the single most important factor in preventing fatal pulmonary thromboembolism.
  • Post-injection pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) is an expected consequence of worm die-off, not a rare complication: watch for fever, cough, hemoptysis, and exacerbation of right-sided heart failure, usually appearing 7–10 days after melarsomine but possible up to 4 weeks later. Manage with strict activity restriction and glucocorticoids; aspirin for an antithrombotic effect is not recommended.
  • Post-treatment retesting — two separate tests: test for microfilariae about 4 weeks after the last injection (about Day 120); if positive, give a microfilaricide and recheck. Then confirm cure with an antigen test 9 months after the last melarsomine injection — AHS moved this from 6 to 9 months in 2018 because residual antigen from dying worms can give an earlier false positive. Do not call a dog heartworm-free on an earlier antigen test.
  • Wolbachia and doxycycline: Wolbachia is a gram-negative intracellular endosymbiont essential to heartworm development and reproduction, transmitted vertically from adult female to microfilariae. Doxycycline at 10 mg/kg twice daily for 28 days suppresses Wolbachia, reduces post-treatment pulmonary pathology, lowers microfilarial counts, and renders microfilariae non-infective to mosquitoes (limiting spread). Wolbachia's major surface protein (WSP) induces a specific IgG response in the infected host and is speculated to contribute to the pulmonary and renal inflammation seen with heartworm disease. The effect of doxycycline is durable — Wolbachia numbers stay suppressed for roughly 12 months after a course of treatment.
  • Slow-kill (macrocyclic-lactone-only) adulticide is not recommended except in specific situations (a prior anaphylactic reaction to melarsomine, a grave non-heartworm prognosis); it is contraindicated in performance dogs or dogs that cannot be exercise-restricted, and where ML-resistance is suspected. ML resistance is not confined to the classic Mississippi Delta focus: genetic testing of D. immitis isolates from naturally infected dogs in southeast Missouri (the northern edge of the Delta region) found 94.8% carried a resistant genotype — evidence that resistance is already established well beyond where it was first characterised, reinforcing melarsomine (not an ML alone) as first-line adulticide therapy (Fisher, Veterinary Parasitology, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Caval syndrome: a heavy worm burden (more than ~40 adult worms raises the risk) partially obstructs the tricuspid valve and interferes with valve closure, producing severe passive hepatic congestion, a coarse systolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation, jugular pulsations, and sudden-onset lethargy, dyspnea, pale mucous membranes, weakness, and hemoglobinemia/hemoglobinuria from mechanical red-cell shearing. Fatal within about 2 days without surgery: worms are retrieved via the right external jugular vein with alligator forceps or an intravascular retrieval snare; the murmur should soften immediately and hemoglobinuria typically resolves within 12–24 hours. IV fluids are essential for the accompanying hypovolemia.
  • Slow-kill doesn't fully normalise cardiac/coagulation biomarkers, even when clinically "stable": in dogs on an ivermectin + doxycycline slow-kill protocol, NT-proBNP became elevated across all severity groups by day 180 (significantly higher in moderate and severe dogs than asymptomatic dogs), and D-dimer — despite decreasing somewhat over the 180 days — remained above the reference range throughout, again significantly higher in moderate/severe dogs. Slow-kill did not meaningfully reduce these biomarkers, supporting the idea that concurrent cardiac/antithrombotic therapy may be warranted given the long retention of adult worms on this protocol (Rajković, The Veterinary Journal, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
Shelter reality — "moxi-doxy" as a cost-constrained alternativeAHS still considers the 3-dose melarsomine protocol the safest and most effective adulticide. But melarsomine is costly and sometimes unavailable, so shelter medicine increasingly uses a non-arsenical "moxi-doxy" protocol when melarsomine is not feasible: topical moxidectin monthly + doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q12–24h × 28 days, repeated until antigen-negative (NAD). It is slower and may allow some progression of pulmonary pathology, but serious respiratory complications were uncommon across studies and it is far better than no treatment. Evidence: Savadelis et al., Frontiers in Veterinary Science 2021 (UF Shelter Medicine — "An Accessible Alternative to Melarsomine: Moxi-Doxy"). Exercise restriction still applies.

Post-adulticide follow-up & when to retreat AHS

  • Two rechecks, two different questions: the microfilaria (Knott's) test asks “is the microfilaricide working / is the dog still a reservoir?”; the antigen test asks “did the adulticide work — are the adults gone?” They are decoupled — a dog can be microfilaria-positive but antigen-negative (adults dead, microfilariae clearing slowly) or antigen-positive but microfilaria-negative (adults alive, the preventive suppressing microfilariae).
  • Microfilaricide kinetics are gradual, not instant: a macrocyclic lactone (moxidectin, including the sustained-release ProHeart depot) drops microfilaria counts sharply by about day 30 but may not fully clear them until ~5 months (about day 150). Persistent low microfilaremia at a few months is usually slow clearance, not treatment failure — judge by the trend (a falling count reassures; a stable or rising count suggests adults are still producing microfilariae). Do not stack a second macrocyclic lactone onto an active ProHeart depot: same drug class, no added benefit, higher reaction risk.
  • The retreatment trigger is the 9-month ANTIGEN test, not microfilaria status. Antigen can linger up to ~3 months after the last adult worm dies, so earlier antigen testing is unreliable; by 9 months any antigen is assumed to be surviving adults or a new infection.
  • If antigen-positive at 9 months (confirm on a recheck; heat-treating the sample can unmask antigen): retreat with 30 days of doxycycline followed by 2 doses of melarsomine; some clinicians allow an extra 3 months before retreating if the result is borderline. Antigen-negative at 9 months = cured, even if microfilariae were slow to clear.
  • "Cured" is not risk-free long-term: a large retrospective cohort found dogs successfully treated for heartworm infection had significantly elevated long-term risk of right heart failure (3.6×), left heart failure (1.8×), and cardiomyopathy (2.8×) compared with dogs that never tested positive — successful adulticide therapy clears the infection but does not fully reverse the cardiopulmonary damage already done, which matters for adoption counselling and long-term monitoring recommendations (Mwacalimba, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Still microfilaria-positive? Keep the microfilaricide going and re-check the count, and confirm the microfilariae are truly D. immitis — rule out the non-pathogenic look-alike Acanthocheilonema (Dipetalonema) reconditum, a false “still positive.”
  • High microfilarial counts raise the risk of an anaphylactoid reaction once the microfilaricide starts killing them off — glucocorticoids and antihistamines may be needed to manage this reaction in heavily microfilaremic dogs.

The 30–120-day susceptibility gap AHS

  • The two drug classes hit different worm ages: macrocyclic-lactone preventives reliably kill only worms present 30 days or less (some activity to ~45 days, but unreliable past 30 days); melarsomine is an adulticide effective only against worms aged 120 days and older.
  • The classic "gap": worms between 30 and 120 days old were traditionally thought too old for the preventive but too young for the adulticide — hence the old advice to give 60 days of preventive plus doxycycline before melarsomine, letting the juveniles mature.
  • Modern view — no gap if you start at diagnosis: when the full 3-dose melarsomine protocol + doxycycline + ongoing macrocyclic lactone is begun at the time of diagnosis, the combination clears all stages (including those 120 days and younger). Delaying treatment does not improve efficacy and allows the disease to progress.

Feline heartworm diagnostics AHS

  • No single feline test is sensitive enough — cats need a multi-step work-up combining antibody, antigen, and imaging.
  • Antibody tests detect infection by both male and female worms as early as ~2 months post-infection (a larva of either sex stimulates a response), but only indicate that infection occurred at some point — not that it persists.
  • Antigen tests detect only adult female worms, with detectable antigenemia developing ~5.5 to 8 months post-infection. Because 30–50% of infected cats have no female worm (single-sex male or immature infections), that many infected cats are antigen-negative.
  • Discordance between antibody and antigen results is common because each test is sensitive to a different larval stage (antibody: any recent exposure by either sex; antigen: a mature adult female present now) — this is exactly why cats need both, not either alone, since both juvenile and adult worms can cause clinical signs in cats.
  • Heat-treating the serum sample can also increase antigen-positive detection in cats, the same principle used to unmask immune-complexed antigen in dogs.
  • Screening policy: routine heartworm screening is NOT indicated in asymptomatic shelter cats — reserve testing for symptomatic cats. In a symptomatic cat, use the full panel together — thoracic radiographs, serum antibody, serum antigen, and echocardiography — and counsel adopters about feline heartworm risk.
  • CT can catch early disease that serology misses: in cats naturally infected with immature D. immitis (seropositive with respiratory signs), CT showed significantly higher bronchus-to-artery and bronchus-to-vein ratios than in seronegative controls — early bronchial remodeling occurs even before the vasculature is heavily affected, suggesting CT ratios could supplement serology/imaging in a diagnostically difficult early-infection cat (García-Rodríguez, Animals, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Feline seroprevalence in a high-canine-prevalence area: in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, feline heartworm antigen seroprevalence was 1.2% — low in absolute terms, but heartworm-positive cats were significantly more likely to show vomiting and abnormal lung sounds, so heartworm disease should stay on the differential for these signs even where testing is uncommon (Alberigi, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Ferret heartworm diagnostics std ref

  • Microfilaremia is transient in ferrets, which makes detection challenging — but a positive result is diagnostic when found.
  • Combine an antigen test with thoracic radiographs, echocardiography and angiography for the highest detection rate; a false-negative antigen test is presumed due to low worm burden (often just a single worm).
  • Antibody testing lacks proven efficacy in ferrets — unlike in cats, it is not a reliable second test here.
  • Radiographic signs: pleural effusion and dilated pulmonary vasculature.
  • Routine heartworm screening is NOT indicated in asymptomatic shelter ferrets — the same policy as for cats.

Shelter strategy and prevention ASV PSAHS

  • The ASV endorses the AHS guidelines and urges year-round prevention for dogs, cats, and ferrets, since some transmission risk exists everywhere. Screen at-risk dogs; provide prevention even when screening is not done; treat infected dogs promptly to reduce pathology and limit transmission.
  • Heartworm management should not divert resources from the care of other animals. A shelter without treatment capacity may transfer infected dogs to a partner with capacity, or release to an informed adopter with clear follow-up expectations.
  • In high-prevalence areas it is more cost-effective to screen dogs by microfilaria first; in low-prevalence areas, antigen-test first. A treated dog is not considered heartworm-free until two antigen tests 6 months apart are negative.
  • Start age & retest timing: puppies should start heartworm prevention as early as 8 weeks of age. A dog started on prevention after 8 weeks of age should be tested 6 months after starting prevention and then annually thereafter.
  • Macrocyclic lactones (ivermectin, selamectin, milbemycin, moxidectin) are reliable preventives only against worms present 30 days or less; DEET is unsafe in dogs, cats, and ferrets.
  • MDR1/ABCB1-mutant dogs tolerate standard heartworm preventive dosing — the toxicity risk associated with ivermectin in these breeds comes from overdosing (as with large-animal/livestock ivermectin formulations), not from the standard labelled heartworm-prevention dose.
  • Permethrin spot-ons block mosquito transmission (e.g. a permethrin + dinotefuran + pyriproxyfen topical on relocated dogs reduces the chance an infected dog seeds local mosquitoes) — but permethrin is NEVER used on cats, in which it causes severe, potentially fatal toxicity. Topical mosquito repellents have been shown to be about 95% effective at preventing heartworm infection, and ectoparasiticides kill mosquitoes within 3 days of a blood meal — before the mosquito could transmit heartworm onward to another dog. In challenge trials, dogs given a macrocyclic lactone plus a topical repellent/ectoparasiticide were 100% protected from infection.
  • Elimination is achievable with sustained effort: on Linosa Island, Italy — historically hyperendemic — a combined program of doxycycline + monthly topical moxidectin for antigen/microfilaria-positive dogs, plus chemoprophylaxis for negative dogs, reduced the microfilaraemic dog count by 76.5% within month 1 and drove new infections to zero, demonstrating a real-world One Health elimination model rather than just theoretical control (Brianti, Emerging Infectious Diseases, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Compliance is the weak link even in well-resourced populations: in the Golden Retriever Lifetime Study (a large, closely monitored cohort), heartworm preventive use prevalence was only 39.5% — receiving other vaccinations, living in the southern US, and being desexed were all associated with higher preventive use, meaning education/outreach can be targeted at owners lacking those factors (Wisnieski, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Shelter dogs carry disproportionate risk even outside "hyperendemic" regions: a multicenter survey of owned and sheltered dogs in southern Italy (a historically low-risk area) found 17% overall microfilaria prevalence, with sheltered dogs, mongrels, and rural-housed dogs all significantly more affected — a reminder that intake screening assumptions based on "non-endemic" status can be wrong for the shelter population specifically (Napoli, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Reassuring data for routine spay/neuter in heartworm-positive dogs: in dogs undergoing elective ovariohysterectomy, heartworm-infected dogs (n=11) showed no significant difference in surgical blood loss (median 7 g in both groups, P=0.49), buccal mucosal bleeding time, platelet function (PFA-100 closure time), or thromboelastography compared with uninfected dogs (n=21) — heartworm infection alone does not appear to increase perioperative bleeding risk, which is useful when a shelter needs to sterilize a heartworm-positive dog before transfer or treatment (Cole, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Ectoparasites

External parasites are all arthropods. Most can feed on any host; only lice and Demodex are strictly host-specific. The management split is between parasites that live entirely on the host and those (fleas, myiasis) with environmental stages.

Host specificity and lice notes

  • Only lice and Demodex mites are strictly host-specific and do not infest a different species. All other arthropods are opportunistic feeders.
  • Lice (pediculosis) — two feeding types: sucking lice (Anoplura) have a head about as wide as the thorax and feed on blood (heavy burdens → anaemia in the young/debilitated); chewing/biting lice (Mallophaga) have a head wider than the thorax and eat skin debris/secretions. Lice are host- AND species-specific — dog lice do not infest cats or humans (essentially no zoonosis).
  • Species: dogs — the sucking louse Linognathus setosus and the chewing louse Trichodectes canis; cats — a single chewing louse, Felicola subrostratus.
  • Clinical & who gets them: pruritus, scaling, a dull/unkempt coat, restlessness and patchy alopecia; heavy sucking-lice burdens cause anaemia. Pediculosis is a marker of poor husbandry — seen in young, old, debilitated, matted or overcrowded animals.
  • Life cycle & transmission: the entire cycle runs on the host (egg/nit → nymph → adult, ~3–4 weeks); nits are cemented to hair shafts. Spread by direct contact and fomites (shared brushes/bedding); lice survive only a few days off the host, so environmental control matters far less than for fleas.
  • Treatment: most ectoparasiticides work — fipronil, imidacloprid, selamectin, lime sulfur, permethrin (dogs only), or isoxazolines; treat all in-contact animals and repeat (nits survive the first dose).
  • Nits are not killed by insecticides, so a population persists unless nits are mechanically removed; treat at least twice, one week apart, and separate infested animals.
  • Diagnosis: lice are visible to the naked eye — find adult lice or nits (eggs cemented to hair shafts) directly or with a hand lens; separate sucking from chewing lice by head width (vs the thorax).

Demodex in shelter dogs notesstd ref

  • Biology: Demodex canis is a cigar-shaped mite living in hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Pups acquire it from the dam during the first 1–3 days of nursing — this is the ONLY significant route of transmission for D. canis. It is NOT contagious between adults.
  • Feline species: cats have two main Demodex (plus a third, unnamed). D. cati is long-bodied and lives in follicles — facial/periocular alopecia and crusting, usually a marker of immunosuppression or systemic disease (FIV/FeLV, diabetes, hyperadrenocorticism). D. gatoi is short and lives superficially in the stratum corneum — intensely pruritic and contagious between cats. The D. gatoi contagiousness is a high-frequency exam distinction. A 100-cat survey of shelter and feral populations found 10% D. gatoi-positive on PCR, but most positives had a negative skin scrape and no visible lesions — asymptomatic carriers are a real source of contagion, so a negative scrape in a pruritic cat should not rule out D. gatoi (Baiz, Veterinary Dermatology, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Localized vs. generalized:
    • Localized: 1–4 focal patches of alopecia and scaling, usually on the face or forelimbs. Fewer than 5% progress to generalized. Resolves spontaneously in most cases. Treatment may not be required.
    • Generalized: 5+ focal areas or involvement of an entire body region, or pododemodicosis (all feet). Generalized demodicosis is a flag for underlying immunosuppression.
  • Immunosuppression — why it matters in shelters:
    • Juvenile-onset (under 18 months): indicates a heritable defect in T-cell-mediated immunity. Neuter the dog before adoption to prevent passing on the predisposition. Recommend owner not breed related animals.
    • Adult-onset (over 18 months): always indicates an underlying disease. Investigate: hypothyroidism, hyperadrenocorticism, neoplasia, immunosuppressive drugs. In cats: FIV or FeLV status.
    • Shelter-specific risk: prolonged LOS animals (>2–4 weeks) are at elevated risk due to chronic stress-mediated cortisol elevation and secondary immunosuppression. Monitor these animals actively for early skin changes.
  • Screening in the shelter: perform a deep skin scrape (pinch the skin fold firmly before scraping, to express mites from follicles) or pluck hairs from the margin of a lesion, on any LOS dog with alopecia, scaling, follicular papules, or thickened skin. A negative scrape does not rule out demodicosis in heavily scarred or crusted lesions (PCR of follicular contents may be needed).
  • Treatment:
    • Isoxazolines (fluralaner, afoxolaner, sarolaner, lotilaner) are now the treatment of choice for generalized demodicosis: easy oral dosing, safe, highly effective, used monthly or as a single dose depending on the product. A 130-dog trial of monthly oral sarolaner+moxidectin+pyrantel found no live mites detectable after two to three monthly treatments, non-inferior to an afoxolaner+milbemycin comparator (Becskei, Veterinary Dermatology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
    • Amitraz remains FDA-approved for canine demodicosis; whole-body dips every 1–2 weeks are effective but labor-intensive, require PPE, and carry toxicity risk for staff. Less practical in a high-volume shelter setting.
    • Ivermectin (daily oral) is effective and widely used but not FDA-approved; avoid in MDR1/ABCB1-mutation-risk breeds (collies, shelties, Australian shepherds, etc.).
  • Adoption criteria: generalized demodicosis should be fully resolved (or at minimum showing consistent improvement with a documented treatment plan) before adoption. Document the heritable status for juvenile-onset cases and provide a written neuter recommendation with the adoption file.

Fleas notes

  • Life cycle: egg → larva (3 stages) → pupa → adult; only ~5% of the population (adults) is on the host — the other ~95% (eggs, larvae, pupae) is in the environment. Egg-to-adult takes ~2–3 weeks (up to months); the pupa is the hardiest stage (survives months, resists insecticides) and hatches in response to heat, CO₂ and vibration. Fleas are acquired from a contaminated environment, NOT by jumping host-to-host.
  • Adults take blood (flea-bite anemia in the young, old, or debilitated) and cause flea-allergy dermatitis with lumbosacral and tail-base alopecia. C. felis transmits Dipylidium, Bartonella, Rickettsia, hemoplasmas, and Francisella tularensis.
  • Diagnosis: find adult fleas or "flea dirt" (digested-blood faeces) on flea-combing — flea dirt smeared on a damp white paper turns rusty red; in flea-allergy dermatitis (FAD) few or no fleas may actually be seen.
  • Treat with an adulticide plus environmental control (washing and vacuuming). For an obvious infestation at admission, nitenpyram (Capstar) kills adult fleas within hours, alongside a product effective against the developing (immature) stages.
  • Products for the immature stages: insect growth regulators (IGRs) — lufenuron (Program), (S)-methoprene, pyriproxyfen — and long-acting adulticides that also break the life cycle: spinosad, imidacloprid, selamectin, and the isoxazolines (fluralaner / afoxolaner / sarolaner / lotilaner). Because ~95% of the population is in the environment, an IGR + environmental clean-up is essential — not an adulticide alone.

Myiasis and isoxazolines notes

  • Primary myiasis requires living tissue (the screwworm Cochliomyia hominivorax is a reportable disease). Secondary myiasis is opportunistic invasion of devitalized tissue on soiled, matted, or wounded animals; forensic entomology can estimate the duration of neglect or time since death.
  • Diagnosis: directly visualise fly larvae (maggots) in wounds or soiled/matted fur (clip and examine); identify and report the screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) if present.
  • The isoxazolines (afoxolaner, fluralaner, lotilaner, sarolaner) are highly effective against sarcoptes, demodex, otodectes, and lynxacarus, and are often the treatment of choice for generalized demodicosis given ease of dosing, short course, and safety.
Exam trapFleas do not commonly jump from host to host; animals acquire fleas from a contaminated environment, and the pupa is the hardiest stage, persisting for months. Environmental control is therefore as important as treating the animal.

Tick biology and management std ref

  • The brown dog tick is the shelter exception: Rhipicephalus sanguineus is uniquely adapted so that all life stages readily feed on dogs and it can complete its entire life cycle indoors within human habitations — long called "the bane of shelters, boarding kennels, and domiciles." An unrecognized infestation spreads quickly and widely and usually requires professional pest control of the premises; inspect cracks, behind cages, and other daytime hiding sites routinely.
  • All other ticks self-limit indoors: Ixodes, Dermacentor, and Amblyomma need rodent, bird, or reptile larval hosts, so even an engorged female that drops off in the shelter will not persist in the environment.
  • Removal technique: use fine-tipped forceps (never bare hands), grasp as close to the skin as possible, pull with steady gentle traction, and remove the mouthparts (retained mouthparts cause an inflammatory reaction). Confirm the tick is dead before disposal, then still treat with a topical or isoxazoline acaricide and re-inspect, since tiny ticks are easily missed.
  • Why prompt removal matters: transmission of many tick-borne pathogens (e.g. Lyme disease) is time-dependent — the tick must feed for a period before organisms cross its salivary glands — so removing ticks early can prevent infection.
  • Tick paralysis: feeding female ticks secrete a salivary neurotoxin → ascending lower-motor-neuron (flaccid) paralysis (weakness → recumbency → respiratory failure), often with megaoesophagus/regurgitation. Main species: Dermacentor (North America) and Ixodes holocyclus (Australia — the most severe). Removing every tick reverses it in ~24–72 h for most species, but I. holocyclus can worsen for 1–2 days after removal and needs tick-antitoxin serum + intensive/respiratory care. Search the whole animal (inside ears, between toes, lips).
  • Emerging tick: the Asian longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), introduced to the United States in 2017, can build up to massive infestations and is an expanding concern.
  • Fluralaner's duration of protection against the Asian longhorned tick: a field trial in China found fluralaner chewable tablets reduced tick counts by 99.95% (vs. 99.83% for a reference isoxazoline), with 98.3% of dogs tick-free by day 7 and 100% tick-free from day 14 onward — providing full protection for 12 weeks against Haemaphysalis longicornis and 8 weeks against Rhipicephalus sanguineus under field conditions (Hu, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Natural DEET alternatives for pets (DEET itself is unsafe in dogs, cats, and ferrets): among five major essential-oil components tested against brown dog tick larvae, carvacrol and geraniol showed strong repellent activity, with carvacrol approaching the efficacy of 15% DEET at some concentrations and time points — early evidence that plant-derived repellents could become viable options where DEET cannot be used (Koc, Veterinary Parasitology, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Chagas disease correctedstandard ref

  • Trypanosoma cruzi is endemic in the southern United States and along the Mexican border. It is transmitted by triatomine (kissing) bugs, which deposit infective stages in their feces near the bite wound, or it is acquired when a dog ingests the bug. Signs can include acute myocarditis and right-sided cardiomegaly.
  • US prevalence data (systematic review/meta-analysis, 2000–2024): pooled canine T. cruzi seroprevalence across 16 studies (4,974 dogs, 5 states) was 12% (95% CI 7–21%), highest in Louisiana (18%) and in working dogs (32%), and lowest in shelter dogs (7%); prevalence also rose significantly with study year (~11% higher odds per year), consistent with either a true increase in transmission or improved detection over time (Victor, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Diagnosis: motile trypomastigotes on a blood smear / buffy-coat during the brief acute phase; serology (IFA / ELISA) for the chronic phase (scant parasitaemia); PCR confirms. Treatment: benznidazole or nifurtimox have limited efficacy (mainly acute) and are largely unavailable in veterinary practice — management is chiefly supportive/cardiac, and prognosis is guarded to poor once myocardial disease develops.
ClarifiedThe two Texas figures often quoted are both validly sourced and are not contradictory — they use different denominators: ~20.3% seropositivity among samples submitted to the Texas state diagnostic lab (Kjos 2008) vs ~8.8% among shelter-housed dogs sampled statewide (Tenney 2014). Either is defensible depending on the population cited. The key testable point remains the vector and the stercorarian (fecal) transmission route.
Exam trap — Chagas is not just a "southern border" diseaseA 12-dog case series found T. cruzi infection diagnosed in dogs that had spent as little as under a year in Texas before moving to a nonendemic area, with cardiac abnormalities in 11/12 and 5 dying unexpectedly — and a companion survey found 49% of North American veterinary cardiologists reported limited self-knowledge of Chagas disease. Any dog with unexplained cardiomyopathy or sudden death with recent time in an endemic area, anywhere in the country, warrants T. cruzi on the differential (Gavic, JAVMA, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Cytauxzoon felis ("bobcat fever") std ref

  • Protozoan transmitted by ticks (Amblyomma americanum, Dermacentor variabilis); the bobcat is the natural reservoir. South-central and south-eastern US.
  • Acutely fatal in domestic cats: high fever, lethargy, anorexia, icterus, dyspnoea, then hypothermia and death within about a week if untreated. Schizont-laden macrophages occlude vessels (lungs, liver, spleen, lymph nodes); piroplasms then infect RBCs.
  • Diagnosis: "signet-ring" piroplasms in RBCs on blood smear; PCR. Treatment: atovaquone + azithromycin with intensive supportive care; survivors may become subclinical carriers.

Hepatozoonosis std ref

  • Unique route — the dog is infected by EATING the infected tick, not by the tick's bite.
  • American canine hepatozoonosis (H. americanum) — vector Amblyomma maculatum (Gulf Coast tick); severe disease with marked periosteal bone proliferation, fever, muscle wasting/pain, and an extreme neutrophilic leukocytosis. H. canis — vector Rhipicephalus sanguineus; usually milder.
  • Diagnosis: gamonts in neutrophils/monocytes on a blood smear; muscle biopsy ("onion-skin" cysts) for H. americanum; PCR. Treatment is prolonged and typically controls rather than cures.

Babesia gibsoni standard ref

  • A tick-borne (and bite-transmitted) intracellular protozoan causing hemolytic anemia, with notable spread among Pit Bull-type dogs through bite wounds.
  • Diagnosis: small single intra-erythrocytic signet-ring piroplasms (smaller than B. canis) on a stained blood smear — low sensitivity in chronic carriers; PCR is the most sensitive and speciates it; serology (IFA) shows exposure. Expect regenerative haemolytic anaemia + thrombocytopenia; prepatent ~1–2 weeks.
  • Therapy reduces parasitemia and clinical signs but rarely achieves complete clearance, so treated dogs are permanent subclinical carriers at risk of relapse and need lifelong tick prevention.
  • Atovaquone plus azithromycin is the more effective protocol; the older triple therapy (clindamycin, metronidazole, doxycycline) reduces but does not clear infection.
  • Alternative combination: atovaquone + azithromycin + artesunate (10-day course) achieved successful long-term parasitaemia clearance (no relapse out to 720 days) in 12 naturally infected American Pit Bull Terriers, with hematology/biochemistry normalising within 14 days (Karasova, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below). In Europe, B. gibsoni prevalence runs around 1% overall but is disproportionately higher in American Pit Bull Terriers, consistent with the bite-transmission route (Teodorowski, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
Adoption counselingThese dogs can ethically be adopted out with full disclosure of permanent carrier status, relapse potential, and the need for routine tick prevention. Note that bite transmission, not only ticks, is a major route in fighting-breed dogs.

Babesia canis (large Babesia) std ref

  • The common large Babesia: B. canis (vogeli) is the most common large Babesia species, transmitted by the brown dog tick Rhipicephalus sanguineus, and most frequently diagnosed in the southeastern United States.
  • Morphology: B. canis piroplasms are typically found in pairs within red cells — contrast B. gibsoni, whose piroplasms are smaller and ring-shaped. Accurate species identification guides therapy.
  • Epidemiology: prevalence is higher in adult dogs than in puppies, and greyhounds have a notably higher seroprevalence than other breeds. Disease is usually milder than with B. gibsoni, and many B. canis infections are subclinical.
  • Treatment: imidocarb dipropionate 5–6.6 mg/kg IM or SC, repeated in 2 weeks — a different protocol from B. gibsoni (atovaquone + azithromycin), which is why distinguishing the two species on smear or PCR matters.
  • Co-infection is common at the US–Mexico border: in shelter dogs sampled in San Diego/Imperial counties (US) and Mexicali/Tijuana (Mexico), E. canis and A. platys infection ranged 0–27% and 0–33% by shelter, with seropositivity to Rickettsia (RMSF), Ehrlichia, and Anaplasma all present at every site — a reminder that RMSF can co-occur with these other brown-dog-tick pathogens, which is itself a human health risk to staff (Backus, JAVMA, 2023 — see journal abstracts below). A shelter-dog triple-infection case series (B. vogeli + E. canis + A. platys) found 28 days of doxycycline monotherapy (10 mg/kg/day) cleared all three pathogens by PCR and normalised blood counts, with severe thrombocytopenia taking longer (~70 days) than mild-moderate cases to fully resolve (Wongtawan, Animals, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

  • Reduce environmental hazards around the shelter: remove standing water, garbage, decaying organic matter, and long grass to limit vector breeding.

Moxidectin is repeated monthly until no antigen detected (NAD) status is confirmed. Sustained release injectable moxidectin, in combination with doxycycline, may provide an alternative in remote regions or in settings where significant compliance or accessibility concerns exist, but more studies are needed. In moxi-doxy protocols, doxycycline should be repeated annually until NAD. This review summarizes the safety and efficacy of moxi-doxy, addresses controversies surrounding this treatment approach, and provides detailed recommendations for treatment regimens and post-treatment testing.

Optimizing heartworm diagnosis in dogs using multiple test combinations
Lane · Parasites & Vectors · 2021

Abstract. Background: Various heartworm (HW) diagnostic testing modalities detect products of, or reactions to, different life cycle stages of Dirofilaria immitis. Microfilariae (Mf) can be directly visualized in blood, antigen (Ag) from immature and adult heartworms may be detected on commercial assays, and antibody (Ab) tests detect the host immune response to larval stages. Ag and Mf tests are commonly used in dogs, which frequently carry adult HW infections, but Ab tests have only been validated for use in cats. In some HW-infected dogs, Ag is blocked by immune complexing leading to false-negative results. Heat-treatment (HT) to disrupt these complexes can increase the sensitivity of HW Ag tests. The aim of this study was to compare different methods for diagnosing HW infection in dogs at high risk using individual and paired diagnostic tests, including an exploration of using Ab tests designed for cats to test canine samples.

Methods: One hundred stray adult (≥ 2-year-old) dogs in Florida shelters were tested using Mf, HW Ag, and HW Ab tests (feline HW Ab tests currently not commercially validated/approved for use in dogs); two versions of each test platform were used.

Results: Fourteen dogs tested positive using point-of-care (POC) Ag tests; an additional 2 dogs tested positive with microtiter well assay, and an additional 12 dogs tested positive using HT Ag testing. For individual tests, Ag test sensitivity/specificity compared to HT Ag was 50-57%/100%, and Ab tests were 46-64%/82-94%. Sensitivity estimates for individual tests were higher when comparing to non-HT Ag. Pairing POC Ag tests with Mf tests improved sensitivity without loss of specificity, while pairing POC Ag and Ab tests modestly increased sensitivity at the expense of specificity.

Conclusions: Screening dogs for HW infection using both POC Ag and Mf detection, which is recommended by the American Heartworm Society, improved diagnostic performance in this study compared to single Ag test use, but may have missed more than one in four infected dogs. The need to improve access to highly accurate, rapid, and inexpensive large-scale HW testing for dogs in animal shelters remains largely unmet by current testing availability. The development of practical and validated protocols that incorporate heat or chemical treatment to disrupt Ag-Ab complexes in POC testing or decreasing the cost and time required for such testing in reference laboratories might provide solutions to this unmet need. Similar studies performed in countries where the prevalence of parasites such as D. repens or A. vasorum is different to the USA could potentially yield very different positive predictive values for both HT and non-HT Ag tests.

Abstract. BACKGROUND: Giardia duodenalis is a protozoan of dogs and cats that comprises several genotypes (assemblages A-G). The common assemblages affecting small animals (C, D, F, G) are not associated with disease in humans. However, assemblages A and B can be zoonotic. Abstract. BACKGROUND: Borrelia burgdorferi and Anaplasma phagocytophilum are transmitted by Ixodes spp., with antibodies having been detected in cats in endemic areas. The combination of selamectin plus sarolaner (Revolution® Plus/Stronghold® Plus; Zoetis; RP) is effective against Ixodes spp. for 1 month. Abstract.
