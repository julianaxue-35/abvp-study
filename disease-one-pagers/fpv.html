Feline Panleukopenia (FPV)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

The feline parvovirus — a non-enveloped single-stranded DNA virus and close cousin of canine parvovirus (CPV). Targets rapidly dividing cells (bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, gut epithelium, fetal / neonatal cerebellum) → profound panleukopenia + cerebellar hypoplasia. Only one serotype; does not pass on to dogs.

Virology

  • Non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA virus
  • Targets rapidly dividing cells: bone marrow (BM) (BM = bone marrow), lymphoid tissue, gastrointestinal (GI) epithelium, and the fetal & neonatal cerebellum
  • ONLY ONE SEROTYPE — does not pass on to dogs

Transmission

  • Faecal–oral (direct / fomite)
  • Shed in all secretions & excretions
  • Transplacental infection
  • Droplet spread can occur if a concurrent upper-respiratory-tract (URT) (URT = upper respiratory tract) virus causes sneezing
  • Flies & fleas can act as mechanical vectors

Pathophysiology

  • Virus → transferrin receptor → oropharyngeal → lymphoid tissue → actively dividing tissue (requires the S-phase (synthesis phase) of cell division)+
  • → ↓ white blood cells (WBCs) (WBC = white blood cell)
  • Antibody appears 3–4 days after the WBC count rebounds
  • → crypt epithelium destruction
  • In utero: targets the cerebellar Purkinje cells → cerebellar hypoplasia

Clinical Signs

  • GI signs · ↓ WBC · immunosuppression · cerebellar hypoplasia
  • Early embryonic death · abortion · resorption · retinal dysplasia
  • Incubation 2–14 days (usually 2–7 days); no carrier state
  • Shedding 5–7 days, occasionally ~6 weeks; starts 1–2 days POST-infection, ~2–3 days before signs
⚠ Trap: a modified-live vaccine (MLV) → false positive on the faecal antigen test for up to 5–12 days.

Clinical Forms 4

  1. Subacute — recovers within 1–3 days
  2. Peracute — sudden death 4–9 days after exposure + GI signs; mortality ~100% (kittens < 6 months)
  3. Acute (most common) — biphasic fever, GI signs + crouched-head posture; mortality 25–90%
  4. Perinatal — early gestation vs late gestation (hypoplasia) → stillbirth, abortion

Prognostic Factors

  • Leukopenia < 2000/µL
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Hypoalbuminaemia < 30 g/L
  • Hypokalaemia < 4 mmol/L
  • The protective antibody titre at the time of exposure
+ note: recovery → lifelong immunity.

Diagnosis

  1. Faecal antigen ELISA (ELISA = enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) test
  2. Antibody testing — via haemagglutination inhibition (HI), ELISA, immunofluorescent antibody (IFA) (HI = haemagglutination inhibition; IFA = immunofluorescent antibody)
    • Does not separate vaccine-induced from infection-induced antibody → useless in sick cats
  3. CBC (complete blood count) · histopathology

Treatment

  • Similar to CPV, but here CPMA (canine parvovirus monoclonal antibody) is used in cats
  • Survival ~85%; shorter treatment course
+ check: CPMA is labelled as a canine product, so use in cats is off-label / an emerging use — confirm before relying on it.

Titre Testing exposure

  • ≥ 80 — protected, low risk
  • < 80 — high risk → 14-day quarantine

📄 Journal Articles

  • Feline chaphamaparvovirus (FeChPV / "fechavirus"): found in 30/136 cats (22%) overall — 12% in an asymptomatic shelter, 11–25% in shelters with vomiting/diarrhoea clusters, up to 88% in one shelter with acute disease; detected in 33% of diarrheic vs 17% of healthy cats. It correlates with facility outbreaks, but its precise pathogenic role remains ambiguous because it is also found in healthy cats.
  • CPMA for feline panleukopenia (shelter): 55 cats (24 given CPMA at 0.2 mL/kg SQ, 31 without). Median time to clinical resolution & treatment discontinuation was ~3.5 days shorter with CPMA; overall survival 85.5% with no significant mortality difference (20.8% CPMA vs 9.7% without, p=0.27) and no adverse effects. CPMA is canine-labelled — used off-label here to shorten isolation length of stay.
  • Faecal FPV DNA shedding after clinical panleukopenia (kittens): median viral copy numbers fell below the diagnostic cut-off by day 7, with declining PCR positivity over time; SNAP results, diarrhoea and systemic signs were inconsistent with qPCR positivity. The authors recommend isolation for at least 14 days after diagnosis.
  • FPV DNA shedding after MLV vaccination (shelter): 21.6% of vaccinated cats without signs were PCR-positive (mainly day 7), but with much lower copy numbers than clinical infection, and the point-of-care antigen test was negative in all. FPV qPCR cannot distinguish subclinical infection from vaccine shedding.
  • ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in shelter cats: extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales were found in 2.3% of faecal samples — low prevalence, but shelter cats can harbour these resistant bacteria. (antimicrobial-resistance carriage, filed on this feline-enteric sheet)
  • FPV diagnostic testing in shelters (SNAP vs PCR): faecal SNAP sensitivity was only ~55% while vomit PCR reached 100%; specificity stayed high (96–100%). Positive (even weak-positive) SNAP results correlated with higher viral copy numbers. SNAP is a screening tool — a positive faecal result is reliable in symptomatic cats, but a negative needs PCR confirmation before ruling out FPV.
  • FPV evolution & virulence: FPV (origin ~1920s) held a stable population size then expanded after 2000, spreading mostly by local evolution with few long-distance introductions. Different strains may carry differential virulence — viral phylogeny/strain may matter for clinical severity.
