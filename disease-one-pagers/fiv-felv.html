FIV, FeLV & Haemotropic Mycoplasma (feline)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Two feline retroviruses plus red-cell haemoplasmas — the anaemia / immunosuppression cluster of shelter cats. FeLV = the "friendly cat" virus (grooming & nursing); FIV = the "fighting cat" virus (bite wounds); haemotropic mycoplasmas attach to red cells → immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia.

FeLV vs FIV — the basics

  • FeLV (feline leukaemia virus) — the "friendly cat" virus; an oncornavirus (gammaretrovirus)
  • Spread by mutual grooming / nursing → shortened lifespan ~5 years
  • FIV (feline immunodeficiency virus) — the "fighting cat" virus; a lentivirus
  • Spread by deep inoculation (bite wounds) → normal lifespan; can affect other animals

Do Not Share Between Cats

  • Needles · suture · tattoo equipment
  • Surgical / dental instruments
  • Endotracheal (ET) tubes

FIV — Diagnosis

  • FIV antibody test; western blot (now replaced by PCR — polymerase chain reaction)
  • Retest in 60 days after recent exposure
  • A positive in a kitten < 6 months is almost always MDA (maternally-derived antibody)
⚠ Trap: a kitten < 6 mo may test antibody-positive purely from maternal antibody — not true infection. Retest after 6 months of age.

FIV — Vaccine

  • Not recommended in shelters
  • If given: 3 vaccines 2–3 weeks apart, then annual
  • Use Zoetis Witness & Anigen Rapid to detect true infection vs vaccine-induced antibody

FeLV — Outcomes After Exposure 3

  1. Abortive (20–30%)
    • IFA (immunofluorescent antibody), virus isolation & PCR all NEGATIVE
    • Antibody POSITIVE
  2. Regressive (30–40%)
    • IFA & antigen NEGATIVE
    • PCR detectable but low copy number
    • Antigenaemic phase; low disease risk
    • No shedding unless reactivated
  3. Progressive (30–40%)
    • IFA, antigen & PCR POSITIVE (high copy number)
    • Viraemia spreads to organs; high disease risk

Clinical Signs FeLV / FIV shared

  • Fading kitten syndrome in neonates
  • Both drive anaemia, chronic inflammation, neoplasia & opportunistic infection
  • B-cell lymphoma in old cats
  • T-cell lymphoma in young cats

FeLV — Diagnosis

  • Antigen test → p27 antigen
  • IFA (immunofluorescent antibody)
  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction)
  • Retest in 30 days after recent exposure
  • Unweaned kittens antigen-positive? — early transient antigenaemia often transitions to a regressive infection (retest twice, ~6 weeks apart)
⚠ Trap: never euthanise a healthy unweaned kitten on a single intake FeLV positive.

FeLV — Vaccine

  • Lowest-sarcoma-risk formulations preferred (injection-site sarcoma risk)
  • Non-core; for at-risk cats (e.g. sanctuaries)
  • From 8 weeks: 2 doses 3–4 weeks apart, then annual
  • Long-term high-density sanctuary housing ↑ infection rates, ↑ the lethal progressive FeLV form & ↑ chronic stress

Feline Haemotropic Mycoplasma

  • Obligate epicellular parasites that attach to the red blood cell (RBC) surface → IMHA (immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia)
  • Diagnosis: PCR / blood-smear evaluation
Signs
  • Collapse · splenomegaly · fever · lethargy
  • ↑ respiratory rate · pale mucous membranes

Species

  • Mycoplasma haemofelis — the most pathogenic; causes severe haemolytic anaemia
  • M. haemominutum (Haemobartonella felis) — low pathogenicity; in immunocompromised cats; subclinical; can cause anaemia if combined with FeLV / FIV + stress
+ check: "Haemobartonella felis" was the old genus name for both feline haemoplasmas — large form = M. haemofelis, small form = "Candidatus M. haemominutum". Your note pairs it with M. haemominutum.

Transmission

  • Fleas · bite wounds
  • Blood transfusion · shared vet equipment
  • Always co-test for FIV + FeLV

Treatment & Prognosis

  • Doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO SID × 28 days (PO = by mouth, SID = once daily)
  • Alternatives: pradofloxacin / marbofloxacin
  • Supportive: prednisolone · blood transfusion
  • Prognosis: many cats remain PCR-positive long-term; relapse can occur
  • Strict flea control

📄 Journal Articles

  • FeLV point-of-care (POC) test performance: FeLV true prevalence was 0.8% in apparently-healthy vs 5.3% in sick shelter cats. Positive predictive value (PPV) was just 40.1% in healthy vs 99% in sick cats; negative predictive value (NPV) 79.8% in healthy vs 99.2% in sick cats. PPV tracks prevalence — the healthy-cat PPV is poor, and the authors note serial re-testing may not meaningfully improve it (ties to the biostats sheet).+
  • FeLV lifetime study — whole blood improves detection: testing anticoagulated whole blood on SNAP identified 5 and 9 more low-positive FeLV cats than plasma or serum respectively; some positives gave variable SNAP results on plasma/serum. Combine SNAP (whole blood) + PetChek ELISA + qPCR to class cats as high-positive, low-positive or cryptic/negative — high-positive cats carry the greatest risk of early mortality.
  • FIV & FeLV in the Brazilian semiarid: 454 cats — FIV prevalence 30.2%, FeLV 1.1%, 0.7% co-infected. FIV was strongly associated with male sex, age 19–78 months, crossbreed, oral disease, reduced red-cell count and an albumin:globulin (A:G) ratio <0.6. FeLV numbers were too low for statistical analysis.
  • Leukocyte ratios as retrovirus survival markers: FeLV-positive cats had a lower monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) than controls, and MLR at diagnosis was higher in FeLV+ cats that died 1–3 years later than in those alive at 3 years. Overall, the neutrophil-, monocyte- and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios did not differ significantly by retrovirus status and had very limited prognostic value.
  • FIV prevalence & standards-of-care opinions: FIV prevalence is ~5–8% and has not changed in the 40 years since discovery; >90% of articles report higher prevalence in older male cats, and prevalence is lower in North America/Europe than other continents. FIV is not typically a high-concern disease and is often linked to oral-cavity infection; vaccination is not generally recommended and has been discontinued in North America.
  • Shelter management of FeLV/FIV testing: 83% of shelters did at least some testing, mostly point-of-care combo devices (FeLV antigen + FIV antibody); adoptable cats were screened most (94%), TNR cats least (18%). Outcomes for positive cats: adoption 64%, transfer 54%, euthanasia 43% — euthanasia was more common for FeLV+ than FIV+ cats.
  • Discordant FeLV p27 vs PCR (21 cats, haematologic disease): cats with bone-marrow disease resembling progressive FeLV that are positive on lateral-flow and ELISA p27 antigen tests should be confirmed with FeLV proviral-DNA PCR — especially in low-prevalence populations.
  • Ocular disease in feline systemic infections: the most important agents causing ocular disease were FeLV, FIP and Cryptococcus spp.; the commonest ocular findings were uveitis, optic neuritis and optic-nerve meningitis.
  • Clinicopathology of FeLV+ cats at a referral centre (Florida, 2008–2019): mean age at diagnosis ~9 yr (older than prior data). Main reasons for presentation: abnormal CBC, pleural effusion, anorexia. Common abnormalities: anaemia 71% (74% non-regenerative), thrombocytopenia, ↑AST, hyperbilirubinaemia, hypokalaemia; 7% were also FIV+. Commonest diagnoses: neoplasia 76% (younger cats), bone-marrow disorders 12%; survival to discharge 88%. FeLV is uncommon at referral centres — the main associated diagnosis is neoplasia in cats <4 yr.
  • Feral-cat retrovirus prevalence, San Mateo CA (2001–2016): over 15 years FIV prevalence rose 5.52% → 6.41% while FeLV fell 1.73% → 0.29%. Despite a 61.63% drop in feral-cat clinic admissions, male FIV prevalence stayed similar and female FIV prevalence increased.
  • FeLV prevalence in owned cats (SE Asia & Taiwan): FeLV ranged from 0% (Indonesia) to 18.5% (Thailand); prevalence varied by country of origin, age, lifestyle and oral-mucosal abnormalities.
  • FeLV p27 antigen concentration & proviral load predict survival: using cut-offs to separate high- vs low-positive cats, high-positive cats had a median survival of 1.37 years, whereas 93.1% of low-positive cats were still alive at follow-up. Quantitative p27 antigen concentration and proviral DNA load correlated strongly with survival.
