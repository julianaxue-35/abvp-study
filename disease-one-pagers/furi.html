Feline Upper Respiratory Infection (FURI)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

A multi-pathogen upper respiratory complex of shelter cats. Not airborne and rarely fatal on its own — the real drivers are stress and length of stay (LOS), so control is about biosecurity and flow, not sterility.

Overview

  • Multi-pathogen complex — LOS (length of stay) is the single greatest risk factor; fomites are the main route
  • FHV-1 (feline herpesvirus-1) is triggered by stress → biosecurity means managing it, not eliminating it
  • URTD (upper respiratory tract disease) is NOT airborne → separate caging / room ventilation is not required; can travel within 5 ft (1.5 m)

FHV-1 herpesvirus

  • Feline herpesvirus-1 — latency in the trigeminal ganglia (50–97% seroprevalence; stress reactivation)
  • Enveloped DNA herpesvirus
  • Spread by oral / nasal / ocular secretions over short distances / fomites
  • Active shedding peaks @ day 7, lasts 2–3 weeks; can shed intermittently
  • Chronic cases → rhinosinusitis → turbinate destruction
  • Can cause dendritic corneal ulcers (pathognomonic for FHV-1)+
+ check: the note's "non-enveloped RNA" label belongs to FCV, not FHV-1. FHV-1 is an enveloped DNA herpesvirus.

FCV calicivirus

  • Feline calicivirus — non-enveloped RNA virus
  • Causes oral / tongue ulcers · febrile limping
  • A positive PCR (polymerase chain reaction) indicates primary disease
  • NO corneal ulcers (contrast with FHV-1)
  • Chronic carrier state — can shed continuously for months

Other Pathogens

  • Mycoplasma felis — normal flora + opportunist; a positive PCR indicates primary disease; causes chemosis; short-lived in the environment; 4 weeks of doxycycline; cat-to-cat contact; an obligate intracellular bacterium with no cell wall
  • Chlamydia felis — causes chemosis; an obligate intracellular bacterium
  • Bordetella bronchiseptica (Bb) — destroys respiratory cilia; zoonotic; dog → cat spread
  • Streptococcus zooepidemicus ("Strep zoo") — acute outbreak, necrotizing disease; requires PCR testing
  • Feline influenza (H7N2) — mild signs; zoonotic; cats are also susceptible to avian, swine & canine strains

Prevention

  • FCV — use bleach 1:32; accelerated hydrogen peroxide (AHP) / potassium peroxymonosulfate
  • Reduce stress and LOS (length of stay); use double-compartment housing
  • Risk by timeframe
    • Short-term → FHV-1, Bordetella
    • Long-term / sanctuary → FCV, Mycoplasma felis
  • High-stress shelters skew the mix toward the persistent shedders

Diagnosis

  • RT-PCR (reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction) — use sterile tubes and plastic-stemmed synthetic-fibre swabs
  • Severe signs + high mortality + rapid spread → likely FCV; confirm by molecular / necropsy
⚠ Trap: a modified-live (MLV) FVRCP (feline viral rhinotracheitis, calicivirus, panleukopenia) vaccine can cause a false-positive PCR for ~1–3 weeks.

Treatment Tx

  • Famciclovir 90 mg/kg PO q8h (by mouth, every 8 h)
  • Herpetic keratitis — topical idoxuridine / cidofovir
  • Doxycycline — for Mycoplasma felis, Bordetella & Chlamydia felis
  • Fluoroquinolones — use marbofloxacin & pradofloxacin as the preferred agents
  • Supportive care

📄 Journal Articles

  • SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) in shelter cats — pooled: active infection is rare (typically 0–2% PCR-positive; often none detected, e.g. 0/163). Many "inconclusive" results traced to a viral variant that escaped the standard N2 probe — a redesigned cat-specific probe reclassified ~97% of those as positive, so cats may carry an undescribed SARS-CoV-2-related virus. Both cat-to-cat and zoonotic transmission risk in shelters (including around spay surgery) appears LOW. (One Central-Valley study also found feline enteric coronavirus (FECV) in 18% of cats, mostly in adjacent cages.)
  • Intranasal (IN) maropitant for feline URT disease: IN maropitant showed no clinical benefit vs saline, with no evidence to support its routine use in managing FURI.
  • FCV — current understanding & control: FCV evades the host immune response; after the acute phase some cats stay persistently infected or undergo reinfection cycles with variants of the same strain or distinct field strains, becoming asymptomatic carriers. An integrated approach is essential to reduce FCV transmission and disease burden in feline populations.
  • Feline bordetellosis outbreak (limited-admission shelter): 43 cats — 93% morbidity but 100% live outcomes for treated cats; an off-site isolation facility and diagnostic-test-targeted treatment drove resolution in 45 days.
  • Mycoplasma felis PCR in feline lower-respiratory disease: in 55 cats, M. felis-positive BALF was associated with fewer systemic signs (0% vs 28%), more bronchial collapse (28% vs 6%), more radiographic alveolar lesions (57% vs 24%), higher BALF neutrophils and lower eosinophils; these cats received antibiotics more often. Detection did NOT worsen prognosis or survival.
  • Multiplex qPCR for SARS-CoV-2 + FRDC pathogens: two validated one-step multiplex assays (viral + bacterial), high specificity. Of 63 FRDC-suspect cats, 92.1% were positive for ≥1 pathogen and 57.1% had co-infection; M. felis (61.9%) was most common, then FHV-1 (30.2%), C. felis (28.7%), FCV (27%); SARS-CoV-2 in 2. Enables rapid combined viral/bacterial detection.
  • Doxycycline ± famciclovir in 373 kittens: for mild upper-respiratory/ocular disease, adding famciclovir sped clinical recovery/resolution (~75% resolved 4–5 days earlier) and reduced corneal disease vs doxycycline alone. Early famciclovir in mild FURI may be preferable to waiting for severe disease — shortening length of stay.
  • Chlamydia felis isolation & pathogenesis (Guangxi, China): of 396 cats screened for upper-respiratory pathogens, Mycoplasma felis was most prevalent (24.75%), then FCV (15.91%), C. felis (11.62%), FHV-1 (5.56%) and feline influenza A (1.52%); C. felis and M. felis co-occurred in 13/55 co-infected cats. On challenge, C. felis caused severe conjunctivitis with transient fever and respiratory signs, replicated heavily in turbinate / trachea / lung, then spread to liver, spleen, pancreas, kidney, heart and intestine.
  • FCV-associated paw-and-mouth disease: a household cat with feline calicivirus paw-and-mouth disease was treated with trypsin; management differs from that of respiratory FCV.
  • Nanoparticle ocular immunotherapy for herpesvirus keratitis: HSV-1 / FHV-1 eye infections are currently managed with antiviral drops (frequent dosing, irritation, slow resolution); non-specific ocular immunotherapy shows promise as a novel treatment for HSV-1 and FHV-1 ocular infection.
  • Multidrug-resistant Bordetella bronchiseptica outbreak (16 shelter cats): early use of necropsy, PCR and bacterial culture enabled rapid deployment of effective second-line antibiotics; the report stresses outbreak expertise and antibiotic stewardship in resource-limited shelters.
  • Chronic URTD & the respiratory/gut microbiome: in cats with chronic upper-respiratory disease, specific nasal and gut microbial community members were associated with the chronic clinical course — hinting the microbiome shapes persistence.
