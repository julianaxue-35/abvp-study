Canine Distemper Virus (CDV)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Enveloped, environmentally fragile ssRNA (single-stranded RNA) morbillivirus (family Paramyxoviridae). Multisystemic, biphasic disease: respiratory → gastrointestinal → neurological / ocular. A single serotype makes vaccination highly effective, but mildly-affected & recovered dogs keep shedding for months — a "smouldering" shelter problem.

Virology

  • Enveloped, environmentally fragile virus
  • Single-stranded RNA (ribonucleic acid); genus Morbillivirus, family Paramyxoviridae
  • Dog-to-dog spread; can infect wildlife animals; does NOT affect cats
  • Only 1 serotype → vaccine is very effective
⚠ Trap: CDV does NOT affect cats. Contrast canine parvovirus (CPV), which can cross-infect cats.+

Transmission & Pathogenesis

  • Direct contact; aerosol / droplet inhalation
  • Shed in all secretions & excretions
  • On PCR (polymerase chain reaction): low Ct (cycle threshold) = ↑ high viral load; high Ct = likely vaccine-related
  • Mildly affected & recovered dogs help maintain transmission cycles = a "smouldering problem"
  • CDV → lymphoid tissue → lymph nodes (LN). Replicates in macrophages / monocytes and T + B lymphocytes → lymph nodes, spleen, bone marrow (BM), Kupffer cells
⚠ Trap: high Ct = low viral load → think vaccine shedding, not active infection.

Risk

  • Unvaccinated animals at ANY age

Incubation & Clinical Signs

  • Incubation — 1–6 weeks
  • Biphasic course — starts with pyrexia, then malaise, respiratory signs, then GIT (gastrointestinal tract) signs:
    • Skin — digital & nasal hyperkeratosis
    • Neurological signs
    • Ocular — conjunctivitis, mucopurulent discharge, keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS), uveitis
    • Grey-pink retinal lesions that heal as hyper-reflective scars; sudden blindness

Recovery & Shedding

  • Recovered patients can still shed up to 4 months. No carrier state.
  • Recovery confirmed via qPCR (quantitative PCR) testing; a positive = ongoing contagiousness
  • On recovery → vaccinate immediately
⚠ Trap: "No carrier state" — but recovered dogs still shed for up to 4 months.

Diagnostic Samples

  • Deep laryngeal / nasal swab
  • Urine sediment / rectal sample
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)
  • Post-mortem: brain epithelium

Treatment

  • Only supportive treatment
  • Neurological signs are less responsive to recovery than respiratory signs

Prevention

  • Vaccinate dogs
  • In pregnant animals use recombinant canarypox-vectored distemper vaccine (rCDV)
  • Use an all-in / all-out system; avoid co-mingling

Outbreak Management triage

  • Triage — vaccinated vs unvaccinated, and ≥ 5 months vs < 5 months of age
  • Use serology + RT-PCR (reverse transcription PCR) testing to confirm immunity
  • During an outbreak, all dogs must be vaccinated during / before intake
  • House incoming dogs at least 25 ft (~6 m) away from medium / high-risk dogs

📄 Journal Article

  • Distemper outbreak, Galapagos Islands (2019): of 125 dogs with CDV-compatible signs, 74.4% tested positive; among positives, 82.2% had respiratory signs, 48.8% neurological and 28.9% gastrointestinal — a persistent threat, including to the endangered Galapagos sea lion.
  • Prolonged CDV RNA persistence & virus isolation (shelter dogs): CDV-positive dogs remained RT-PCR-positive for ~34 days on average, and 25% of those still shedding at 62 days stayed positive — yet infectious virus was isolated only in the first ~2 weeks (around peak RNA). RT-PCR can therefore gauge the end of infectious risk and clear recovered dogs faster for live release, improving welfare and lifesaving.
  • CDV seroprevalence in free-roaming dogs, Chitwan (Nepal): 80% of 100 free-roaming dogs were seropositive (past exposure); males had lower seroprevalence than females and adults higher than juveniles — a reservoir threatening wildlife.
  • CDV in autochthonous vs imported dogs, Southern Italy (2014–2021): CDV RNA in 6.8% overall (none in 2020–2021); detection was higher in imported dogs (18.3%) than stray (7.4%) or household dogs (3.9%).
  • CDV & nectin-4 in raccoon skin: raccoons are key CDV reservoirs; immunohistochemistry of even grossly/histologically normal skin (epithelium & endothelium) helped diagnose CDV, with the receptor nectin-4 co-distributed with infected cells.
  • CDV at a wildlife interface, Janos (Mexico): 62% of domestic dogs were seropositive; free-roaming owned dogs had significantly higher antibody than movement-restricted dogs, and a seropositive bobcat confirmed cross-species transmission is possible.
  • CDV lineages in Canada (wild & domestic): raccoons are important in maintaining and transmitting heterogeneous CDV lineages in Ontario; animal travel and trade can introduce novel lineages (one traced to South America).
  • CDV metaphyseal sclerosis (4 juvenile dogs): CDV infection can cause radiographic metaphyseal sclerosis in young dogs.
  • CDV renal disease (terminal patients): CDV can cause anaemia, hypergammaglobulinaemia and proteinuria; glomerular & tubular lesions were confirmed histologically in 84.6% of cases, with IgM deposition in 92.3%.
  • Prolonged CDV excretion (case report): a mixed-breed dog excreted CDV for 17 months with PCR-positive urine — CDV shedding/infectivity can be far more prolonged than usually assumed.
