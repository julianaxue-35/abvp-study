Canine Infectious Respiratory Disease Complex (CIRDC)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

A multifactorial complex of viral & bacterial respiratory pathogens; outcome relies on the interplay of host + environment + agent. High morbidity, low mortality — the classic crowded-shelter "kennel cough" that can progress to pneumonia.

Overview

  • Multifactorial complex — multiple pathogens acting together
  • Outcome relies on host + environment + agent (the classic disease triad)+
  • High morbidity

Pathogens — Viral

  • CDV (canine distemper virus) & CIV (canine influenza virus) can be involved
  • CAV-2 (canine adenovirus-2) — vaccine-protected; non-enveloped only
  • CPiV (canine parainfluenza virus) — honking cough; sheds 8–10 days; can be subclinical or a carrier despite vaccination; mucosal IgA (immunoglobulin A) is more protective
  • CRCoV (canine respiratory coronavirus) — the "lawnmower virus"; no vaccine
  • CHV (canine herpesvirus) — fatal in neonates; latent; recrudesces in stress
  • CPnV (canine pneumovirus) — related to human RSV (respiratory syncytial virus)

Pathogens — Bacterial

  • Bordetella bronchiseptica (Bb) — zoonotic; sheds weeks → months
  • Mycoplasma — co-infects with CPiV
    ⚠ Trap: do NOT use amoxicillin — Mycoplasma have no cell wall.
  • Streptococcus zooepidemicus ("Strep zoo") — ↑ mortality; haemorrhagic pneumonia; treat with penicillin; requires prophylactic treatment of ALL exposed dogs; resistant to doxycycline

Diagnosis & Testing

  • Clinical signs / PCR (polymerase chain reaction) — sample acutely, within 1–2 days of onset; do not wait until 5–7 days
  • Sample 10–30% of the population
  • Necropsy — lung tissue → histopathology
  • Tests: PCR, VI (virus isolation), serology; ancillary CBC (complete blood count), necropsy
  • Use synthetic polyester swabs in plastic shafts
  • Human influenza A test kit — positive = true positive; negative = cannot rule out

Morbidity & Mortality

  • High morbidity, low mortality
  • ~80–90% develop signs; 10–20% are subclinical shedders
  • Pneumonia fatality 5–8%
  • CIV destroys the ciliated columnar epithelial cells → secondary (2°) bacterial invasion → pneumonia

Incubation

  • 3–10 days
  • For CDV — minimum 14 days, up to 6 weeks
  • Animal from a CDV-endemic area → sheds 7–8 days

Canine Influenza Virus CIV

  • H3N8 — greyhound / dogs only
  • H3N2 — avian origin; from China / Korea; affects many other animals; highly contagious; sporadic regional outbreaks
  • Shedding: H3N8 up to 7 days (isolate); H3N2 21 → 28 days (isolate)
  • Incubation 2–5 days; peak shedding 2–4 days post-infection, overlapping incubation
  • No true carrier state

Prevention

  • Aggressive 14-day quarantine & testing, especially dogs from China / Korea
  • Suspect CIV → identify subtype:
    • H3N8 — quarantine 7 days, isolate
    • H3N2 — quarantine 21 → 28 days, isolate
  • PCR at day 4 → negative = release; day 7 → positive = isolate
  • Vaccine: killed, non-core; 2 doses 2–3 weeks apart

Sanitation

  • Don't use quaternary ammonium compounds (QUATs) — not effective for Bordetella / CAV-2
  • Alcohol handrub > 60% doesn't work on CAV-2

Housing

  • Ventilation: 10–12 air changes per hour; separate zoned HVAC (heating, ventilation & air conditioning) for isolation
  • Double-compartment housing
  • Capacity for care (C4C)
  • Droplets can travel up to 25 ft (7.6 m); keep runs a minimum of 6 m apart

📄 Journal Articles

  • Influenza A virus (IAV) in dogs & cats, central Chile: IAV was detected by PCR in 3.2% of dogs and 5.8% of cats; serology showed IAV seropositivity in 55.5% of dogs and 50% of cats.
  • Multiplex PCR (mPCR) for CIRDC pathogens: an assay targeting the H, E3 and bfrZ genes of CDV, CAV-2 and Bordetella bronchiseptica respectively — 100% specificity with no cross-reactivity, and on 55 clinical samples 94.12% sensitivity, 94.74% specificity and 94.55% overall accuracy: a rapid, specific, cost-effective single-reaction test for key CIRDC pathogens.
  • H3N2 canine influenza — 20-year evolution: in North America CIV shows a complex history — local outbreaks, regional lineage die-outs, and repeated reintroductions from Asia (2–3 from China in the past 3 years). It spreads rapidly in kennels/shelters then dies out locally, so long-distance movement sustains it; still best adapted to dense populations, not prolonged circulation.
  • H5N1 antibodies in hunting dogs (Washington): antibodies to H5 and N1 influenza A were found in 4/194 (2%) dogs that hunted or trained with wild fowl; seropositive dogs had high waterfowl exposure.
  • H5N1 (clade 2.3.4.4b) in dairy cattle & cats, USA 2024: cats developed disease after being fed raw (unpasteurised) colostrum and milk from affected dairy cows — a novel mammalian exposure route for highly pathogenic avian influenza.
  • H5N1 in cats, South Korea 2023: infection was suspected to come from improperly sterilised raw food made from domestic duck meat.
  • H5N1 pathogenicity (cat isolates) in mice & ferrets, South Korea 2023: the 2023 feline H5N1 outbreak virus caused fatal infection in mice and ferrets, showing high pathogenicity and mammalian transmission — with potential to affect humans.
  • Influenza A serology in cats near infected wild birds (Spain): seroprevalence was only 2.19% — a low level, suggesting cats do not play an important epidemiological role in influenza A transmission there.
  • Canine herpesvirus (CAHV-1) seroprevalence (N. Queensland, Australia): pooled seroprevalence 13% (11.4% Douglas, 17.1% show dogs). Dogs with conjunctivitis in the previous 3 months or involved in breeding were more likely to be seropositive; no other significant risk factors.
  • Streptococcus zooepidemicus MLST in dogs: Strep zoo has several different strains; a ST-173 strain was found in a US outbreak. It seems to thrive in environments with a high risk of transmissibility — overcrowding, stress and naïve populations, particularly in shelters/kennels. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) alone cannot determine the virulence phenotype of Strep zoo in dogs.
  • Vets' knowledge of avian influenza (UK): in a small survey (26 vets), veterinarians expressed a lack of confidence in treating birds and handling suspected highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) cases — a preparedness gap.
  • Canine pneumovirus respiratory outbreak (US shelter): the outbreak resolved within ~1 month once clean-break procedures, restriction of incoming dogs and a deep clean of the premises were adhered to.
  • Influenza A (H7N2) in a shelter cat (case report): cats arrived with no apparent signs, then developed upper-respiratory congestion progressing to severe pneumonia unresponsive to treatment, with dyspnoea, tachypnoea and collapse.
  • H5N1 (clade 2.3.4.4b) in a domestic cat, France 2022: avian influenza A can spread to cats — particularly those housed near a duck farm.
