Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

A mutation of feline coronavirus (FCoV): only a small subset of infected cats develop FIP, when the virus gains macrophage tropism → pyogranulomas & effusion. Shelter management targets capacity-for-care, length of stay & stress — not isolating affected cats.

Overview & Signalment

  • Age — mostly < 3 years (~80%), more < 2 years, ~50% < 7 months
  • Predisposing — purebred · multi-cat · foster · FeLV / FIV (feline leukaemia / immunodeficiency virus) · stress
  • FIP is a mutation of feline coronavirus (FCoV) infection
  • Shelter focus = capacity-for-care (CfC), length of stay (LOS) & stress, rather than isolating affected cats

Virology

  • RNA (ribonucleic acid) alphacoronavirus; common mutation site = the spike (S) protein gene
  • FECV (feline enteric coronavirus) — replicates in enterocytes; self-limiting diarrhoea; oro-nasal spread
    • Endemic in catteries (~87–90% seroprevalence); strays ~2%
    • No protective immunity
    • Kittens 8–16 weeks shed ~10,000× more than adults → don't mix litters
  • FIPV (feline infectious peritonitis virus) — the biotype that replicates at high levels in macrophages → pyogranulomas & effusion of FIP

Shelter Incidence outbreak

  • > 1% = an outbreak
  • Hoarding / multi-cat 5–10%
  • Breeding cattery < 10%
+ check: the cattery figure "< 10%" sits above the "> 1% = outbreak" threshold — read it as an upper bound for an endemic cattery. Confirm.

Serotypes & Pathogenesis

  • Two serotypes
    • Type I — unique to cats; dominant worldwide (80–95%)
    • Type II — less common; FCoV / CCoV (canine coronavirus) recombination
  • FIP arises when FECV mutates to gain macrophage tropism
  • Only a small subset of FCoV-infected cats develop FIP; < 5% shedders; < 3% of seropositive cats+
  • FIPV is NOT infectious by the faecal–oral route
  • Shelter outbreaks usually from housing & husbandry that drive up FCoV loads & amplifying risk factors
⚠ Clinical form = the immune response (IR):
  1. Strong humoral + weak / absent cell-mediated immunity = effusive (wet) FIP (Type IV hypersensitivity)
  2. Intermediate cell-mediated immunity = pyogranulomas (dry)
  3. Strong immunity = prevents disease

Treatment

  1. GS-441524 — 6–15 mg/kg PO (by mouth) q24h (once daily)
    • Low dose for wet / dry form
    • 8 mg/kg with ocular signs
    • 10–15 mg/kg with neurological signs
    • Continuous course 84 days (12 weeks)
    • Monitor bloods: A:G (albumin:globulin), globulin, haematocrit, weight gain
    • If relapse → retreat & ↑ by 5 mg/kg
  2. Others — GC376; remdesivir to load a sick / inappetent cat, then start

Wet vs Dry FIP compare

FeatureWET (effusive)DRY (non-effusive)
Prevalence ~14% ~28% (58% show mixed signs)
Pathogenesis Strong humoral + weak cell-mediated immunity; Type IV hypersensitivity; vasculitis Pyogranulomatous nodules; intermediate cellular immunity
Primary signs Abdominal distension; thoracic effusion (dyspnoea / ↑ respiratory rate); icterus; pallor Neurological signs (seizures, ataxia); localized organ dysfunction; mesenteric lymphadenopathy
Shared signs Waxing & waning fever, lethargy, anorexia, ocular lesions (uveitis, keratic precipitates)
Prognosis Rapidly fatal (days–weeks) More gradual (~months)
Diagnosis Non-septic exudate, yellow-straw colour, protein > 3.5 g/dL, low cellularity; A:G ratio < 0.8 (albumin:globulin) — 0.5 → positive predictive value (PPV) 89%; > 1 → negative predictive value (NPV) 91%; use the Rivalta test Biochemistry: low A:G ratio & gamma-globulin, hyperbilirubinaemia; IHC (immunohistochemistry) on tissue / necropsy; direct immunofluorescence (gold standard); pyogranulomas on serosal surfaces, around mesenteric vessels, in the renal cortex; gross vasculitis

Littermate Management

  • Higher FCoV viral-load exposure
  • Most siblings won't develop FIP
  • They share a genetic predisposition
  • Options
    • Adopt out with disclosure
    • 2–4 weeks quarantine
    • Euthanasia
    • Can incubate for years
