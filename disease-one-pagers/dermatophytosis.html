Dermatophytosis (Ringworm)

Infectious Disease · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Fungal infection of keratinised tissue (hair, nails, stratum corneum) by dermatophytes — Microsporum canis dominates in cats. Zoonotic, environmentally persistent, and a classic shelter problem in kittens, crowded / hoarding cats and long-haired breeds.

Dermatophytes agents

  • Microsporum
    • M. canis — cats, 90 %+
    • M. gypseum — more in dogs
  • Trichophyton → dogs, rodents
  • Epidermophyton

Risk Factors

  • Young < 1 year
  • Immunocompromised
  • Long-haired cats
  • Insufficient grooming
  • Friendly cats (mutual rubbing / allogrooming spreads spores)+
  • Yorkshire Terrier

Environment

  • Warm, humid · kitten season · overcrowding
  • Hoarding cases · barn-cat colonies · dirt runs

Pathogenesis

  • Spores persist for months in the environment
  • Spread by direct contact, fomites, fleas; airborne is possible
  • Spores → microtrauma → incubate 2–4 weeks
  • Recovery relies on a strong cell-mediated response
  • FeLV / FIV (feline leukaemia virus / feline immunodeficiency virus) cats may fail to clear infection despite therapy
  • Can be focal or generalized

Clinical Signs

  • Alopecia (circular) · scaling · inflammation
⚠ Trap: in dogs, classic ringworm-like lesions are more often bacterial pyoderma / demodicosis than actual ringworm.

Diagnosis

  1. History — immune status, age, environment, littermates, prior exposure
  2. Visual exam — muzzle, lips, periocular skin, ears, digits, nail beds
  3. Wood's lamp — use 365 nm (nanometres); look for apple-green fluorescence at the hair base. Only ~50 % of M. canis fluoresce.
    ⚠ Trap: a positive is a TRUE positive, but a negative does NOT rule out ringworm.
  4. Direct microscopy — pluck a fluorescing hair into mineral oil; infected hair is wide, irregular, filamentous, with cuffs or refractile ectothrix spores.
    • 10× to confirm · 40× to check for spores
  5. Fungal culture (gold standard) — incubate at 27–30 °C, hold 21 days; read on days 7, 10, 14, 21. Day 10 is the key — over 95 % of positives are identified on days 10–14.
    ⚠ Key rule: if not suspected by day 7 or positive by day 10, it is unlikely to be M. canis. Hold the CULTURES (not the animals) for the 21 days.
    • DTM (dermatophyte test medium) appearance: pathogen = pale / white colonies; the medium turns red simultaneously with growth.
    ⚠ Trap: a plate overgrown in the first 7–10 days can mask a TRUE positive → reculture.
  6. PCR (polymerase chain reaction) — high sensitivity (a false negative is unlikely); low specificity; can detect non-viable organisms.
    ⚠ Trap: must confirm all positive PCR via culture.

Culture — P-score interpretation

P-score = plate / pathogen score; number = colony count on the plate.+

  • P1 (1–4) — no lesion, negative Wood's lamp, fomite dust / mop carrier
    • Option: reculture, or lime sulfur & release
  • P2 (5–9) — focal / suspect lesion, infection, fomite
    • Option: re-examine
    • Few lesions → dip & adopt
    • Lesion? → isolate
  • P3 (10+) — confirmed infection → strict isolation

Treatment

Shelters must use a DUAL approach — systemic + topical.

  1. Systemic therapy
    • Itraconazole 5–10 mg/kg PO (by mouth) SID (once daily) for 21–28 days
    • Terbinafine 10–40 mg/kg PO SID
    ⚠ Do NOT use: griseofulvin, ketoconazole, lufenuron.
  2. Twice-weekly topical therapy — lime sulfur: 8 oz (236 mL) per gallon (3.78 L) of warm water, with the lime sulfur added first. Do not rinse or pre-wet; clip long hair; can blow-dry. + check: 8 oz per gallon (3.78 L) works out to a ~1:16 dilution; the 3.78 L confirms a US gallon (128 oz), not an imperial one. Confirm your source.

Verification of Cure

  • 3 consecutive weekly negative cultures; start monitoring after week 1
  • 2 negatives suffice ONLY if combined lime sulfur + concurrent oral itraconazole
  • Typical course ~18 days (range 10–49 days)

Sanitation

  • Bleach (sodium hypochlorite) 1:10 to 1:32, minimum 10 min contact time
  • AHP (accelerated hydrogen peroxide) 1:16, 5–10 min contact time
  • Potassium peroxymonosulfate — must be 2:1
  • Heat (50 °C) to inactivate Trichophyton spores
  • Cat trees — lime sulfur + direct sunlight; culture until 2 negatives

📄 Journal Articles

  • Topical dermatophytosis RCT (3 treatments, shelter cats): 76 cats with Microsporum canis, all on oral itraconazole, randomised to lime sulfur (LS), miconazole-chlorhexidine (MC) or hydrogen peroxide (HP) shampoo. LS cured all cats by 7 weeks vs 62.5% (MC) and 78.3% (HP); mean time to cure LS 27 d, MC 37 d, HP 36 d. Side effects: bleached hair (HP), hypothermia (MC & HP). LS was superior; HP is an acceptable alternative if preferred.
  • Adhesive tape impression (ATI) for diagnosis: in 20 animals (5 dogs, 15 cats) ATI was positive in 100% vs trichogram 90%, culture 95% and Wood's lamp 65% (ATI significantly better than Wood's lamp), and detected fungal elements more easily than trichogram. ATI had the highest ability to correctly diagnose dermatophytosis — though the sample was small.
  • PCR vs culture for dermatophyte diagnosis (shelter cats & dogs): PCR overall sensitivity 86.1%, specificity 94.8%; for Microsporum canis sens 86.4% / spec 97.8%, for Trichophyton sens 84.6% / spec 97.9%. The variability in PCR sensitivity warns against relying on PCR alone — in-house culture remains valuable.
  • Terbinafine for feline sporotrichosis — not effective: the low clinical-cure rate suggests terbinafine is not an effective option for feline sporotrichosis. (sporotrichosis, not dermatophytosis — filed here as the nearest fungal topic)
