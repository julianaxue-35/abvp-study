Animal Hoarding — Classification & Criteria

Animal hoarding is a recognised syndrome characterised by accumulation of animals beyond the capacity to provide adequate care, combined with denial of the problem and ongoing deterioration of the environment. It is distinct from simple multi-animal neglect due to its psychological dimension and extremely high recidivism.

Diagnostic criteria Patronek 1999 / Berry et al. 2005

All four criteria must be present for a diagnosis of animal hoarding:

  • 1. Accumulation of a large number of animals: no specific minimum number defines hoarding — the relevant criterion is that the number exceeds what can be adequately cared for. Studies commonly involve 5 to 500+ animals.
  • 2. Failure to provide minimum standards of nutrition, sanitation, shelter, and veterinary care: the animals suffer as a result of the accumulation — starvation, disease, overcrowding, lack of treatment for obvious illness.
  • 3. Denial of the problem: the hoarder believes the animals are being adequately cared for despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary. This is not dishonesty — it is a cognitive distortion that is central to the syndrome.
  • 4. Deteriorating environment for both animals and humans: the living environment deteriorates to an unsafe state for both animals and the human inhabitants — accumulated faeces and urine, structural damage, fire hazards, zoonotic disease exposure.
Exam trap — no minimum number defines hoardingThere is no legally or medically established minimum animal count (e.g. "more than 15" or "more than 50") that defines hoarding. The defining features are failure of care, denial, and environmental deterioration — not a specific number. Do not rely on a number threshold.

This profile is the most likely to be tested on exam questions about hoarding classification.

Psychological profile of hoarders Patronek / Frost / DSM-5

DSM-5 hoarding disorder

  • Animal hoarding is closely related to hoarding disorder (DSM-5, 2013), which is now a standalone diagnosis — no longer classified as a subtype of OCD. The key features are: persistent difficulty discarding possessions (including living animals), strong perceived need to save, and significant distress or impairment from accumulated clutter.
  • Animal hoarding specifically involves living creatures rather than objects, adding layers of welfare, public health, and legal complexity not present in object hoarding.

Comorbid conditions

  • Depression and anxiety: extremely common comorbidities. Animals often fill a role as emotional regulators for individuals with severe social anxiety and major depression.
  • PTSD and trauma history: many hoarders — particularly overwhelmed caregivers and rescue hoarders — have significant histories of early trauma, loss, or neglect. Animal acquisition may represent an attempt to gain control over loss.
  • ADHD: executive function deficits contribute to the disorganisation and inability to manage care logistics that characterise the escalation phase.
  • Personality disorders: borderline, dependent, and schizotypal features are commonly observed. Exploiter hoarders frequently exhibit antisocial and narcissistic traits.

Attachment and cognitive patterns

  • Insecure attachment: hoarders often have insecure or disorganised attachment styles. Animals provide unconditional, non-judgmental relationships that human relationships have failed to provide. Removing animals is experienced as catastrophic loss.
  • Magical thinking about death: a common cognitive pattern is the belief that hoarding animals prevents their death — that releasing an animal into any other environment is equivalent to condemning it. This is particularly prominent in rescue hoarders.
  • Cognitive denial: the inability to perceive deteriorating conditions as harmful is not deception — it is a genuine perceptual distortion. Hoarders typically believe the animals are well-cared for despite evidence visible to everyone else.
  • Social isolation: hoarding exists in a context of progressive withdrawal from human relationships. Animals become the primary social world, further entrenching the hoarding dynamic.

Demographic patterns

  • Approximately 60–70% female in published case series (differs from object hoarding which is roughly equal gender distribution).
  • Most commonly identified in individuals aged 50s–70s, though hoarding can begin much earlier.
  • Cats are the most commonly hoarded species, followed by dogs, rabbits, and birds — reflecting their availability and low acquisition barriers.
  • Living alone or with equally affected co-residents; social support networks have typically eroded by the time of investigation.
Exam context — profile informs intervention strategy Understanding the psychological profile explains why legal penalties alone fail. The hoarder is not a rational actor who responds to deterrents — they are experiencing genuine cognitive distortions about the welfare of their animals. Any effective intervention must pair legal action with mental health treatment targeting the specific disorder type.

Recidivism and psychological dimension Patronek 1999 / Berry et al. 2005

  • Recidivism rate: 70–100% without treatment targeting the underlying psychological disorder. This is one of the highest recidivism rates of any animal cruelty category.
  • Animal hoarding is now recognised in psychiatric literature as related to hoarding disorder (DSM-5), with attachment-based pathology and cognitive distortions around loss and control.
  • Legal intervention alone (fines, prohibition orders) without mandatory psychological treatment produces near-certain recidivism. Effective intervention requires mental health services, social services, and case management.
  • Hoarders who are ordered not to have animals frequently acquire them again, sometimes immediately after conviction. Prohibition orders must be actively monitored to have any effect.
70–100% recidivism — the exam numberThis statistic is the most frequently tested fact about animal hoarding. Without psychological treatment, nearly all hoarders reoffend. Know this number.

Multi-Agency Response & Shelter Impact

Human health impacts in hoarding environments Public Health Literature

  • Zoonotic disease exposure: dermatophytosis (ringworm — Microsporum canis from cats), Bartonella (cat scratch disease), Salmonella, Pasteurella multocida, Toxoplasma gondii, Cryptosporidium, and others. Ammonia from accumulated excrement causes respiratory irritation.
  • Ammonia and fecal contamination: levels of ammonia in severely hoarded homes can reach concentrations that cause mucosal irritation and respiratory damage in both animals and humans. Buildings may be uninhabitable after seizure.
  • Structural damage: urine and faeces degrade flooring, subfloor, drywall, and structural elements over time. Buildings may need partial or full demolition after long-term hoarding.
  • Human co-residents: adults in hoarding households are at risk for self-neglect; children are at risk for neglect and abuse; elderly residents are at risk for elder abuse. Public health and social service agencies must be notified.
  • Fire hazard: accumulated material (animal bedding, waste, newspapers) creates extreme fire load. Multiple fatalities (animal and human) have occurred in fires in hoarding households.

9-step ASPCA forensic investigation protocol ASPCA

The ASPCA forensic investigation protocol for hoarding and cruelty cases follows a structured sequence to ensure animal welfare, evidence integrity, and legal defensibility:

#StepKey requirements
1Pre-seizure planningObtain warrant; assemble multi-agency team; prepare supplies (cages, PPE, documentation forms, body bags); establish command structure; brief all personnel on roles
2Scene documentationPhotograph and video the entire scene before any animal is moved or environmental conditions are altered; document ambient temperature, odour (ammonia levels if measurable), structural hazards
3Environmental assessmentAssess air quality (ammonia concentration), temperature extremes, structural safety for responders; determine PPE requirements; identify hazards (biohazards, sharps, unstable floors)
4Individual animal identification and triageAssign a unique case number to each animal; conduct rapid triage (alive/dead; critical/stable); photograph each animal in situ before movement; document GPS location or room location
5Individual veterinary examinationComplete physical examination for each living animal; document body condition score, injuries, disease signs, estimated age, reproductive status; complete ASPCA body map or equivalent evidence form
6Evidence collection and preservationCollect relevant physical evidence (food supply, medications, veterinary records, deceased animals for necropsy); maintain chain of custody for all evidence; necropsy deceased animals to document cause of death
7Chain of custody documentationSign-off at each handover point; document who handled each animal, when, and where; photograph all transfers; ensure evidence forms accompany animals throughout
8Safe removal and transportTransport animals in species-appropriate enclosures; separate animals by species, size, and health status; do not mix healthy and visibly ill animals; maintain temperature control during transport
9Veterinary forensic reporting and case preparationCompile findings into a formal veterinary forensic report suitable for criminal proceedings; the attending veterinarian may be called as an expert witness; maintain records in accessible format for the duration of the case
Exam trap — scene documentation must precede animal movement Step 2 (full photographic/video documentation) must be completed before any animal is relocated, even for welfare reasons. If an animal requires emergency life-saving treatment before documentation is complete, the pre-treatment condition must be described in writing and photographed at the first opportunity. Failure to document before intervention compromises the evidentiary record.
High-yield — chain of custody is continuous and documented Chain of custody begins at the scene and continues through veterinary examination, transport, shelter intake, and storage. Any break in chain of custody weakens the prosecution's case. Every person who handles an evidence animal must be documented by name, role, and timestamp.

Shelter challenges in hoarding cases ASV Guidelines / ASPCA

  • Sheer animal numbers: hoarding cases frequently involve 30–300+ animals, overwhelming normal intake capacity. Mutual aid agreements with other shelters, rescue organisations, and veterinary schools are essential.
  • Species variety: cats are the most common hoarded species, but dogs, rabbits, birds, reptiles, and even livestock have been found in hoarding cases. Not all shelters have capacity or expertise for all species.
  • High disease prevalence: animals from hoarding environments have extremely high rates of: upper respiratory infection (cats), parvovirus exposure, dermatophytosis, extreme ectoparasite burden, malnutrition, dental disease, and untreated injuries. Infectious disease triage and isolation protocols must be robust.
  • Behavioural sequelae: animals raised in hoarding environments may have severely abnormal socialisation — either hyperattachment (never alone, over-stimulated by other animals) or fear-based behaviours (feral-like cats). Individual behavioural assessment is required.
  • Long legal hold periods: hoarding animals are evidence animals and cannot be adopted out, transferred, or euthanised without court authorisation until the criminal case resolves — which may take months to years. This creates significant financial and space pressure on shelters.
  • Cost-of-care bonds: in jurisdictions with cost-of-care bond statutes, shelters can petition courts to require hoarders to post bonds covering holding costs or forfeit the animals. This is a critical financial and legal tool in hoarding cases.
Exam trap — infectious disease containment in hoarding intakeAnimals from hoarding environments must be triaged for infectious disease before entering the general shelter population. Mass intake of potentially URD-positive cats or parvo-exposed dogs without isolation protocols can devastate a shelter population. Establish a separate intake and holding area for hoarding case animals.

Without treatment targeting the underlying psychological disorder, what is the approximate recidivism rate for animal hoarding?

D is correct. Animal hoarding has a recidivism rate of 70–100% without psychological intervention targeting the underlying disorder (Patronek 1999; Berry et al. 2005). This is one of the highest recidivism rates in any animal cruelty category. Legal intervention alone (fines, prohibition orders, animal surrender) is insufficient — mental health treatment, social services, and active monitoring of prohibition orders are all required to have any impact on reoffending.
Q2Hoarding

An individual runs a cat rescue organisation, sincerely believes she is saving cats from euthanasia, has 180 cats in her home, cannot provide adequate food or veterinary care, denies any problem, and has supporters who donate and defend her. Which hoarding type does she BEST represent?

B is correct. This is the rescue hoarder profile: a mission-driven individual who collects animals to "save" them, often with organisational structure, genuine belief in her mission, supporter networks, and denial of the problem. The presence of supporters who defend and enable the behaviour is characteristic of rescue hoarding and makes intervention legally and socially complex. The rescue hoarder type has moderate recidivism and requires psychological treatment focused on the mission-based cognition.
Q3Hoarding

Which hoarding type has the HIGHEST recidivism rate and is MOST resistant to intervention?

C is correct. The exploiter hoarder has the highest recidivism rate and the worst prognosis. Key features: lacks empathy for animals and people, charismatic and manipulative, acquires animals for personal benefit (not mission or compassion), refuses all intervention, and is the most likely to immediately reoffend after legal action. This type requires both legal intervention (prohibition orders, active monitoring) and recognition that psychological treatment is typically rejected.
Q4Hoarding

Which of the following is a diagnostic criterion for animal hoarding?

B is correct. Animal hoarding is defined by four criteria: (1) large number of animals, (2) failure to provide minimum standards of care, (3) denial of the problem, and (4) deteriorating environment for animals and humans. There is NO specific number threshold (not 15, not 50, not any other number). The diagnostic criteria are about failure of care and denial, not a count. Multi-species ownership is common in normal multi-pet households and is not hoarding.
Q5Hoarding

A large hoarding seizure involves 120 cats with high suspected rates of upper respiratory disease. What is the MOST important intake consideration specific to this population?

B is correct. Animals from hoarding environments have very high rates of infectious disease. Integrating them with the general shelter population without isolation protocols risks a shelter-wide epidemic. Establish a separate intake area with dedicated equipment and staff. Do not use modified live vaccines in actively ill or stressed animals (immunocompromised state may allow vaccine strain disease). Triage — treat treatable animals, euthanise only those for whom euthanasia is the most humane option after individual assessment.
Q6Hoarding

Which agency is MOST essential to engage in a hoarding case to address the underlying cause and reduce recidivism?

C is correct. The defining feature of hoarding that distinguishes it from simple neglect is the underlying psychological disorder. Without mental health intervention targeting this disorder, recidivism is 70–100% regardless of criminal penalties, property condemnation, or animal removal. Mental health services are the most essential non-animal-welfare agency in any hoarding response. All other agencies are important but do not address the root cause.
Q7Hoarding

Which zoonotic disease is MOST commonly associated with hoarding of cats?

A is correct. Dermatophytosis (ringworm, caused primarily by Microsporum canis in cats) is extremely common in cat hoarding environments and readily transmits to humans (and shelter staff handling seized animals). Bartonella (cat scratch disease) is also common. Rabies is a concern for unvaccinated animals but is not the most common zoonosis in hoarding environments. Brucellosis is primarily a dog/ruminant disease. Leptospirosis is more associated with dogs and outdoor environments.
Q8Hoarding

A cost-of-care bond statute is MOST useful in a hoarding case because it:

B is correct. Hoarding cases frequently involve very long criminal proceedings (months to years) during which dozens to hundreds of animals are held as evidence at the shelter's expense. Cost-of-care bond statutes require the owner to post periodic bonds covering the daily care cost; failure to post results in forfeiture of the animals, allowing the shelter to proceed with adoption or other disposition. This is a critical legal tool for shelters managing expensive, prolonged hoarding holds.
Q9Hoarding

In DSM-5, hoarding disorder is classified as:

B is correct. In DSM-5 (2013), hoarding disorder is a standalone diagnosis — it was removed from the OCD spectrum where it previously existed as a specifier. The core features are: persistent difficulty discarding possessions regardless of value, perceived need to save, and significant distress or functional impairment. Animal hoarding is an expression of hoarding disorder applied to living animals, adding animal welfare, public health, and legal dimensions not present in object hoarding.
Q10Hoarding

According to the ASPCA 9-step forensic investigation protocol, which step MUST be completed before any animal is physically moved from its location?

B is correct. Scene documentation (Step 2) must precede any animal movement to preserve the evidentiary record. Photographs and video capture the conditions as found — animal locations, environmental state, degree of ammonia contamination, structural hazards — before any human intervention alters the scene. If an animal requires emergency life-saving treatment before documentation is complete, the pre-treatment condition must be recorded in writing and photographed at the first available opportunity. Failure to document prior to movement can weaken or destroy the prosecution's case.
Q11Hoarding

Which psychological feature best explains why removing animals from a hoarder — even with full criminal conviction — consistently fails to prevent reoffending without additional intervention?

B is correct. The cognitive distortion at the core of hoarding disorder means the hoarder does not experience prosecution and animal removal as consequences of harmful behaviour — they experience it as persecution of someone who is protecting animals. Without mental health intervention targeting the hoarding disorder, the attachment pathology, and the cognitive denial, the motivation to acquire animals is unchanged. This is why 70–100% of animal hoarders reoffend without psychological treatment, regardless of the severity of criminal penalties imposed.

A Retrospective, Descriptive Study Of Medical Conditions and Outcomes of Cats Relinquished to an Urban, Limited Admission Shelter from Hoarding Environments
A Retrospective, Descriptive Study Of Medical Conditions · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. Introduction: Animal hoarding is a complex, often underrecognized public health problem affecting the health and welfare of humans and animals. Limited peer review literature exists on the conditions, outcomes, and resources needed to care for cats from hoarding environments in shelter settings. This study investigated intake medical conditions and outcomes of cats surrendered from hoarding environments to the New York City sheltering programs of the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (ASPCA). The objectives of this study were to (1) describe the prevalence of medical conditions at intake; (2) identify associations between medical conditions and outcomes; (3) summarize key interventions provided prior to outcome; and (4) compare outcomes of cats surrendered from hoarding environments to other owner/guardian surrendered (OGS) cats. Methods: This retrospective, descriptive study examined case records of cats voluntarily surrendered from hoarding cases to ASPCA sheltering programs between January 1, 2021, and July 31, 2023. Demographic, medical, and outcome data were collected on 613 cats relinquished in 34 case groups. Outcome data were compared to 775 non-hoarded OGS cats in-shelter during the same time period. Results: Only 27.4% of cats were already altered on intake. Dental disease was the most common medical condition (52.8%); 20.3% had moderate to severe disease requiring dentistry procedures. On intake, cats also had otitis externa (33.3%), Upper respiratory infection (URI) (23.8%), ocular disease (21.9%), dermatitis (19.9%), ectoparasites (17.6%), diarrhea (14.2%), dermatophytosis (10.1%), and matting/unkempt fur (4.1%). Once analyzed for interactions, body condition score (BCS) 1–2 (emaciation) at intake (P < 0.001), moderate to severe dental disease (P = 0.007), and increased number of medical conditions per cat (P < 0.001) were associated with non-live outcomes. Over half of cats (57.8%) went to foster homes. Most (63.1%) required antibiotics, and a third (36.4%) received psychopharmaceuticals. Difference in median length of stay (LOS) between hoarded and non-hoarded OGS cats was significant (52 days vs. 28 days; P < 0.001). Proportions of live outcomes for hoarded (89.2%) and nonhoarded cats (88.3%) did not significantly differ (P = 0.6). When euthanasia was the outcome, OGS cats were more often euthanized for medical conditions (64.8%) compared to hoarded cats (45.9%). Behavioral euthanasia rates were significantly higher for hoarded than OGS cats (47.5% vs. 25%; P = 0.017). Conclusion: The significant need in hoarded cat populations for dentistry and spay/neuter procedures and the significantly longer LOS of hoarded cats suggests organizations working with these populations need to proactively strategize to optimize care delivery without negatively impacting shelter capacity for care. Harm reduction approaches to hoarding employ a collaborative, staged means to reduce populations in the home, including offering surrender of some animals and the provision of spay/neuter and/or other services for remaining animals. This approach, in addition to improving animal welfare, enables the shelter to better manage capacity and resources. A Retrospective Study of Cat Hoarding Cases and Their Management Through Voluntary Spay Neuter and Relinquishment In New York City
A Retrospective Study of Cat Hoarding Cases and Their · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2024

Abstract. Introduction: Animal hoarding cases are complex sources of shelter intake. Cases require significant planning, collaboration, and resources, often from multiple responding organizations. Study objectives included description of cat hoarding cases in New York City not criminally pursued, case outcomes when managed by a spay/neuter and relinquishment program utilizing a collaborative approach with caregivers, and characteristics or interventions leading to better outcomes. Methods: Data were extracted from the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animal’s Community Engagement (CE) case files. Eligible cases were retrieved using a keyword search and screened for inclusion criteria. Client demographics, case factors, and outcomes were described with descriptive statistics. Data were further analyzed using adjusted logistic regression models to investigate variables predictive of outcomes. Results: The study population included 79 cases with a median population size of 22 cats. The majority of clients were female, lived alone, and had high levels of social vulnerability. Object hoarding was reported in 29.1% of cases and unsanitary conditions in 68.4% of cases. Almost one-third (30.4%) identified as rescuers or community cat caregivers. At the time of the first interaction with CE, 88.6% of clients were interested in spay/neuter and 76.0% in surrender services. Social service agencies were involved in the initial CE intervention in 26.4% of cases. Successful outcomes were defined as cases in which clients were left with a manageable population of cats, or all cats were removed. Successful outcomes were achieved in 67.1% of cases after the first CE intervention. Recidivism occurred in 41.5% of these cases. Clients who showed an initial interest in surrender were 10 times more likely (OR 10.0, P = 0.014) to experience a successful outcome than clients not interested in surrender. Significant predictors of re-collection included clients identifying as rescuers (OR 4.8, P = 0.039) and the involvement of human service agencies during or after the CE intervention (OR 6.1, P = 0.041). Conclusion: Results demonstrate animal hoarding cases involving cooperative caregivers and rescuers can be successfully managed by programs using a collaborative approach with clients. The high rates of initial interest in spay/neuter and surrender in caregivers suggest a need to expand access to veterinary care and strategically manage case intake into shelters. Further interdisciplinary research is needed on how mental health, social service, and animal service providers can attain successful outcomes long-term and reduce recidivism in animal hoarding cases.
