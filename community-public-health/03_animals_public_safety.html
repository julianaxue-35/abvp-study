Community Cat Definitions

Understanding the vocabulary is essential — "free-roaming," "stray," and "feral" are not interchangeable, and the distinction between stray and feral is based on socialization level, not origin.

Speed

The umbrella: free-roaming cats ONENOTE

  • Free-roaming cats is the broadest term — encompassing owned cats allowed outdoor access, stray cats (escaped, lost, or abandoned), and feral cats.
  • Stray cats were once socialized to humans but became lost, abandoned, or escaped.
  • Feral cats are untamed and not socialized to humans. They may live in varying degrees of dependence on humans (e.g., colony feeders) but are defined by their socialization level, not by whether they were born outdoors.
  • This stray vs. feral distinction is not consistently used in published literature.
  • ~15% of cats presented for ovariohysterectomy in several studies were feral.

Habitat and ranging behaviour ONENOTE

  • Female cats have smaller home ranges than males.
  • Cats fed by humans show smaller home ranges — closer to those of owned pet cats.
  • Food and resource availability affects cat density and dispersal patterns within an area.
  • Human behaviour (especially abandonment) is the primary driver of free-roaming population growth.
Exam Trap: Feral ≠ born outdoors Feral cats are defined by lack of socialization to humans, not by birth location. A kitten born outdoors can become socialized with early handling; an adult owned cat abandoned outdoors retains socialization. The distinction directly affects outcome decisions (TNR vs. socialization attempts for adoption).

Population Dynamics

Feline reproductive potential is high. The ≥70% sterilization threshold is one of the most frequently tested board facts in this subdomain.

Reproductive statistics ONENOTE

  • Cats are seasonally polyestrous.
  • 1.4 litters per year; 4–5 fetuses per pregnancy; approximately 3 live births per litter.
  • An unspayed female can produce 50–100 kittens in a lifetime.
  • Population growth rates as high as 150% reported despite kitten mortality of 50–75% for kittens under 6 months.
  • Increasing food availability → more frequent births, larger litters, higher kitten survival rates.
Board-tested threshold — population medicine
TNR requires ≥70% sterilization of intact females
Must be sustained within a geographically locked cohort to outpace feline fecundity (1.4 litters/year, ~3 live births/litter). Applies specifically to the intact female population, not total colony size. Below this threshold, the population continues to grow despite the TNR program.
Correction: ≥70% threshold not in your ONENOTE notes Your notes describe reproductive rates and management options but do not explicitly state the ≥70% sterilization threshold for population stabilization. This is a high-yield board fact from population medicine modeling that is directly tested. Anything below 70% sterilization of intact females fails to outpace feline reproductive capacity.

Pre-trapping acclimatization Position Statement

  • Place food inside unsecured (unset) traps for 2–7 days before formal trapping begins.
  • Simultaneously withhold ALL external food sources in the colony area.
  • Goal: cats become comfortable entering traps; trapping efficiency improves; trap-shyness (especially in females) is reduced.
Exam Trap: Acclimatization ≠ starvation The protocol places food inside unset traps while withholding food from other sources — directing the cats' food motivation toward the trap. Withholding all food and water for 14 days is inhumane and not recommended.

Post-return outcomes Literature

  • Confirmed death rate post-return to capture site: approximately 6% in two separate studies.
  • Cats that survive to adulthood are expected to do well for several years or longer.
  • Human feeding is associated with improved survival and longer lifespans.
  • Sterilization improves health: reduces pyometra, mammary tumours, and fighting-related disease (FIV).

Management Strategies

A spectrum of approaches exists for managing free-roaming cat populations. The ABVP exam tests knowledge of evidence, limitations, and appropriate contexts for each.

Why management is needed: nuisance behaviours and community concerns ONENOTE Position Statement

Nuisance behaviours

  • Yowling and fighting: intact and hormonally active cats (including TVHR cats — see TVHR tab) engage in loud mating vocalisation and territorial fighting, particularly at night. Fighting injuries and noise are the most frequent community complaints.
  • Urine marking and spraying: intact male cats produce highly concentrated, pungent urine used to mark territory. A single intact male can spray dozens of times per night. This is a primary driver of resident complaints about free-roaming cats near homes, cars, and building entrances.
  • Property damage: scratching cars and garden furniture, digging in flowerbeds, defecating in vegetable gardens and children's sandboxes, fouling patios and driveways.
  • Predation on pets: free-roaming cats may prey on or injure small owned pets (rabbits, guinea pigs, birds) in residential gardens.

Public and community health concerns

  • Toxoplasma oocysts in soil and sandboxes: cats are the only definitive host for Toxoplasma gondii; free-roaming cats defecating in playgrounds and gardens are the source of environmentally persistent oocysts. Risk to pregnant women and children with pica is a legitimate public health concern used to justify management programs.
  • Ringworm (Microsporum canis): free-roaming cats are a significant community source of ringworm, transmissible by direct contact to children and immunocompromised individuals. Spores persist on surfaces and in soil for months.
  • Flea burden: unmanaged feral cat colonies can sustain large flea populations, with associated risks for Bartonella transmission, Dipylidium caninum (flea tapeworm), and flea allergy dermatitis in neighbouring owned pets. Flea control at TNR reduces this risk.
  • Bite and scratch risk: stray and feral cats may bite or scratch when cornered or handled by the public; Pasteurella multocida and Bartonella henselae are the primary bite-wound pathogens. Unsocialised cats should not be handled by the public.

Ecological and wildlife concerns

  • Predation on wildlife: free-roaming cats are opportunistic hunters and are documented predators of birds, small mammals, reptiles, and amphibians. Hungry or unfed cats hunt more; regularly fed colony members spend less than 1% of their time hunting wildlife.
  • Complexity of removal: the mesopredator release effect complicates simple predation arguments — island studies have documented worse net wildlife outcomes following cat removal when rodent populations (the next-level predator) expanded. Management context matters.
  • Stakeholder polarisation: cat colony caretakers and bird conservation professionals represent the most polarised stakeholder groups in community cat management. Effective programmes require acknowledging both perspectives and building coalitions rather than dismissing either side.
Exam context — this is what drives demand for management The ABVP exam tests WHY management programs exist, not just what they involve. Community concerns about nuisance, disease, and wildlife predation are the drivers of public demand for intervention. TNR addresses the primary complaints (sterilisation eliminates yowling and spraying; vaccination addresses zoonotic concerns) — which is why it has higher public acceptance than lethal control.

Exam Trap: The Vacuum Effect Removing cats from an area without preventing immigration and reproduction causes rapid repopulation by surrounding cats moving into the vacated territory. TRE programs that are not sustained intensively fail due to this effect. TNR reduces immigration by maintaining sterilized residents that defend territories.

TNR vs TVHR

TVHR is frequently tested as a foil to TNR. The key distinction: TVHR prevents reproduction while preserving gonadal hormone production, with significant real-world disadvantages.

What TVHR involves ONENOTE

  • Males: vasectomy — reproductive ducts severed, testes retained, testosterone produced normally.
  • Females: hysterectomy — uterus removed, ovaries retained, oestrogen/progesterone produced normally.
  • Result: infertility without gonadal hormone suppression.

Exam Trap: "TVHR is more humane than TNR" False. TVHR preserves yowling, fighting, and urine-spraying, along with ongoing FIV/FeLV and mammary tumour risk. Standard gonadectomy (TNR) reduces all of these. The only thing TVHR and TNR share is infertility.

Welfare, Legal & Public Perception

Welfare is assessed using a validated visual scale. Legal frameworks vary widely. Public opinion strongly favours TNR over euthanasia across all published surveys.

Welfare assessment ONENOTE

Visual welfare scale — four parameters

  • Body condition score (BCS)
  • Coat condition (matting, alopecia, soiling)
  • Nasal and/or ocular discharge; ear crusting
  • Injuries (wounds, lameness, missing eye/tail/limb)

Key finding: fewer clean feeding stations → more thin/apathetic cats. Sterilized adults are more likely to be obese and less likely to have skin lesions than intact adults, but more likely to carry a permanent disability — reflecting survival bias in colonies with sterilization programs.

Disease prevalence — zoonotic risk Literature

  • Infectious disease and parasitic infection rates in free-roaming cats are similar to owned cats — they do not represent greater public health reservoirs.
  • Toxoplasma: cats shed oocysts briefly post-initial infection. Risk in free-roaming cats is similar to owned cats. Human infection mostly from unwashed produce or undercooked meat.
  • Rabies: cats are the most frequently reported domestic animals to test positive in the US. NASPHV guideline: vaccinate all TNR cats with 3-year rabies vaccine; re-trap at 1 year then every 3 years. Even a single immunization provides significant public health benefit — feral cats mount adequate antibody after one injection.

Legal considerations ONENOTE

  • Laws vary significantly between states, counties, and municipalities — no uniform framework.
  • Some jurisdictions protect free-roaming cats under anti-cruelty laws; others do not.
  • Key local law questions: feeding prohibitions in public parks; "caretaker" definitions and associated liability; available grants; permitted management methods in the jurisdiction.
  • Veterinarians and caretakers participating in TNR may face liability under wildlife protection laws in some jurisdictions.
  • All involved in TNR should be familiar with state and local laws before beginning a program.

Public perception Literature

  • Studies consistently show high attachment between colony caretakers and cats they care for, even without physical contact.
  • Majority of surveyed public favour TNR programs.
  • Euthanasia is consistently identified as the LEAST acceptable option for community cat management.
  • Overwhelming majority prefer leaving free-roaming cats outdoors over capture and euthanasia — even knowing a cat could die of traumatic injury within 2 years.
  • Significant polarisation between bird conservation professionals and cat colony caretakers.
  • No management strategy — lethal or non-lethal — succeeds at large scale without public cooperation and stakeholder inclusion.
Predation and ecology Cats are opportunistic hunters. Managed colonies whose members are well-fed spend less than 1% of their time hunting wildlife. Documented cases exist where island cat removal actually worsened native species outcomes by eliminating apex predators and allowing mesopredator (rat) populations to increase, causing higher net predation on vulnerable bird species.

Dog Bites & Public Safety

Dog bites are a significant public health concern and carry specific shelter documentation and disclosure obligations. The ABVP exam tests epidemiology, risk factors, shelter protocols, BSL, and the 10-day rabies rule.

Epidemiology CDC / Literature

  • Approximately 4.5 million dog bites per year in the United States.
  • Approximately 800,000 require medical attention; about half of these are children.
  • 30–50 fatalities per year attributable to dog bites; children and the elderly are most often affected.
  • Children under 5 and the elderly are at highest risk of serious injury or death.
  • Most bites occur with familiar dogs known to the victim — not random stranger attacks.

Risk factors ASV Guidelines

Animal risk factors

  • Intact males — significantly higher bite rate than neutered males
  • Resource guarding behaviour
  • Fear-based aggression
  • Pain-induced aggression
  • Predatory behaviour (especially toward small children)
  • Prior bite history

Human and situational risk factors

  • Children under 5 (lack of ability to read warning signals; face-level proximity)
  • Elderly (slower reflexes; reduced physical resilience)
  • Unfamiliar dogs encountered in unfamiliar contexts
  • Approaching a dog that is eating, sleeping, or caring for puppies
  • Running or making sudden movements around dogs

Shelter obligations — documentation & disclosure ASV Guidelines

  • All known bite incidents must be documented in hardcopy or electronic form and maintained in the animal's record.
  • This documentation must be provided to adopters, foster carers, and transfer partners — this is a non-negotiable disclosure obligation.
  • The public must be prevented from having contact with animals that pose a high bite risk — clearly mark and restrict access to housing areas for these animals.
  • Before any live outcome for an animal with a history of mild to moderate aggressive behaviour, a careful, in-depth risk assessment must be conducted.
  • Training in animal body language and safe handling reduces — but does not eliminate — bite risk for staff.
Exam Trap: Disclosure is mandatory An animal with a documented bite history cannot be placed without disclosing that history to adopters, fosters, and transfer partners. Failure to disclose creates significant legal liability for the shelter. Documentation must be in writing — verbal disclosure alone is insufficient.

Bite wound management ONENOTE

  • Immediately wash the wound with soap, preferably an antiseptic with virucidal activity (e.g., povidone-iodine) and water.
  • Irrigate under pressure: 20 mL syringe + 18G needle with saline.
  • Cover the wound and seek medical attention promptly.
  • Elevate the hand if it is injured.
  • All bites that break the skin carry infection risk — immediate washing reduces this risk.
  • Dog bites: more common; common pathogens = Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Pasteurella.
  • Cat bites: less common but more likely to become infected — deep puncture wounds + Pasteurella = high risk.

The 10-day rabies observation rule ASV / NASPHV

  • Any dog, cat, or ferret that bites a human must be observed for 10 days, regardless of vaccination status.
  • At intake, shelter staff must ask whether the animal has bitten anyone within the past 10 days.
  • Aggression may be a symptom of rabies — biting animals must be managed per state/local regulations.
  • If the animal dies for any reason within the 10-day window → rabies testing is mandated; head must be cooled, not frozen, and shipped to an approved laboratory.
  • Personnel who routinely handle animals should receive pre-exposure rabies vaccination (PrEP).
  • Local public health authorities can be contacted for guidance on management of biting animals of other species.

Exam context — Level 3 vs Level 4 is the critical threshold The distinction between Level 3 (no puncture exceeds half the canine tooth depth) and Level 4 (at least one puncture exceeds half the canine tooth depth) is the most board-tested Dunbar scale distinction. Level 4 indicates the dog applied sustained bite pressure and did not self-inhibit — a qualitatively different risk profile.
Exam Trap: Disclosure applies regardless of Dunbar level Any documented bite history — even a Level 1 snap without contact — must be disclosed to adopters, foster carers, and transfer partners in writing. The Dunbar scale informs risk assessment and placement decisions; it does not create an exemption from disclosure obligations.

Dangerous dog law & Breed-Specific Legislation (BSL) AVMA Position

  • Dangerous dog laws: based on individual animal behaviour history — ban or restrict dogs based on documented dangerous behaviour regardless of breed. Generally considered more effective and equitable than BSL.
  • Breed-Specific Legislation (BSL): prohibits or restricts specific breeds (often pit bull-type dogs, Rottweilers, German Shepherds) regardless of individual temperament or behaviour.
  • AVMA position: BSL is NOT effective at reducing bite incidence. Does not account for individual dog behaviour, owner responsibility, or victim circumstances.
  • BSL creates significant operational challenges for shelters: animals may be legally euthanized solely based on breed/appearance, not temperament.
  • Evidence-based alternatives to BSL: owner education, bite prevention programs, enforcement of existing dangerous dog laws, responsible ownership ordinances.
AVMA: BSL is ineffective and not supported The American Veterinary Medical Association, along with the ASV and most major veterinary organisations, opposes BSL as it is not evidence-based for reducing dog bite incidence. The ABVP exam tests the AVMA position — know that BSL is opposed by professional organisations and that individual behaviour-based assessment is the preferred approach.

A Case of Dog Bite Identification Using Trace DNA Recovered from Clothing Without Apparent Bite Marks
Reina Ueda, Yuko Kihara, Aki Tanaka · Animals (MDPI) · 2025

Abstract. Dog bite incidents represent a significant public health concern, and DNA analysis, in addition to morphological comparison, has been recognized as a useful tool for identifying the offending dog. STR typing has been established as a reliable method for individual identification in dogs, with many successful applications reported. However, most previous studies have analyzed samples showing visible traces such as blood or saliva, and there have been no documented cases in which the offending dog was identified from clothing without apparent evidence. In the present study, STR analysis was performed on trace DNA extracted from the clothing of a victim who sustained a minor dog bite injury. The STR profile obtained from the sample completely matched that of one of the three suspected dogs, a Weimaraner, at all 19 loci examined. No visible saliva or blood contamination was observed on the clothing. This case demonstrates that even trace DNA from clothing without visible markings can yield a complete STR profile when appropriate sampling and analytical methods are applied. The findings highlight the potential of canine DNA forensics to contribute to animal-related investigations and underscore the importance of accumulating allele frequency data and standardizing analytical procedures for future applications.

Fatal Dog Attacks in Italy (2009–2025): The Urgent Need for a National Risk Registry
Iarussi F, Sessa F, Piccirillo S, Francaviglia M, Recchia A, Colella A, Bolcato M, Salerno M, Peli A, Pomara C · Animals · 2025 · 10.3390/ani15243523

Abstract. Fatal dog attacks are rare but devastating events with significant public health, forensic, and legal implications. Italy lacks a centralized registry for such incidents, limiting the ability to monitor trends and implement preventive strategies. This retrospective study analyzes all verified fatal dog attacks in Italy from 2009 to 2025. Data were collected from national and regional media, cross-verified, and organized into a comprehensive database. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, logistic and Poisson regressions, and interaction analyses were performed to identify patterns in victim demographics, breed involvement, ownership status, and environmental context. A total of 54 fatal attacks were recorded, with an increasing trend observed in the last five years. Elderly individuals (≥65) and preschool-aged children (≤4) were the most affected groups. Molosser and bull-type breeds were implicated in 69% of cases, and 92.6% of attacks involved owned dogs—more than half belonging to the victim. Private settings accounted for 66.7% of incidents. Comparative analysis with U.S. data revealed similar demographic and breed-related patterns, but also highlighted Italy's lack of a centralized behavioral risk registry. Fatal dog attacks in Italy follow recurring and preventable patterns. The absence of a national database severely limits surveillance and intervention. A centralized behavioral risk registry, modeled on international systems, should be established to support early detection, policy development, and multidisciplinary collaboration.

Living on the edge: Detections and activity pattern of wildcats (Felis silvestris) and domestic cats (Felis catus) by camera trapping in areas of low wildcat population density
Gruber Á, Cserkész T, Kiss C, Lanszki J · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106472

Abstract. The European wildcat (Felis silvestris) is one of the most endangered European mammal species and it is strictly protected in Hungary. Its population status was previously unknown in the western part of the country, in the region bordering Austria and Slovenia, which may be the peripheral distribution area of wildcats and where it is sympatric with the frequently occurring free-ranging domestic cats (Felis catus). We aimed to detect the presence of wildcats and domestic cats by camera trapping to determine the ratio of the two species and analyse the differences between their diel activity patterns in an area where wildcats were considered rare. We conducted the survey over 31 months, mainly in natural forests and surrounding areas, using camera traps and scent lures at 47 locations. We analysed data from 14,065 cumulative camera-trap days. The two species (and their suspected hybrids) were identified on the basis of their pelage characteristics. Wildcats were photographed at 55.3 % of the locations, and domestic cats at 46.8 %. The presence of both species overlapped at 42.3 % of wildcat locations. We identified 47 individuals with wildcat traits (including at least 20 hybrids) and 45 domestic cats. Wildcat abundance was low and varied significantly across areas. The mean number of unique detections per 100 camera-trap days was 0.77 for wildcats and 1.68 for domestic cats. Both species showed mainly nocturnal activity, with a significant difference in activity patterns observed only in spring. Their behavioural response to attractants differed; after scent-baiting, the detection frequency of wildcats increased slowly before decreasing again, while the domestic cat had a fast increase in the attractant effect at first, which gradually decreased. We could detect the presence of individuals with convincing wildcat phenotype outside their previously known range across large areas far from each other. We collected evidence of frequent invasive domestic cat occurrences inside wildcat habitats. Also, we recorded the activity patterns of the two species in their shared natural habitat with cameras. The knowledge gained from the research can be used to establish a conservation action plan for the wildcat and its habitat.

New Zealanders' attitudes to outdoor access for owned domestic cats
Guinnefollau L, Chung HJ, Moody C, Zobel G, Waran N, Railton R · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106817

Abstract. This study investigates domestic cat management practices in New Zealand, focusing on attitudes toward free-roaming (e.g., unrestricted outdoor access) among cat owners and non-owners. Most owned cats in New Zealand have some form of outdoor access, a debated topic centered around wildlife conservation and cat safety. Understanding factors influencing owner views about free-roaming is crucial for policy development. We distributed an online questionnaire to New Zealand residents (n = 1846) assessing attitudes toward cat free-roaming, current management, and the factors that predict both. Cat owners (82.2 %) were more likely to indicate cats should be allowed to free-roam (P < 0.0001) and that free-roaming is necessary for cats to have a good life (P < 0.0001), compared to non-cat owners. Younger respondents were more likely to favour restricting outdoor access compared to older age groups (P < 0.05). Urban respondents favoured restricting outdoor access compared to suburban or rural-based respondents (P < 0.05). Among cat owners, those providing vertical space and/or scratching areas were less likely to support free-roaming (P < 0.05) compared to those not providing these items. Conversely, owners not providing litter boxes were more likely to support free-roaming (P < 0.05). Owners interacting more than once daily with their cat through play were less likely to believe that free-roaming was necessary and more likely to restrict outdoor access. These results identify specific owner characteristics and care practices associated with attitudes toward indoor and outdoor cat management; these insights can guide stakeholders developing cat welfare policies and educational programs.

Social Inequalities in Dog Bites and Strikes in Scotland: Evidence from Administrative Health Records and Implications for Prevention Policy
Hooper J, Buchanan-Smith HM, Robertson T, Lambert P · Animals · 2025 · 10.3390/ani15131971

Abstract. This study analysed Scottish administrative health records - NHS 24 telephone calls, accident and emergency attendances, and hospital admissions - covering 2007-2019, comprising 59,111 injury records from 48,599 individuals involving dogs. Injury rates rose over the period, partly reflecting improved reporting rather than solely more incidents. Strong social patterning was evident: younger people were more often involved, and incidents occurred more frequently in areas of higher deprivation. The authors argue that dog-bite prevention policy would benefit from addressing social inequalities in risk, as is done for other public-health inequalities.

Animal Control and Field Services Officers’ Perspectives on Community Engagement: A Qualitative Phenomenology Study
Moss · Animals · 2023

Abstract. Very little is known about the prevalence, scope, and methods of community engagement employed by animal control and field services officers to address the issue of animal cruelty and neglect. This study used a phenomenological approach to understand how officers defined community engagement. The researchers conducted semi-structured interviews with twenty-nine animal control and field services officers. The definitions of community engagement varied greatly across this sample of U.S. officers. However, most officers agreed that strategies such as relationship-building, providing assistance or information, and allowing time for compliance were among the most effective community engagement strategies. In addition, several barriers to incorporating community engagement strategies in the work of animal control professionals were identified. Future research and policymaking should seek to establish a consistent definition of community engagement in animal control and field services that can then be optimized for specific communities through rigorous evaluation.

Clinical and epidemiologic features of persons accessing emergency departments for dog and cat bite injuries in California (2005–2019)
Campagna · JAVMA · 2023

Abstract. OBJECTIVE To describe the clinical and epidemiologic features of persons with dog and cat bite injuries who presented to emergency departments.

SAMPLE Records of 648,492 dog and cat bite–related emergency department visits in California from 2005 to 2019.

PROCEDURES Visits were selected by standardized International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes that indicated a bite as an external cause of injury in the medical record. Incidence rates were calculated for patient demographics, location and month of bite incident, characteristics of bite injury, infection, patient outcome/disposition, and expected source of payment. Cross-sectional descriptive analysis was performed.

RESULTS The average annual incidence of dog bites was highest in children aged < 10 years and males, while that of cat bites was highest in adults aged ≥ 80 years and females. Bites were more likely to occur in rural settings, in private residences, and during the summer. The median household income for zip codes in which animal bite patients resided was lower than the statewide median household income. Both dog and cat bite injuries were more likely to occur to upper limbs. Bacteria were isolated from 3% of dog bite injuries and 21.5% of cat bite injuries at initial presentation.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE Epidemiologic findings about persons presenting to emergency departments for animal bite injuries can inform bite prevention efforts by identifying at-risk populations. Effective animal bite prevention demands an ongoing multisectoral program of veterinarians and other health professionals, collaborating with community and governmental organizations, to develop and implement integrated strategies within the context of other socially contributory factors.

Factors regarding the dog owner’s household situation, antisocial behaviours, animal views and animal treatment in a population of dogs confiscated after biting humans and/ or other animals
van Herwijnen · PLOS ONE · 2023

Abstract. To examine the dog ownership factors characteristic to a population of dogs confiscated after a human and/ or animal-directed biting incident, we compared bite risk assessment reports of 159 confiscated dogs in the time frame 2008, 2009, 2010 (tf1) and of 215 confiscated dogs in the time frame 2020, 2021, 2022 (until mid-May; tf2). The reports were compiled by the same institute in a standardized format. We studied frequencies and chi-square pairwise comparisons (P<0.05) for 30 identified ownership factors. Overall (tf1 and tf2), 1,308 ownership factors were mentioned in the reports and reports mentioning ≥5 factors were twice as frequent in tf2 (38%) than tf1 (16%). Our data suggest that nine factors may in particular serve as a warning signal for biting incidents, as these factors were most frequently (≥15%) prevalent in the total of reported cases: having a multiple dog household, a dog reportedly roaming a neighbourhood without an owner, a dog’s care tasks being transferred, a short leash and muzzle obligation served to the owner for a dog, an isolated and/ or confined keeping of a dog, a dog owner’s (suspected) substance abuse, a dog owner’s (suspected) animal abuse, a dog owner aggressing at confiscation of the dog and a dog owner being reported on for antisocial behaviours such as intimidation. Particularly, a dog owner’s aggressive or antisocial behaviours and previous obligations to muzzle and short leash a dog (in our dataset often inappropriately adhered to by owners), may indicate that a proportion of owners of confiscated dogs, may not always be willing and/ or capable to guarantee societal safety. The results show that identification of dog ownership factors, might be useful for establishing biting incident policies and further studies should be done on preventive measures and controls.

Dog bites in Portugal: Analysis of incidents, and consequences for both people and dogs
Fragoso · J of Vet Behavior · 2022

Abstract. Dog bites are a public health problem worldwide, and Portugal is not an exception. Thus, it is fundamental to develop effective prevention programs and interventions strategies using evidence-based information. In the present study, we investigated single dog bite incidents in Portugal retrospectively through an online survey. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study analyzing dog bite consequences for both people and dogs at a national level in Portugal. A total of 729 valid responses of bite incidents were received. The characteristics of the people, dogs, and consequences of the incidents for both were analyzed. The mean age of the people was 28.2 years (range: 9 months-89 years), and 78.1% of the people were female. The majority of dogs involved were male (71.1%), and were familiar to the person (78.7%). More than half of the bites were on the upper extremities (58.1%). In 78.3% of the incidents, the bites did not require medical intervention. Among the incidents requiring medical care, 18.6% of the incidents required surgical intervention. Single bites were more frequent (87.2%). While 75% of the single bite incidents resulted in minor injury, this number was reduced to 61% for multiple bite incidents. Children who are under 12 years old had a relatively higher number of head injuries (22.3%) than the other age groups. While in 58.3% of the incidents, nothing happened to the dog as a consequence, 11 dogs (1.9%) were euthanized. The odds of major injuries leading to the dog's euthanasia were 4.3 times higher than that of the minor injuries (95% confidence intervals (CI95), 1.3-14.2). Biting is a natural and a preventable behavior, that deserves better mitigation strategies with a deeper understanding of the risk and the protective factors. The results of this study contribute to creating a scientific basis for the investigation of dog bites in Portugal.

Forensic Death Investigations of Dog Bite Injuries in 31 Cats
Hsiou CL, Hsu CC, Liao PW, Yang FH, Lee AN, Huang WH · Animals · 2022 · 10.3390/ani12182404

Abstract. This forensic veterinary study analyzed bite injury patterns in 31 necropsied cats and applied DNA analysis for species identification. Researchers found that puncture wounds, linear or small round contusions/abrasions, and lacerations/avulsions are characteristic bite-related injuries. The examination of 31 cats revealed the most commonly injured regions included the ventral thorax, hindlimbs, shoulder-to-dorsal thorax, back, abdomen, neck, and hip/tail/perineum. Canine mitochondrial DNA was successfully identified in wound swabs and hair samples from broken claws. The study provided an applicable method for canine DNA identification in cats and reference data for future veterinary forensic investigations of dog bite injuries.

Occurrence and Risk Factors of Dog Bites in Northern Indigenous Communities: A Scoping Review
Daigle · Frontiers · 2022

Abstract. The relationship between northern Indigenous people and dogs has evolved over the past years alongside events such as colonization, settlement, proliferation of snowmobiling and other socio-cultural and environmental changes. These changes have had negative impacts on this relationship, and with the endemic presence of arctic fox rabies, dog bites have become an important public health burden. The objective of this study was to synthesize the state of knowledge regarding the occurrence of dog bites and associated risk factors in the specific context of northern Indigenous communities. A scoping review was conducted in seven bibliographic databases, from June 2018 to May 2020. From this search, 257 original studies were identified and eight papers were included for final analysis. Annual occurrence of dog bites in northern Indigenous communities ranged from 0.61 to 59.6/10,000 inhabitants. Dog bites affected 27–62.9% of the population in those regions during their lifetime. Very few studies compared the occurrence of dog bites between people living in northern communities with other populations or settings, but available evidence suggests that Indigenous people living in northern communities are at higher risk of dog bites than the rest of the population. Several individual and environmental risk factors were identified in the selected studies, although the strength of evidence varied significantly. Age (children) and gender (male) were well documented individual risk factors. Other factors, such as organizational barriers to dog management and lack of access to veterinary services, were identified and discussed by several authors. The results of this study support concerns about the higher risk of bites in northern Indigenous communities, and underscore the urgent need for more research into the contextual and environmental factors that impact the mitigation of these risks.

Analysis of tooth mark patterns on bone remains caused by wolves (Canis lupus) and domestic dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) for taxonomic identification: A scoping review focused on their value as a forensic tool
Gonzalez, V. · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. The interaction between canids and humans is not free of conflicts. In Europe, wolf and dog attacks on domestic animals cause social and financial damages. Governments spend significant sums in compensation payments. Some of the allegations of wolf attacks on livestock may be false or difficult to prove. The insufficient expertise and unreliable methods used during the investigations often make it difficult to achieve a successful perpetrator identification, which leads to the stigmatization of this species and wrongly paid compensations. Despite notable advances in bite mark analysis, most studies were carried out comparing bite marks from wolves and/or dogs and taxa belonging to other families. Comparative studies of wolf and dog bite marks and tooth marks to identify a potential aggressor agent are very limited. These studies are commonly used in archaeological, paleontological and taphonomic contexts, but not in forensics. In our study, 12,120 records were reviewed and only 16 of them fulfilled the search criteria set by the authors. Only one article carried out, exclusively, a comparison of wolf and dog bite mark patterns. The aim of the present work is to review and evaluate the studies on the identification of tooth marks on bone remains caused by two subspecies belonging to the same genus, wolves and domestic dogs. Currently, in a forensic context, there is inconclusive evidence to distinguish with certainty whether death was caused by wolves or domestic dogs using the forensic analysis of tooth/bite mark patterns from both canids (beyond any reasonable doubt). New and complementary forensic tools must be developed to differentiate between these two subspecies with a higher degree of certainty. Forensic veterinary odontology could play an important role in fulfilling this goal.

The first assessment of a dog bite prevention program for pre-school children in Turkey
Isparta · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2021

Abstract. Child safety around dogs is a serious public health concern, in particular in countries where free-ranging dogs and dog-mediated urban rabies are an issue, such as Turkey. The present study is the first to investigate the effectiveness of a dog bite prevention program in pre-school children in Turkey. In this study, pre-school students aged between 3 and 6 years (N = 117) received an interactive education program. The program consisted of an interactive oral presentation, a drama session (“Being a Tree”), and a practical session, i.e. meeting a dog. Two different questionnaires were administered to children before (PrE), immediately after (PE0), and one week after the program (PE1): (i) the Dog-Human Interaction questionnaire and (ii) the Contexts for Approaching Dogs questionnaire. Significant improvements in the scores in both questionnaires were found between the evaluations before and after the education. Moreover, older students had significantly higher scores in both questionnaires during PE0 and PE1. The majority of the students were found to be successful in the evaluation of the “Being a Tree'' concept during PE0 and PE1. The results of this study show that the interactive dog bite prevention program can increase the awareness and knowledge of pre-school children on dog behavior. Findings also suggest that age should be considered when designing a dog bite education program. As being a tree concept can be learned by children from all age groups, it can be a useful and beneficial component of dog bite prevention programs in countries having a big population of free-ranging dogs.
