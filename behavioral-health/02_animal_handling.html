Low-Stress Dog Handling

Low-stress handling is any process that reduces stress in the animal being handled. It improves welfare and safety, reduces the chance of forming negative handling associations, and keeps staff safe from bites and injury. It depends first on reading canine body language so the handler can judge the dog's emotional state and adjust in real time.

Scientific Protocols for Shelter Animal Handling

Speed

Getting difficult dogs out of kennels

  • Show the dog the leash and speak kindly, using a familiar cue such as "wanna go for a walk?" to change her emotional state before the door opens. your notes
  • Crack the kennel front open and brace it with a foot so the dog cannot burst out. your notes
  • Slide a long sausage treat through the door from the outside so the dog puts her own head into the loop of the slip lead, rather than reaching in to throw the lead over her head. your notes
  • For fear-aggressive or shut-down dogs, keep a treat bucket outside the kennel or toss a treat every time someone walks past (Pavlovian conditioning). your notes
  • If a fearful dog is confirmed dog-social, consider co-kenneling with a more outgoing companion. Feed separately to prevent food guarding. If co-kenneling is not possible, consider play sessions. your notes
  • For fearful dogs use slip leads, with two handlers (one leash each, one on each side). A control pole is acceptable only at the moment of a dangerous event; the leash stays the main tool to direct and lead. your notes
Exam trap: slip-lead geometry Placing the lead "high up behind the ears" is only half the rule. The lead must then run straight up behind the head to the handler, which maximises control and reduces slipping. Be ready for a stem that gives the high placement but tests the straight-up direction. your notes

Walking dogs down the pod

  • Avoid having walked dogs face kenneled dogs. If unavoidable, the handler stands farthest from the kenneled dogs to take any redirected bite away from the dog and onto controllable space. your notes
  • If kenneled dogs are on both sides, keep the greatest distance possible and keep the walked dog in the centre of the aisle. your notes
  • Consider rolling cages to move dogs, or carry or towel-wrap smaller dogs. your notes
  • The walker's own personality shapes the walk: more neurotic and extroverted volunteers pull the leash harder and elicit more lip-licking and body shaking in the dog, while calmer, more agreeable volunteers pull less and elicit more relaxed gazing behaviour. (Shih, Frontiers in Psychology, 2021 — see journal abstracts below)

Handler and the dog: building trust

  • Let the dog approach at her own pace and give her more control. Limit early contact to 10 to 15 minutes so the dog is not overwhelmed; some need shorter sessions or drive-by treats to start. your notes
  • Turn sideways, crouch if safe, and gently toss high-value treats toward the dog. Avoid sudden moves, avoid direct eye contact, and talk quietly in a welcoming tone. your notes
  • As confidence grows, toss treats closer to the handler. Allow plenty of sniffing time and continue to ignore the dog until she solicits contact (leaning, pawing, pushing her head under the hand). your notes
  • Repeated exposure builds trust in its own right: growing familiarity with the setting and handler across handling sessions was the strongest driver of falling stress-linked heart rate and rising heart rate variability, more so than the handler's own emotional state. (Grigg, 2022 — see journal abstracts below)
  • With a shy or unknown dog, avoid reaching over the head. Pet gently on the side of the shoulder or neck. your notes

Returning dogs to the kennel

  • Toss treats into the corner of the kennel; if resources allow, give a food-stuffed toy. your notes
  • Recognise the limit: even high-value food sometimes will not overcome a dog's desire to avoid the kennel or stay with people. your notes

Handling for Procedures

For examinations and procedures, give the dog as much choice as is safe, escalate contact gradually, and use food to maintain a positive emotional state rather than only to manage an already fearful animal.

Giving the dog choice

  • Let the dog choose the location of the exam (floor versus table) and the stance of examination where practical. your notes
  • Approach from the side, not from behind or head-on. your notes
  • Prefer passive, low-restriction restraint over muzzle-hold or full-body restraint: in one shelter study, muzzle-hold restraint produced more escape attempts and full-body restraint produced more vocalising than passive restraint, with the lowest fear scores seen in passive restraint across the board. (Cisneros et al., Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2025 — see journal abstracts below)
  • Adaptive, collaborative exam procedures aimed at minimising fear are associated with a greater drop in cortisol across repeat visits than standard restraint-based exams. (Squair, Animals, 2023 — see journal abstracts below)

The touch gradient std ref

The touch gradient (Fear Free) means touching the animal lightly first, then continuing the exam with gradually escalating intensity of touch. Throughout, the handler maintains constant contact with at least one hand on the dog.

Food as part of handling

  • Use food as a distraction: peanut butter, tuna, dog food, baby food, squeeze cheese, freeze-dried real meat. your notes
  • Offer food even to calm dogs to maintain their positive emotional state and reduce fear, not only to manage dogs that are already fearful. your notes

Muzzle and room conditioning

Conditioning builds a positive emotional response to specific locations, so that a room which may host one aversive event also accumulates many positive ones. your notes

  • Let the dog investigate and sniff the room at her own pace. your notes
  • Toss high-value treats on the ground or offer them by hand; scattering food encourages exploration. your notes
  • Ignore attention-seeking behaviour; once the dog is calm and comfortable, begin interaction and take measures to keep her comfortable. your notes

Aggression and Dogfight Intervention

This panel covers the highest-risk handling scenarios: redirected aggression, highly aroused dogs, off-leash bites, and breaking up dog fights. Many of the exam points here are exact sequences and absolute prohibitions.

Common scenarios

  • Redirected aggression: prevent it with good leash handling and body position. Straighten the leash arm to hold the dog away. Walk a known redirector on a head halter, a muzzle, or both. your notes
  • Highly aroused dogs: provide social time, allow feeding and chewing enrichment in the kennel, consider co-kenneling (especially dog-social, mouthy adolescents), and consider foster, even for a single night. your notes
  • Leash grabbing: spray a bad-tasting substance (vinegar or bitter spray) on the lead, use a chain leash or cover the lead with PVC pipe, offer a toy or rope to carry, fit the slip lead high behind the ears running straight up to the handler, and straighten the arms to hold the dog away if she grabs sleeves or body. your notes

Off-leash dog that tries to bite

  • First, avoid confrontational body language: slow movements, no direct eye contact, back away slowly. your notes
  • If needed, place yourself in a kennel run or climb on or over a table, fence, or vehicle. As a last resort, put your back to a wall or into a corner so the dog cannot pull you down. your notes
  • If the dog grips clothing or a body part and is shaking her head, grab the collar, scruff, or sides of her head to minimise head movement. your notes
  • If a dangerous dog pulls you down, curl into a ball and play dead, using your hands to cover your neck. your notes

Bite-release hierarchy

Exam trap: feeding the bite and water release "Feeding the bite" means pushing the biting dog forward into the victim, which triggers a recoil-and-release reflex (not pulling the victim away). If water is used up the nose, the dog becomes harder to handle when wet and slippery, and someone must already have the dog in a control position before release to prevent redirected aggression. The most extreme options (choke for air, taser, IM injection) are last resorts only. your notes

Breaking up a dog fight

Absolute prohibition: the four nevers When breaking up a fight, do NOT grab their collars, punch, kick, or pry mouths apart. Expect a stem asking you to identify all four forbidden actions. your notes
  • Assess first: where are the tools, who else is around, and which dog is the aggressor (that is the one removed first). Use a loud alarm such as an air horn or walkie-talkie. your notes
  • Non-combatant dogs: other handlers quickly move them to safety or clip them to the fence with a carabiner. your notes
  • Least invasive method: pick up each dog's drag leash and walk in opposite directions. This does NOT work on a gripping fight and can increase damage if attempted. your notes
  • Hands-on methods (higher bite risk): wheelbarrow (only if not gripping), feeding the bite, and break stick. your notes
  • Other tools: pig board, trash can, folding table or chair brought between the dogs, then remove the aggressor; or a blanket, towel, or jacket over the heads to make them "go dark" and pause. your notes

Dogs can fight from minutes to hours, so there is usually time to get tools, EXCEPT in these emergencies: large versus small dog, blocked airway, or blocked blood flow (a tight neck grip with the victim losing consciousness). your notes

Break stick technique

  • Start by grabbing the collar right at the base of the skull so the dog cannot redirect onto you (use a leash or scruff if there is no collar). your notes
  • Hold the break stick parallel to the jaw and work it into the mouth right behind the canine teeth, about 1 inch (2.5 cm) in. your notes
  • Twist the handle, rotating the stick until the grip breaks, then remove the aggressor. Large dogs may need two sticks and two handlers, one on each side. your notes
  • Caution: this can fracture teeth, so use discretion. your notes

Equipment and Defensive Handling

Defensive handling is a set of skills and techniques to keep the handler safe: reading body language, using the leash and tools at hand, and handling gently and calmly to minimise fear and reduce the risk of aggression.

Leashes and head gear

  • Slip lead (5 to 6 ft (1.5 to 1.8 m) slip leads), harness, collar (martingale collar), and head halters. your notes
  • No single restraint device suits every dog: for dogs that pull, non-tightening front-clip harnesses balance comfort against reduced pulling best; back-clip (chest-strap or Y-shaped) harnesses suit brachycephalic breeds or dogs that do not pull; flat collars are appropriate only for dogs that do not pull, and tightening harnesses, martingale collars, and head collars warrant caution due to greater discomfort. (Cavalli & Protopopova, Animals (Basel), 2025 — see journal abstracts below)

Balance and body awareness

  • With dogs that panic or aggress, stay balanced and ready to counteract the dog's movements so you are not pulled off your feet or lose control. Wear safe, practical clothing. your notes
  • Stay standing, keep hands low (around belly-button height) and close to the body to increase safety and strength. your notes
Heuristic, not a measured constant "Belly-button height" and "10 to 15 minute" contact limits are practical training heuristics for keeping a low centre of gravity and avoiding overwhelm. They are useful and worth memorising for the exam, but they are not validated physiological thresholds. std ref

Control poles (catch or rabies poles)

ASV-aligned position Control poles are designed to keep a dog's head at a safe distance from the handler. They are not meant to lift, push, or pull a dog and are not appropriate for routine use. They can cause significant injury or death and are not suitable for cats or small dogs. This matches the ASV emphasis on minimising fear, anxiety and stress and avoiding forceful handling except in emergencies. ASV
  • Every ward should have a control pole near the fight kit. your notes
  • Never store it by hanging it by the wire noose or by propping the noose on the ground; this kinks the line and makes it hard to tighten and loosen. Hang it using attachments to the pole handle and check it regularly for wear. your notes
  • Position the pole at the top of the dog's neck and avoid twisting the wire. Practise tightening and loosening the noose on a stuffed dog or similar object. your notes
  • Do NOT pull too tight (serious injury or death), and NEVER use the pole to drag a dog behind you or to lift the dog onto an elevated surface. your notes

Cat and Feral Handling

Use minimal, gentle restraint for tractable cats. Gentle human contact can blunt the adverse effects of unpleasant stimuli, eliminate fear responses, and ease signs of pain. Cats escalate from stress to aggression very quickly, so the handler reads body language continuously and stops early.

Core techniques

  • Let cats hide their faces under a towel or in the nook of a caregiver's arm. your notes
  • Diversion techniques (gentle tapping or rocking) can redirect focus during a procedure. your notes
  • Give cats time to acclimate to new surroundings before handling, which avoids escapes and recapture. your notes
  • Easing restraint can let a cat keep coping rather than escalate to escape or defence. Chemical restraint may be needed in some situations (and tools such as nets or squeeze devices). your notes
  • Feral cats: use equipment and injectable medications rather than hands-on restraint. your notes

Pre-handling assessment

Before handling any cat, pause and assess the cat, the environment, and yourself. your notes

Assess the catAssess the environmentAssess yourself
Where is she and what is she doing? What is she communicating? Are there signs of stress or fear, and what is triggering them? Is it safe to proceed? Are you in a safe location? Can the cat escape or retreat? Are there stressful stimuli, noises, or activities you can mitigate? Do you have everything you need? Do you feel comfortable handling this cat now? What do you need to feel comfortable? Do you need to ask for help?

Approach and the finger-nose technique

  • Move slowly and smoothly; avoid reaching or leaning over the cat. your notes
  • Offer a tasty treat within easy reach as an icebreaker; talk in a calm, friendly tone (silence suits some cats better). your notes
  • Offer a hand and allow a moment to sniff, then rub and scratch the sides of the face and neck, where most cats enjoy being petted. your notes
  • Aim to maintain continuous contact rather than lifting the hand on and off repeatedly. your notes
Exam trap: the 2-second rule When handling cats, never let a resisting cat struggle for more than a couple of seconds before stopping for a break, because cats escalate from stress to aggression very rapidly and can stay agitated for some time. Treat this as an operational teaching rule rather than a measured constant. your notes

Humane handling principles

  • Ongoing observation and assessment of behaviour, with adjustments to the handling plan as needed. your notes
  • Appropriate choice and management of the environment; enough trained personnel; suitable equipment in good working condition. your notes
  • Use anxiolytic medication when needed to minimise fear, anxiety, and stress and to improve safety. your notes
  • Ensure cats keep a sense of control and perceive that escape or hiding is possible; minimise physical restraint. ASV
ASV-aligned position: forceful handling Forceful handling (for example scruffing cats or pinning dogs to the ground) should NOT be used except in extraordinary circumstances, when a human or animal is in immediate danger and other low-stress options, sedation, or delays are not possible. Lower-stress techniques include allowing hiding, slow introductions, covering a surface for traction, keeping voices low, gentle consistent touch, and treats or toys. ASV

Stress-Reduction Evidence

Handling is not only about safety; structured human interaction measurably reduces stress in shelter dogs. The exam can test the direction of these findings and the fact that benefits do not require a familiar person.

What reduces stress

  • Petting and personal interaction reduce stress in shelter dogs. About 15 minutes of petting produced decreased signs of stress, even when the volunteer was unfamiliar to the dog. std ref
  • This may have a strong impact in social dogs and can help mitigate an aversive experience such as a medical procedure. your notes
  • Play and the passive (non-interactive) presence of a human also decrease cortisol. std ref
  • Implication: shelters need not treat petting as the only lever. Time spent together in other ways, such as play or walking, is also likely beneficial. your notes
  • Caveat: gains from structured cooperative-care training do not always transfer cleanly to the exam itself; one blinded trial found no significant group difference in heart rate/heart rate variability, with the training's effect depending on the individual dog's prior tolerance of handling. (Wess, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below)
Evidence note and confidence The direction of these findings (human interaction, play, and even passive presence lower cortisol in shelter dogs, including from unfamiliar handlers) is consistent with the published literature. The specific "15 minute" figure is the value stated in your notes and is best treated as source-specific rather than a universal threshold. This corroboration is from standard behaviour references and was not verified against an uploaded textbook. std ref

Handle with care: Dogs show negative responses to restrictive handling restraints and tools during routine examinations
Cisneros A, Carroll AD, Moody CM, Stellato AC · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106601

Abstract. Routine veterinary visits are important for monitoring dog welfare; however, handling during physical examinations may elicit fear responses in dogs. Although handling recommendations exist to mitigate negative experiences for dogs, there is little empirical evidence to support these handling recommendations. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the influence of handling techniques and tools on dog behavioral and physiological responses during routine veterinary examinations. A total of 97 dogs were recruited from a local municipal shelter. Using a randomized block design balanced for age (mean age was 3.5), sex (49 female, 48 male), and size (40 small, 57 medium/large), dogs were randomly sorted into one of five treatments: 1) passive restraint, 2) muzzle-hold restraint, 3) basket muzzle, 4) soft muzzle, and 5) full-body restraint. A willingness-to-approach test was conducted to assess sociability toward the handler, followed by a handling phase (2-minute examination while in the handling treatment), and a post-handling phase (30-second observation period). Behavioral responses (posture reductions, lip licking, yawning, vocalizing, elimination, body shaking, trembling, avoidance, escape) were measured during the handling and post-handling phases, and physiological responses (temperature, respiratory and heart rate) were recorded during handling. A scale used to measure fear was also assessed during each phase using a 5-item scale (0 =no fear, 4 =extreme fear). Results indicated that across treatments, most dogs showed reduced posture during handling (63 %, 61/97), and neutral posture post-handling (95 %, 92/97). During handling, dogs in the muzzle-hold restraint displayed more escape attempts (p = 0.009) and dogs that received the full-body restraint performed more vocalizations (p = 0.008) compared to dogs in the passive restraint. It took longer to place dogs in the soft (p = 0.001) and basket muzzle (p<0.001) compared to the passive restraint. Dogs in the soft muzzle were more likely to display avoidance behaviors (p<0.001) than dogs in passive restraint. Physiological measurements did not vary by treatment group. Fear scores were lower during handling in passive restraint compared to all other treatments: muzzle-hold (p=0.001), basket muzzle (p=0.0157), soft muzzle (p=0.0001), and full-body restraint (p=0.0001). Results suggest that dogs display more negative responses when handled with the muzzle-hold and full-body restraints, compared to passive restraint and use of muzzle tools. This study can be used to inform evidence-based recommendations and support the implementation of less restrictive handling to mitigate stress for dogs during routine examinations.

Handling Techniques and Risk Factors Reported by Veterinary Professionals During Dog Examinations
Nakonechny, L · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2025 · 10.3389/fvets.2025.1634970

Abstract. Introduction: Handling techniques are known to influence dog stress in veterinary settings; however, little is known about the current handling techniques applied to dogs during routine veterinary care or risk factors associated with their use. This cross-sectional survey aimed to assess common handling techniques used on calm, fearful, and aggressive dogs by veterinary professionals in Canada and the United States and identify risk factors for minimal and full-body restraint.Methods: A convenience sample of veterinary professionals completed an online questionnaire. It collected information on participant characteristics and clinic experience (e.g., gender, Ten Item Personality Index, bite history, stress-reducing certification), participant professional quality of life (using the ProQOL scale), general examination practices (e.g., use of treats), perceptions and importance of examination factors (e.g., staff safety), and frequency of using 14 different dog handling techniques. Logistic regression models were used to identify risk factors for the use of minimal and full-body restraint on fearful and aggressive dogs.Results: Participants (N = 691) were veterinarians (39.2%, 271/691) and non-veterinarians (60.8%, 420/691), who routinely handle dogs during routine examinations in Canada (21.7%, 150/691) and the United States (79.1%, 541/691). Minimal restraint was reported to be used for calm (82.7%, 566/684), fearful (73.1%, 499/683), and aggressive (51.9%, 352/678) dogs during routine examinations. Full-body restraint was commonly reported to be used for calm dogs (58.5%, 400/684) and most frequently reported for fearful (63.9%, 434/679) and aggressive dogs (68.6%, 465/678). Handling decisions were influenced by factors including age, gender, practice type, graduation year, bite history, stress-reducing certification, and owner presence. Professionals prioritizing staff safety and using stress-reducing strategies (e.g., treats) were more likely to use minimal restraint, while owner presence and focus on examination completeness were linked to full-body restraint. Personality traits and professional well-being, particularly extraversion and secondary traumatic stress, also influenced handling choices.Discussion: Handling techniques vary with dog behavior and are shaped by numerous factors, highlighting the complex relationship between personal and clinic-level influences on veterinary staff interactions with dog patients. These findings generate hypotheses for future observational research exploring factors that support stress-reducing techniques to improve dog welfare in clinical settings.

Review of Collars, Harnesses, and Head Collars for Walking Dogs
Camila Cavalli, Alexandra Protopopova · Animals (Basel) · 2025

Abstract. As dogs are often required to be leashed in public, guardians need to choose between various restraint devices. While using collars typically considered aversive (such as choke, prong, or electric collars) is generally discouraged due to welfare concerns, guidance is less clear when it comes to selecting among other devices such as collars, harnesses, and head collars. This review examined 21 full-text articles and two abstracts on the effects of commonly used restraint devices, aiming to offer practical guidance for guardians and identifying areas for future research. The impact of these devices was examined in terms of walking kinematics, pressure distribution on the body, and behavioural signs of stress. The findings suggest there is no one-size-fits-all device, and selection should consider the individual needs of guardian and dog. For dogs that pull, non-tightening front-clip harnesses appear to offer the best balance between discomfort and reduction in pulling. Tightening harnesses, martingale collars, and head collars can pose greater discomfort and should be used with caution. For brachycephalic breeds or when pulling is not a concern, back-clip harnesses are suitable, especially chest-strap or Y-shaped ones. Flat collars are also appropriate for dogs that do not pull as they produce the least body restriction.

Effects of Changing Veterinary Handling Techniques on Canine Behaviour and Physiology Part 1: Physiological Measurements
Squair · Animals · 2023

Abstract. Signs of distress in dogs are often normalized during routine veterinary care, creating an animal welfare concern. We sought to test whether targeted interventions during veterinary visits affect physiological indicators of stress in dogs. Some 28 dogs were examined within four visits across 8 weeks. All dogs received the same care during the first visit and were then randomized into control and intervention groups for visits 2–4. In the intervention group, 14 dogs underwent procedures designed to reduce stress and to enlist their collaboration during examination. The 14 dogs in the control group received routine care. At each visit, heart rate (HR), serum cortisol (CORT), neutrophil lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and creatine kinase (CK) were measured. A composite stress index based on the summed standardized scores for these markers was constructed. No differences in HR, NLR, and CK parameters between groups were found, and both groups had a decrease in CORT by visit four. However, the intervention group showed a greater overall decrease in CORT between the first and fourth visit than the control group (p < 0.04). The composite stress index differed between the first and fourth visits for the intervention group, but not for the control group (Intervention p = 0.03; Control p= 0.288). There was a tendency for the composite stress index to worsen at visit four vs. visit one for the control group. The findings suggest that dogs that participated in adaptive, collaborative exams and procedures designed to minimize fear had a greater reduction in stress over time compared to those receiving standard care.

Assessing the Relationship Between Emotional States of Dogs and Their Human Handlers, Using Simultaneous Behavioral and Cardiac Measures
Grigg · · 2022

Abstract. Negative stress due to human handling has been reported for a number of domestic animals, including dogs. Many companion dogs display significant stress during routine care in the veterinary clinic, risking injury to staff and potentially compromising the quality of care that these dogs receive. On the other hand, positive interactions with humans can have a beneficial effect on dogs, particularly in stressful situations such as animal shelters. Research has shown that dogs can detect human emotions through visual, auditory, and chemical channels, and that dogs will exhibit emotional contagion, particularly with familiar humans. This study investigated relationships between emotional states of dogs and unfamiliar human handlers, using simultaneous measures of cardiac activity and behavior, during two sessions of three consecutive routine handling sets. Measures of cardiac activity included mean heart rate (HRmean), and two measures of heart rate variability (HRV): the root mean square of successive differences between normal heartbeats (RMSSD); and the high frequency absolute power component of HRV, log transformed (HFlog). We also assessed human handlers' emotional state during handling sessions following an intervention designed to reduce stress, compared with sessions conducted on a different day and following a control activity. Polar H10 cardiac sensors were used to simultaneously record cardiac activity for both canine and human participants, and behavioral data were collected via digital video. The strongest influence on the dogs' stress levels in our study was found to be increasing familiarity with the setting and the handler; HRmean and SI decreased, and HRV (as RMSSD) increased, significantly from the first to the third handling set. Canine HRV (as HFlog) was also highest in set 3, although the difference was not statistically significant. There were no strong patterns found in the human cardiac data across handling set, session, or by pre-handling activity. We did not find consistent support for emotional contagion between the dogs and their handlers in this study, perhaps due to the brief time that the dogs spent with the handlers. Recommendations for application to dog handling, and limitations of our methods, are described.

Effect of cooperative care training on physiological parameters and compliance in dogs undergoing a veterinary examination – A pilot study
Wess, L · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2022

Abstract. One way to reduce fear and lack of compliance during veterinary procedures is ‘cooperative care training’, training animals to voluntarily participate in husbandry and medical care. Here we assessed the effects of cooperative care training on heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), tympanic membrane temperature (TMT) and compliance (i.e., all steps of the examination completed) of dogs during a veterinary examination. A blinded controlled trial with 47 dogs (training group (TG): 26; control group (CG): 21) was carried out. Forty dogs completed the study (TG: 22, CG:18). Dogs and their owners took part in a standardized veterinary visit twice (visit interval: 140 ± 23 days). In between, the TG took part in cooperative care training (10 ± 2 group training sessions, additional training at home). Linear mixed models were calculated (fixed effects HR/HRV: group* visit* period, TMT: group* visit; random effect: dogID). Compliance (completed steps of exam) was analyzed using McNemar tests. HR/HRV was higher/lower during the veterinary examination compared to the waiting room (period: p < 0.001), indicating that the examination was more stressful. No significant differences in HR/HRV regarding group*visit were found. TMT, taken at the end of each examination, resulted in a significant group*visit interaction (p = 0.008); stress appeared increased during visit 2 in the CG whereas in the TG a heterogeneous pattern, with 42% of dogs with reduced rightward bias, was observed. Compliance was lower in the TG during the second visit (p = 0.04). Trainer and owners claimed observable improvement of behavior during training and a decrease in HR between visits correlated significantly with trainer perception of high training success (rs = −0.52, p = 0.049). Overall, transfer of trained skills to the veterinary examination performed by a team blinded to the group allocation was poor. TMT analyses and HR/HRV data within the training group, suggest that the training had differing effects on the dogs based on their previous tolerance of handling and their progress made during training. Further research how to optimize training programs, including desensitization and counterconditioning, and their outcomes is needed.

Behavior Education and Intervention Program at a Small Shelter I. Effect on Behavior Knowledge and Safety
Lilly, Leanne M · Animals · 2021

Abstract. Behavior knowledge is a critical component of shelter staff and volunteer training. Canine behavior knowledge was assessed at a small shelter before and after a six-month program of educational seminars and real-time case interventions for staff and volunteers. Participants were asked to complete surveys assessing their knowledge of body language, motivation, and training methodology, as well as rank their own comfort with these topics. Staff and volunteers who participated in the program and interacted with the dogs were compared to board members (who do not interact with the dogs) and feline-only volunteers. Prior to the program, both groups demonstrated poor scores and self-assessments did not correlate with knowledge scores. Those who participated in the program were more likely to be high performers on the post-program survey, odds ratio: 1.594 (95%CI 1.10–2.137) p = 0.002. Psychometric evaluation of the survey answers themselves illustrated gaps in behavior knowledge in shelter members and suggests that both seminar and case interventions can improve this. Formalized behavior education and measurement of behavior knowledge may facilitate the mission of shelters and safety there-in.

Two Ends of the Leash: Relations Between Personality of Shelter Volunteers and On-leash Walking Behavior With Shelter Dogs
Shih · Frontiers in Psychology · 2021

Abstract. Human personality influences the way people interact with dogs. This study investigated the associations between the personality of animal shelter volunteers and behavior during on-leash walks with shelter dogs. Video recording and a canine leash tension meter were used to monitor the on-leash walking. Personality was measured in five dimensions (neurotic, extroverted, open, agreeable and conscientious) with the NEO Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI). Neurotic volunteers pulled the leash harder and tended to interact with dogs using more body language; dogs being walked by neurotic volunteers in turn displayed more lip-licking and body shaking and were more likely to be rated as well-behaved. Extroverted volunteers were associated with stronger maximal leash tension at both the human and dog ends of the leash, and they praised the dog more, often in a high pitched voice. These volunteers eliciting more tail-wagging and body shaking by the dog. Extroverted volunteers were also more tolerant of different dog behaviors. Volunteers with personalities characterized by “openness to experiences” were less likely to verbally attract the attention of dogs, praise dogs and talk to them in a high-pitched voice; however, dogs walked by these volunteers were more likely to pull on the leash, and engaged in more lip-licking but less sniffing. “Agreeable” volunteers liked to verbally attract the attention of the dogs and more commonly initiated hand gestures and physical contact, causing the dogs to pull less frequently; dogs in these dyads displayed more gazing and lip-licking behaviors. Conscientious volunteers were less likely to pull the leash and tended to have more physical contact with the dogs but did not favor verbal communication and did not use a high pitched voice.
