Definitions: Anxiety, Fear, and Phobia

The single most testable item in this subdomain is the three-way distinction. The presence or absence of an identifiable stimulus, and the proportionality of the response, separate the three states.

Pathological anxiety

An uncontrollable, excessive, inappropriate, generalised, dysfunctional, and aversive emotion that triggers physiological and behavioural responses lacking adaptive value. The response is to the EXAGGERATED ANTICIPATION OR PERCEPTION of threats, INCOMMENSURATE with the actual situation. your notes

Signs of anxiety to recognise clinically (Box 12.3)

  • Hypervigilance, scanning. your notes
  • Increased motor activity: restlessness, pacing, circling. your notes
  • Vocalisation, whining. your notes
  • DISPLACEMENT behaviours: out-of-context grooming and scratching, yawning, lip licking, whining and barking, destructiveness, digging. your notes
  • Changes in social-soliciting behaviour: increase OR decrease in attention seeking. your notes
  • Hiding, escape attempts. your notes
  • Physiologic signs: trembling, dilated pupils, hypersalivation, increased RR, increased HR, urination, defaecation, vomiting. your notes
  • Decreased appetite. your notes

The freeze response and behavioural shutdown

A dog standing rigidly, panting heavily, with dilated pupils, lip licking, and a tucked tail is in a FEAR state with behavioural shutdown (the freeze response), often misread as "calm" or "stubborn." Fear responses can be aggression, cowering, shaking, freezing, or escape. your notes

Neuroanatomy of the Fear Response

Five structures and one cascade. The amygdala triggers it. The locus ceruleus broadcasts noradrenergic alarm. The hippocampus contextualises and is damaged by chronic cortisol. The raphe nuclei supply forebrain serotonin and modulate the response. The forebrain enables unlearning.

The five structures

StructureRole in fear
AmygdalaPRIMARY site for processing fear-evoking triggers (visual, auditory, odour, hypoxia, pain). Inputs from sensory organs through the THALAMUS. Triggers an immediate physiologic startle BEFORE the slower cortical pathway can analyse whether the threat is real, and once the emotional stage is triggered it is difficult to inhibit. Signals the paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus to release corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF), stimulating the HPA axis and NE release from the locus ceruleus. CENTRAL SITE for fear conditioning (integrating prior memory).
Locus ceruleusBrainstem nucleus in the gray matter of the PONS. Contains the HIGHEST concentration of noradrenergic neurons in the brain, with projections to the cortex, hypothalamus, hippocampus, and median and dorsal raphe nuclei. Receives sensory and visceral input plus excitatory afferents from the central nucleus of the amygdala. Stimulation triggers NE release. Dysregulation produces panic attacks, ADHD, sleep and arousal disorders, and affective disorders.
HippocampusMajor nucleus of the limbic system; memory storage; anatomical connections with the amygdala and hypothalamus. Processes CONTEXTUAL information and differentiates safe from dangerous. Normally SUPPRESSES the HPA axis. In chronic stress, sustained cortisol DAMAGES the hippocampus, downregulates the HPA-axis brake, and produces excessively high cortisol, a vicious cycle. Reduced hippocampal volume is documented in PTSD and in animals exposed to chronic stress.
Dorsal and medial raphe nucleiTwo functional neuron clusters in the centromedial brainstem, part of the reticular formation. Provide VIRTUALLY ALL serotonin input to the forebrain. Help modulate fear, anxiety, and autonomic responses, AND the cognitive and motor components that INHIBIT the fight or flight response.
ForebrainModulates the limbic system and hypothalamus. A functioning forebrain is necessary to UNLEARN fear responses (extinction).
Exam trap: cortisol-hippocampus vicious cycle Hippocampus normally SUPPRESSES the HPA axis (negative feedback). Chronic stress damages hippocampal neurons, removing that brake, which produces even higher cortisol, more damage, and reduced hippocampal volume. This is the human PTSD parallel. your notes

The fear cascade (sympathetic and HPA)

  • SNS releases NE and epinephrine (from subcortical brain and adrenal glands), driving fight, flight, or freeze. your notes
  • Physiologic response: increased HR, BP, RR, and vasoconstriction to internal organs. your notes
  • Epinephrine breaks down glycogen and fat (lipolysis), and increases gluconeogenesis to fuel fight or flight. your notes
  • HPA stimulation releases CORTISOL. Chronic stimulation drives long-term medical and behavioural consequences. your notes

Neurotransmitter targets for treatment

Dysregulation of fear pathways involves alterations in SEROTONIN (raphe to forebrain), NORADRENALINE (locus ceruleus), and GABA. These are the targets of SSRIs, TCAs, alpha-2 agonists, and benzodiazepines respectively. your notes

Determinants of Fear and the Learning Principles

Fear has genetic, environmental, and learning determinants. One unpleasant event can produce lasting fear (one-event learning). Fear can stay specific or generalise. The exam tests whether you understand kindling, sensitisation, discrimination, and generalisation.

Determinants of fear (Box 12.2)

CategoryDetail
GeneticsSPECIES: unconditioned stimuli for fear (predators, environmental danger, novel situations, social threats). INDIVIDUAL: temperament.
EnvironmentalInadequate socialisation or habituation; sensory isolation or nutritional deprivation or illness during development; TRAUMATIC or aversive experiences (conditioning); consequences and learning (negative reinforcement by stimulus retreat each time the fear display "works").
Components of fearPHYSIOLOGY: autonomic and neuroendocrine arousal, cardiovascular changes, pupils, piloerection, glucose metabolism. BEHAVIOUR: type determined by genetics, breed, individual, experience, environment, type and intensity of the stimulus, and the presence or absence of conspecifics or family. EMOTION: observation of the emotional state of stimulus, family members, and other dogs (social transmission).
Exam trap: one-event learning, discrimination, and generalisation An intensely aversive event can produce an intensely fearful and LASTING memory. The dog may stay fearful of the specific stimulus (DISCRIMINATION, e.g. a particular noise, place, or person), OR generalise from one example to many similar stimuli (e.g. fear of one child becomes fear of all children). your notes

Kindling and sensitisation

The kindling effect Repeated, intermittent, sub-threshold or threshold stimulation of fear circuits progressively LOWERS the threshold for future stimulation, so phobic reactions intensify with unmanaged exposure over time. Originally a neuroscience term from the epilepsy literature (Goddard 1969), extended to behavioural sensitisation in fear and PTSD models. Operationally, this is why repeated unmanaged exposure to a phobic stimulus (e.g. a noise-phobic dog enduring storm after storm without intervention) makes the next exposure WORSE, not better. std ref

Arousal and the loss of conscious choice

High states of arousal or anxiety place the dog in a state of HYPERVIGILANCE in which fear responses become AUTOMATIC and preclude conscious decisions about how to respond. This is why a panicking dog cannot "think its way out" of the situation, and why training must occur in calm states well below the fear threshold. your notes

Excessive fear: contributing factors

  • Genetic factors and breed predisposition. your notes
  • Inadequate early environmental experience and early socialisation. your notes
  • One or more unpleasant experiences producing conditioned fear. your notes
  • Medical or behavioural pathology. your notes
  • Loss of maintained social and environmental exposure later in life (even adequately socialised dogs can become fearful if exposure lapses). your notes
  • Unpleasant and stressful experiences during early adolescence, INCLUDING punishment-based training, can contribute to fear conditioning and increased avoidance. your notes

Behavioural Modification

Two principles, six techniques, one threshold rule, and one absolute prohibition. Punishment of fear ALWAYS makes the problem worse.

The two principles

  1. Avoidance of any stimuli or situations that evoke a fear response outside of training sessions. Every uncontrolled exposure FURTHER INCREASES the fear (kindling). your notes
  2. Controlled exposure at intensities below the fear threshold, paired with positive outcomes, so the pet relaxes in the presence of the stimulus and it no longer elicits a fear response. your notes

The six core techniques

TechniqueMechanism
Controlled exposureThe umbrella concept; deliberate, graded contact with the fear-eliciting stimulus under conditions the trainer manages.
HabituationRepeated exposure to a mild stimulus until the pet stops responding. Effective only when the fear is mild and the pet can adapt at its own pace.
Systematic desensitisationControlled exposure at an intensity (or distance, volume, familiarity) that is WEAKER than what triggers fear, so the pet remains SUBTHRESHOLD throughout.
CounterconditioningChange the underlying emotional response (the "feeling") about the stimulus by PAIRING it with HIGH-VALUE rewards (food, toys, play) at each subthreshold exposure. Reward must be reserved for stimulus exposure, otherwise the value drops.
Response substitutionTrain an alternative, desirable, calm behaviour that is physically INCOMPATIBLE with the fear response (sit-stay, look or watch, go to a mat) and reward it heavily on exposure.
ShapingReward successive approximations of the desired calm behaviour, increasing criteria gradually.

Designing the exposure gradient

AxisHow to grade
DistanceStart with the stimulus FAR (e.g. 20 m (65.6 ft)), progress to closer (a few metres at a time)
Volume / reproductionFor sound-based fears, start with audio recordings at barely perceptible volume (storms, fireworks, vacuum cleaner) and increase incrementally
Appearance and familiarityTrain with family members or less-threatening individuals before introducing strangers or specific triggers (Box 12.6 example for fear of men: familiar women → unfamiliar women → familiar boys → unfamiliar boys → familiar men → unfamiliar men)
Activity levelLow to high; quiet still child to running playing child
LocationCalmest environment first; train at home or in a calm setting before progressing to public spaces
Exam trap: the threshold rule If at any step the pet shows fear, anxiety, or aggression and refuses the high-value reward, the exposure MUST stop immediately. Move to the previous (lower) distance, volume, or intensity where the pet was calm, allow full recovery, and resume at that level. Forcing exposure when the pet has crossed its threshold STOPS learning and causes SENSITISATION and panic. your notes
Never punish a fearful display Punishment, anger, frustration, or even just an upset emotional state in the owner AGGRAVATES the fearful emotional state. While punishment might briefly reduce a visible undesirable behaviour, fear is an emotional state, and punishment ADDS to it; the underlying problem worsens and defensive aggression risk rises. your notes

Controlled flooding: narrow indication

Controlled flooding (continuous exposure until habituation) is most likely to be effective ONLY IF the stimulus does NOT genuinely threaten, owners can remain CALM, and the pet is restrained until it habituates AND THEN rewarded. It is appropriate only when the pet's fear will quickly dissipate; if fear increases, it becomes a welfare issue and FURTHER SENSITISES the pet. Confinement crates or head halters help with safety. your notes

Diagnosis and conflict signs

  • Fear cluster: aggression, cowering, shaking, freezing, or escape. your notes
  • Signs of CONFLICT indicating discomfort: displacement behaviours such as yawning, lip licking, whining, out-of-context grooming, or circling. your notes
  • Physical signs: tail held low or tucked, ears back, avoidance of eye contact, lowered body position, leaning away, lateral recumbency, submissive urination. your notes

Noise and Storm Phobia Protocol

Noise phobia affects 40 to 50% of dogs. The storm phobia and noise phobia overlap is asymmetric: about 90% of storm-phobic dogs also have noise phobia, but only 75% of noise-phobic dogs have storm phobia. Storm phobia is harder because the natural stimulus (barometric pressure, ionisation, lightning, rain on the roof) is multifactorial and not under owner control.

Prevalence and risk

  • Noise phobia is one of the most common concerns of dog owners, affecting 40 to 50% of dogs. Triggers include fireworks, engine noises, banging doors, vacuum cleaners. your notes
  • Protective factor: exposure to LOUD VOICES before 6 months may have a protective effect. your notes
  • Contributors: genetic, previous experience, SOCIAL TRANSMISSION (learned or copied from other fearful dogs), owner responses. your notes
  • Dogs with generalised anxiety may be fearful of a broad range of stimuli. your notes
Exam trap: the 90 / 75 asymmetry Approximately 90% of dogs with STORM phobia also have NOISE phobia, but only about 75% of dogs with NOISE phobia have STORM phobia. Storm phobia is the more complex condition with broader triggers (pressure, ionisation, light, rain, noise). your notes

Clinical signs

Tremble, salivate, hide, attention seek, attempt to escape. Owners often describe the dog as "becoming a different dog" during events. your notes

Management: layered approach

LayerWhat it looks like
Safe spaceAn indoor room or confinement area where stimuli can be minimised (closet, furnace room, crate with sound-reducing cover, "Thunder Hut" nested cardboard or blanket arrangement). Allow the pet to RETREAT here, not forced.
Sight and sound barriersLight-proof window shades or storm shutters to seal out lightning. Mask external noise with a TV, radio, white noise machine, fan, or MUSIC WITH A DISTRACTING BEAT (drums, rap).
DistractionsFavoured chews, food-filled toys, companionship of a person, and the background sounds described above. Leash and head halter can help focus and settle some dogs.
Commercial calming aidsMutt Muffs (ear), Doggles and Calming Caps (visual), Anxiety Wrap and Thundershirt (gentle constant pressure), Storm Defender Cape (claimed to reduce associated electrical charges; one preliminary lab study showed effect, one study found no difference vs placebo though both groups reported improvement).
Never punishPunishment converts fear into a worse emotional state and adds risk of aggression. your notes
Ignore mild signs ONLY ifThe dog can recover SPONTANEOUSLY. For more intense distress, ACTIVE assistance is better (see below).
Assist the phobic dogIf the reaction is extreme, distract with a head halter, novel chews or toys or treats, or stage a "STORM PARTY" (excitedly toss treats and toys around the home).

Desensitisation and counterconditioning protocol

  1. Train a SETTLE CUE first: in a completely calm environment, reward-train the pet to voluntarily go to its safe space and relax on command. You cannot teach a complex behaviour to a panicking dog. your notes
  2. Use a CD recording of the storm or firework noise at SUBTHRESHOLD volume. your notes
  3. Pair the noise with the dog's favoured rewards (RESERVED only for noise-exposure sessions to keep their value high). your notes
  4. Increase volume only when the dog stays relaxed; drop back if any sign of anxiety. your notes

Anxiolytic support

  • For year-round or seasonal cover: SSRI (e.g. fluoxetine) or clomipramine. your notes
  • Adjunctive as-needed BEFORE the storm: benzodiazepine, clonidine, trazodone, or propranolol. your notes
  • SELEGILINE is sometimes used for noise phobia associated with chronic anxiety, but ONLY after complete withdrawal of any SSRI or TCA (serotonin syndrome risk). your notes
The myth that comforting reinforces fear Comforting a panicking dog does NOT reinforce the fear, because fear is an involuntary EMOTION, not an operant behaviour. Reinforcement principles apply to behaviours, not emotions. Active intervention (distraction, structural support, a "storm party") helps break the panic cycle; ignoring only works if the dog will recover spontaneously. std ref

Prevention

  • Expose early. your notes
  • Habituate to a variety of stimuli during the socialisation window. your notes

Pharmacology of Fear, Phobia, and Anxiety

Two tempos: daily MAINTENANCE drugs that take 2 to 4 weeks (up to 8 weeks) to produce maximal clinical effect, and as-needed EVENT drugs given 1 to 2 hours before a known trigger. The exam tests combinations, dose adjustments, washouts, and one classic contraindication. Full doses live in the Behaviour Medications hub.

Long-term daily maintenance

ClassExamplesNotes
SSRIsFluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, citalopramDecrease overall arousal, impulsivity, reactivity. Onset 2 to 4 weeks, maximal effect up to 8 weeks. First line for chronic anxiety, generalised anxiety, compulsive disorders, separation anxiety, seasonal noise phobias.
TCAsClomipramine, amitriptyline, doxepinCLOMIPRAMINE is the most selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor of the TCAs and is the standard for compulsive disorders, stereotypic behaviours, and ALD. Other TCAs are less effective for compulsive disorders because they have less SSRI effect.
MAOISelegilineUsed for noise phobia associated with chronic anxiety, or for CCDS. STRICTLY contraindicated concurrent with SSRIs or TCAs due to fatal serotonin syndrome risk. Switching FROM an SSRI requires a full washout (see below).

Short-term as-needed (event) medications

  • Benzodiazepines (alprazolam, diazepam, clonazepam): excellent for acute panic or situational anxiety. Give 1 to 2 hours pre-event. Side effects: sedation, INHIBIT learning and memory (so as the pet improves, lower doses), DRUG DISINHIBITION (may make a fearful animal more confident and unexpectedly aggressive), rebound on rapid withdrawal, paradoxical agitation, hepatotoxicity reports in cats. your notes
  • Alpha-2 agonist (clonidine): decreases central noradrenergic output; effective as a situational tool OR as a daily adjunct to SSRI or TCA. your notes
  • Trazodone: serotonergic; can augment daily SSRI or TCA therapy as an event medication. your notes
  • Propranolol: beta-blocker; an option for storm-event cover. your notes

Safe combination rules

TCA + FLUOXETINEReduce TCA dose by 10 to 25%Fluoxetine inhibits cytochrome P450 enzymes, slowing TCA clearance and raising serum levels
TCA + SERTRALINEReduce TCA dose by 50%Even larger pharmacokinetic interaction; still use with caution given serotonin syndrome potential

Exam trap: signs of serotonin syndrome Tremors, HYPERTHERMIA, rigid muscle hypertonicity, mental status changes, and DIARRHOEA. Neuromuscular plus autonomic plus cognitive. your notes

Washout requirements

  • Switching FROM fluoxetine TO an MAOI (selegiline): washout of 5 to 6 weeks required because of fluoxetine's long-lived ACTIVE metabolite NORFLUOXETINE. your notes
  • Switching FROM other SSRIs or TCAs: shorter, drug-specific washouts apply (cross-reference Behaviour Medications hub). your notes
  • Switching FROM an MAOI TO an SSRI or TCA: also requires a washout before starting the second drug. your notes

Treatment duration and tapering

  • If the behavioural problem improves on medication, continue for AT LEAST 6 months; in chronic or compulsive cases, lifelong maintenance is often required. your notes
  • If tapering is attempted, do so GRADUALLY over several months; abrupt discontinuation triggers immediate relapse and behavioural regression. your notes
Acepromazine for noise or storm phobia: contraindicated as monotherapy Acepromazine provides chemical TRANQUILISATION and motor immobilisation but does NOT provide ANXIOLYSIS. The animal remains terrified internally while unable to move or escape, and acepromazine can heighten auditory sensitivity. Modern veterinary behavioural medicine treats this as a welfare problem. Acepromazine is not in your PDF directly but is standard clinical teaching for noise and storm phobia management. std ref

Augmentation and adjuncts

  • BUSPIRONE: additional anxiolysis without sedation; can cause disinhibition. your notes
  • GABAPENTIN: anxiety AND pain combinations with SSRIs. your notes
  • MIRTAZAPINE: for insomnia and agitation. your notes
  • MELATONIN: for chronic anxiety and phobic disorders such as storm phobia. your notes
  • THYROID supplementation: with SSRIs or TCAs as augmentation for mood elevation (human data). your notes
  • PHEROMONES (Adaptil, Feliway): may reduce anxiety in dogs and cats respectively. your notes

Non-pharmacological anxiolytic adjuncts std refnotes

These interventions reduce physiological arousal or perceived threat without pharmacological mechanisms. They are first-line adjuncts for mild-to-moderate anxiety and useful bridging tools while awaiting SSRI onset (~4–6 weeks). None can substitute for behaviour modification or pharmacotherapy in established phobic disorders.

Adaptil (DAP — dog appeasing pheromone)Pheromone analogue of the appeasing pheromone secreted by lactating bitches. Exam trap · pheromones and wraps will not resolve a phobiaThese adjuncts reduce baseline arousal — they do not extinguish conditioned fear associations. A dog with a thunderstorm phobia that receives Adaptil alone will still panic during storms. Clinical rule: use non-pharmacological adjuncts as the first step or as a bridge while SSRI takes effect, not as a substitute for desensitisation/counter-conditioning and pharmacotherapy in established phobic disorders.

The impact of delaying behavior rehabilitation onset on fear and outcome measures in dogs in a shelter setting
Collins K, Miller K, Eagan BH, Patterson-Kane E, Rehner-Fleurant T, Cussen V, Reid P · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025

Abstract. Fearful dogs in shelters are commonly given a 'settling-in' period before behavior modification begins, but the welfare value of delaying treatment has not been tested. This study compared dogs that began behavior modification soon after intake with dogs whose treatment onset was delayed, evaluating changes in fear and behavioral outcome measures over the course of rehabilitation. Dogs that began behavior modification within approximately ten days of intake showed reductions in fear sooner than dogs whose treatment was delayed. Delaying treatment slightly reduced the total number of weeks spent in treatment, but the prolonged settling-in period without active treatment did not itself improve fear-related behaviors. The authors concluded that, when shelter resources allow, starting behavior modification as early as possible is likely beneficial for the welfare and quality of life of fearful shelter dogs and is the most ethical option, rather than waiting for an extended acclimation period before intervening.

Tools for the Approach of Fear, Anxiety, and Stress in the Domestic Feline: An Update
Barrios F, Ruiz P, Damian JP · Veterinary Medicine International · 2025

Abstract. Domestic cats are one of the most popular pets worldwide. Despite their growing popularity, there remains a significant gap in understanding their specific needs, leading to frequent challenges in human-cat coexistence and behavioral issues. The distinct emotional systems of cats, compared to other domestic species and humans, complicate the interpretation of feline behavior. Notably, fear, anxiety, and stress are among the most prevalent behavioral problems in cats. This review delves into the core concepts of anxiety, fear, and stress responses in domestic cats, highlighting their unique characteristics and providing recommendations for effective handling. It also presents key international recommendations for creating a 'cat-friendly' environment, as well as suggestions for pharmacological management to ensure a stress-free visit to the clinic for the cat, its caretaker, and the veterinary team.

Attachment style and social behavior in dogs from commercial breeding kennels
Stahl · Applied Animal Behavior Science · 2024

Abstract. Much of the demand for purebred dogs in the USA is met by commercial breeding kennels (CBKs). In CBKs, adult dogs have been reported to exhibit signs of fear towards strangers. Social fear is a particular welfare concern as it may lead to acute and chronic stress in the kennel, and later, after dogs are retired and rehomed. Studies on pet and shelter dogs have shown that the type of attachment style dogs have with their caretakers is associated with their social behavior towards strangers. Thus, the aims of this study were to characterize the attachment styles of dogs from CBKs to their caretakers, and investigate relationships between the types of attachment styles observed and social fear behaviors in the dogs. Forty-eight adult dogs were tested from three CBKs in Indiana, USA. Twenty (41.67%) dogs were classified as secure, 16 (33.33%) as insecure ambivalent, 9 (18.75%) as insecure avoidant, and 3 (6.25%) as insecure disorganized. Generalized linear mixed-effects models showed that secure and ambivalent dogs approached the stranger more frequently (X2 = 24.636, p < 0.001), and solicited contact more frequently (X2 = 13.092, p = 0.004) than did avoidant dogs. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests showed that secure, ambivalent, and avoidant dogs all initially approached the stranger more quickly in the Paired-Effect test (when the caretaker was present) than in the Stranger Arena test (p < 0.05). Secure and ambivalent dogs also spent more time in proximity to (p < 0.001) and solicited more contact from (p < 0.01) the stranger in the Paired-Effect test than in the Stranger Arena test. These results suggest that there is an association between type of attachment style and fear behaviors towards a stranger in this sample of dogs. Understanding connections between attachment style and social fear, and their implications for stress and welfare in dogs from CBKs may help inform breeders’ socialization, social interactions, and related management practices. This in turn may help to reduce dogs’ social fear and enhance their long-term welfare outcomes in the kennel. These insights may also aid rehoming efforts, as breeders could potentially inform new owners about behaviors they might expect from their dogs, which might help avoid mismatches in expectations, and in so doing, help keep dogs in their homes.

Impact of early socialisation in foster care on kitten behaviour.
Campbell, Arnott, Graham, Niel, Ward, Ma · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2024

Abstract. Objectives: The critical socialisation period for kittens is estimated to be 2–7 weeks of age. Kittens reared in shelters often have poorer outcomes due to high rates of infectious disease. Therefore, many shelters are reliant on foster care programs to improve kitten survival rates and behaviour. However, little is known about the impact of specific early socialisation factors on kitten behaviour and health. Methods: During a 23-month period, 598 kitten foster carers completed a kitten socialisation questionnaire. The relationships between kitten weight/age at intake, days in care, singleton status, and the presence or absence of the queen during care on kitten behaviour and disease incidence were explored. Associations with exposure to noise, adults, children, location where the kitten was kept in the home, time spent alone, and ringworm treat- ment were also investigated. Results: Overall being kept in social areas of the house with less noise/activity and spending some time alone was associated with positive kitten behaviour. There was no difference in fear or anxiety responses for bottle fed kittens, singletons and those without the queen. Ringworm treatment was associated with more fearful behaviour in kittens. Conclusions: and relevance: Future foster carers should be encouraged to keep kittens in social areas of the house, whilst also ensuring kittens can spend some time alone. If the home is loud, carers should slowly introduce the kitten/s to the louder and busier areas of the house before being housed there all the time. Training of carers to use more positive reinforcement techniques for administering ringworm treatment may benefit kitten behaviour

Potential Benefits of a ‘Trauma-Informed Care’ Approach to Improve the Assessment and Management of Dogs Presented with Anxiety Disorders
Corridan · Animals · 2024

Abstract. Dog caregiver reporting on the spectrum of fearful–aggressive behaviours often describes ‘unpredictable’ or ‘exaggerated’ responses to a situation/animal/person. A possible explanation for these behavioural responses considers that the dog is reacting to triggered memories for which the dog has a negative association. For many dogs undergoing veterinary behavioural treatment or rehabilitation through a canine rescue organisation, the assessing clinician relies on “proxy” reporting of the history/background by a caregiver (dog owner, foster carer, or shelter personnel). Detailed information on the event or circumstances resulting in this negative association may be limited or absent altogether. Consideration of a trauma-informed care (TIC) approach, currently applied in a wide range of human psychology and social care fields, may be helpful in guiding the clinical approach taken. The literature relating to adverse early experience (AEE) and trauma-informed care (TIC) in puppies/dogs compared to children/adults was evaluated to identify common themes and conclusions identified across both species. In the absence of known/identifiable trauma, behavioural assessment and management should consider that a ‘problem’ dog may behave as it does, as the result of previous trauma. The dog can then be viewed through a lens of empathy and understanding, often lacking for dogs presenting with impulsive, reactive, or aggressive behaviours. Assessment must avoid re-traumatising the animal through exposure to triggering stimuli and, treatment options should include counselling of caregivers on the impact of adverse early experiences, consideration of the window of tolerance, and TIC behavioural modification techniques.

An investigation into the effectiveness of various professionals and behavior modification programs, with or without medication, for the treatment of canine fears
Dinwoodie · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2022

Abstract. In this follow up study, we investigate a subset of 1,308 dogs whose owners (n = 1,048) described as having at least one form of fearful/anxious behavior. Using a self-reported questionnaire, owners were also asked to indicate the resolutions employed, including training methods and equipment, behavior modification programs, behavior modification and training techniques, medications, and forms of alternative medicine. Owners sought professional help for 50% of the fearful or anxious dogs. Nearly a quarter of the dogs were brought to a veterinarian for help; 15% of which were diagnosed with a medical condition contributing to the dog's misbehavior. Overall, reward-based training, mental stimulation, and habituation were associated with increased odds of improvement. For the specific fear/anxiety-based problems, various consultants and techniques or treatments were found beneficial. Inanimate fears benefited from the use of benzodiazepines, herbal remedies, and dietary changes. Animate fears had increased odds of improvement if the dog was brought to a behavior consultant, use of a relaxation protocol, and systematic desensitization. Situational fears benefitted from mental stimulation, a relaxation protocol, and short, frequent training sessions. Generalized anxiety had increased odds of improvement with nutraceutical therapy and enrollment in dog sporting activities. Negative odds of improvement were found if the dog had pre-existing aggression in conditions involving inanimate fears, situational anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Neutering (male or female) reduced odds of improvement for animate fears, as did consultation with a veterinarian or non-veterinary behaviorist and the use of benzodiazepines. Changes in management had a negative effect on treatment of situational fear/anxiety. Consulting a trainer and hormone therapy reduced odds of improvement with generalized anxiety disorder. Paradoxically, odds of improvement for post-traumatic stress disorder were reduced when increasing a dog's exercise level was employed in treatment.

Secure base effect in former shelter dogs and other family dogs: Strangers do not provide security in a problem-solving task
Cimarelli · PLoS One · 2022

Abstract. Domestic dogs display behavioural patterns towards their owners that fulfil the four criteria of attachment. As such, they use their owners as a secure base, exploring the environment and manipulating objects more when accompanied by their owners than when alone. Although there are some indications that owners serve as a better secure base than other human beings, the evidence regarding a strong owner-stranger differentiation in a manipulative context is not straightforward. In the present study, we conducted two experiments in which pet dogs were tested in an object-manipulation task in the presence of the owner and of a stranger, varying how the human partner would behave (i.e. remaining silent or encouraging the dog, Experiment 1), and when alone (Experiment 2). Further, to gain a better insight into the mechanisms behind a potential owner-stranger differentiation, we investigated the effect of dogs' previous life history (i.e. having lived in a shelter or having lived in the same household since puppyhood). Overall, we found that strangers do not provide a secure base effect and that former shelter dogs show a stronger owner-stranger differentiation than other family dogs. As former shelter dogs show more behavioural signs correlated with anxiety towards the novel environment and the stranger, we concluded that having been re-homed does not necessarily affect the likelihood of forming a secure bond with the new owner but might have an impact on how dogs interact with novel stimuli, including unfamiliar humans. These results confirm the owner's unique role in providing security to their dogs and have practical implications for the bond formation in pet dogs with a past in a shelter.

Age- dependent changes in dogs’ (Canis familiaris) separation- related behaviours in a longitudinal study
Marx, A. · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Separation related problems (SRP) caused by distress associated with separation from the preferred member of the social group, can be characterised by their symptoms e.g., excessive vocalisation. In dogs’ separation whines, nonlinear phenomena (NLP) (abrupt changes in the resonance of the vocal folds) might occur, which could be adaptive in communicating aroused inner states. Previously, using a separation test we found that more dogs that were classified as having SRP by their owner have NLP in their whines than nonaffected dogs and that NLP ratio increases with age, which suggests that separation stress might intensify with age. We repeated the separation test 21.19 ± 9.37 months later with 32 dogs from the previous study to investigate longitudinally how separation behaviour and vocalisations change with age. Beside behaviour, we measured the acoustic structure of the whines (jitter - small-scale irregularity of the pitch, entropy - vocal harshness, call length, pitch (f0) related parameters, and the spectral components) and calculated the NLP ratio. We formed clusters based on the dogs’ behaviour changes from the first test to the second, to see individual ageing patterns. Finally, we compared the dogs’ behaviour and the acoustic structure of their whines in the two occasions. We found that dogs could be clustered by the changes in their separation behaviour. 41 % of the dogs were stable over time, 38 % improved, and 16 % showed an increase in their separation behaviours. 3 % switched from barking to whining. Interestingly, SRP dogs were stable, some of them even showed improvement in their separation behaviour. On the contrary, we also found that SRP dogs tended to have an increased NLP ratio with age from test 1 to test 2 (p = 0.09), showed less escape-related behaviour (p = 0.01), but tended to spend more time passively whining at the door through which the owner left the room (p = 0.05), than non-SRP dogs. The behavioural and vocal results suggest elevated stress levels in SRP dogs with age, although they did not decline but mostly stayed stable in their separation behaviour, confirming that there could be a connection between SRP status, age, and NLP. However, together with the results of the clustering that showed that there are different patterns in dogs’ separation behaviour, we emphasise the importance of individual level longitudinal investigations in order to facilitate the early diagnosis of SRP and to provide a solid basis for the development of individualised treatment plan for SRP dogs.

Digital Dogsitter® reduces vocalization in dogs suffering from separation- related problems
Tiira, K · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Separation-related behaviour problems are common in domestic dogs. Vocalization is one of the most common symptoms in dogs, and neighbors often complain about dog barking and howling. We investigated the effect of digital application Digital Dogsitter® on the dogs’ vocalization (barking, howling, whining) when left alone. Digital Dogsitter® is an application, used via laptop or pc, and it activates as a reaction to the dog’s vocalization and then plays a short owner-recorded feedback. Altogether 40 participants who had a laptop/pc at home and a dog that suffered from separation related symptoms, specifically vocalization, completed the study. Dogs’ vocalization (when alone at home) was recorded before and after using Digital Dogsitter® for two weeks, in order to investigate whether Digital Dogsitter® reduces dog’s vocalization. The amount of total dog vocalization noise (in milliseconds) was significantly reduced (P < 0.001, N = 40) after using Digital Dogsitter® for two weeks. The reduction in the dogs’ vocalization after Digital Dogsitter® was 95.7%, which is very large compared to earlier studies. Owners’ opinions were also asked, and only 9.4% of the owners felt that no improvement was seen. Finally, we wanted to know, whether the effect of Digital Dogsitter® was long lasting, and sent a questionnaire to participants an average of eight months after the first study. Out of 35 participants that responded, 68.7% felt that Digital Dogsitter® did reduce the vocalization either rather well / extremely well, and only 14.3% felt, that no long-lasting effect was noticed. In addition, after eight months the owners also reported significantly less dogs’ destructive behavior compared to the starting situation. Digital Dogsitter® clearly reduces dogs’ vocalization, possibly also alleviating the separation-related stress.

Pet dogs home alone: A video- based study
Stephan, G · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Many dogs are left alone at home by their owners for several hours on a daily basis. This practice is known to be a possible source of complication in dog keeping. Behaviourists and veterinarians are frequently consulted due to excessive vocalisations, destructive behaviour and house-soiling of dogs left alone by their owners. These observations are thought to be symptoms of a disorder which most authors refer to as separation anxiety or separation related behaviour. Little is known about dogs, which showed mostly inconspicuous behaviour during separation, and possible effects of the presence of conspecifics in multi-dog households, which may help coping with separation. Therefore, we videotaped 32 dogs in single-dog and 45 dogs in multi-dog households left home alone as part of their usual routine. We performed a continuous sampling of location, posture, vocalization, and various other behaviours. Calculating and comparing percentages of observation time we tested possible effects of familiar conspecifics, and investigated the effects of sex and neuter status, variables that are discussed to have an influence on the onset of separation anxiety. In general, our dogs showed low physical activity as well as a low vocal activity, independent from the time being alone. Male dogs in multi-dog households showed a significantly higher vocal activity. A further sex related difference was that male dogs were more prone to stay at the exit-door. This bias may be due to sex-specific motivations but seems to get stronger with increasing duration of separation. Dogs in multi-dog households showed a significant higher activity (single dog: 15.3 ± 14.3, multi-dog: 27.5 ± 26.1 mean ± SD). The major difference in activity was found in the first hour of being alone and interactions with other pets played only a marginal role even in multi-dog households (0.24 ± 0.6). Whining was not reduced by the presence of familiar conspecifics. Further research is needed to see if familiar conspecifics facilitate coping with separation from humans in this context. Our data suggest rather the opposite.

Risk-factors associated with veterinary- related fear and aggression in owned domestic dogs
Stellato, A. · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Fear and aggression in dogs within veterinary clinics can lead to canine welfare impairments and pose a safety concern for veterinary staff. However, few studies have explored potential underlying causes for this fear and aggression. We used an online cross-sectional survey targeted to current dog owners to examine risk-factors associated with fear and aggression within veterinary clinics. The factors we assessed in the survey were related to dog characteristics (e.g., sex, breed), fear and aggression responses in everyday scenarios (e.g., stranger-directed fear), experiences at the veterinary clinic (e.g., frequency of visits), training, early socialization, and owner demographics (e.g., gender, age). Data were analyzed using mixed linear regression models, with participant as a random effect to account for multiple dogs within the same household. For fear within veterinary clinics, dogs (n = 1346) were significantly more likely to be rated as fearful by their owners if they were neutered at ≤1 year of age; received their first nail trim at an older age; were rated as having severe non-social fear, or stranger-directed fear and aggression, or as being stressed or aggressive during body handling; had a negative change in behaviour after an aversive clinic experience; and if owners indicated being nervous during situations within the veterinary clinic. For aggression within veterinary clinics, dogs (n = 1776) were more likely to be rated as aggressive if they were reported to be fearful of the veterinary clinic, fearful of feet toweling, or stressed or aggressive during body handling, if the owner reported using positive punishment during routine training, or if any of the following had occurred during clinic visits: examination shortened, use of towel restraint or muzzling, dog bite directed at staff member, general use of positive punishment on the dog, and if the owner indicated being nervous during situations within the veterinary clinic. Random effects for participant were significant for both fear and aggression models, with intra-class correlation coefficients of 0.36 (CI: 0.21−0.54, p = 0.0004) and 0.16 (CI: 0.06−0.36, p = 0.017), respectively, indicating some correlation in behaviour amongst dogs owned by the same person. These results suggest that both fear and aggression within veterinary clinics are related to dog personality, previous early experiences and veterinary clinic experiences, and owner experiences during veterinary visits. These findings can be used to generate hypotheses for future research aimed at preventing the development of fear and aggression within a veterinary setting.
