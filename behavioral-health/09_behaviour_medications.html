Principles and When to Treat

Before reaching for a drug, the shelter veterinarian makes a behavioural diagnosis and plans the whole package: medication is never a substitute for behaviour modification, enrichment, and good housing.

Behaviour Medications in Shelter Animals

Speed

Pre-drug checklist

  • Make a behavioural diagnosis; know the indications, doses, side effects, and contraindications of the likely drugs. your notes
  • Prescribe a concurrent behaviour modification (BMOD) program and train staff to administer therapy. your notes
  • Plan to monitor for effect and side effects (inappetence, vomiting, diarrhoea, ataxia, sedation, increased aggression, serotonin syndrome). your notes
  • Account for expense, and plan adoption and follow-up. your notes
ASV: medication is never used in isolation Psychotropic medication must always be paired with a structured BMOD program, enrichment, and environmental modification. Prescribing drugs as a standalone fix for poor design or missing enrichment does not meet welfare standards. ASV

When drug therapy should be considered

  • When wellbeing is compromised by shelter life and enrichment (exercise, companionship, training) has not improved the animal's state. your notes
  • Anxious or fearful animals still hiding or trembling after 2 to 3 days and not eating, eliminating, or grooming, or that are overactive or compulsive. your notes
  • Dogs with separation-related problems (SRP) do not show signs in the shelter but still benefit from a behaviour program plus drug therapy. Urine marking is also reduced. your notes

The three P's and the working diagnosis

Behavioural pharmacology aims to address neurotransmitter alterations, decrease anxiety, fear, and arousal, and make behavioural and environmental modification easier. The trigger is assessed by Provocation (what is the trigger), Proportion (intensity of the reaction), and Persistence (ability to recover). your notes

Exam trap: recovery time and what we are changing A behaviourally normal, resilient dog should recover from a mild to moderately stressful event within a couple of minutes at most; longer recovery suggests an abnormal pattern or compromised welfare. Medication alters the negative emotional state underlying the behaviour, not the behaviour itself. your notes

Working diagnosis and arousal

  • Identify the behaviour, trigger, target, and motivation; recognising motivation informs the choice of fast versus short-acting and long versus short-term medication. your notes
  • High physiologic arousal (hackles up, dilated pupils, hard panting, heavy salivation, poor recovery) may respond better to clonidine than to trazodone. your notes

Medication choice and monitoring

  • Weigh administration, cost, availability, abuse potential, and legal requirements. A short-term agent is ideal, but an underlying disorder, a long stay, or anticipated future problems may favour a long-term baseline drug. your notes
  • Assign someone to monitor frequency, duration, and intensity of the behaviours of interest, and plan for the animal going home. your notes
  • A written management and education plan accompanies verbal instructions: treatment goals, how to give the drug, side effects to watch for, and who to follow up with. your notes

Disorders and First-Line Treatment

Match the drug to the disorder, always alongside behaviour modification and enrichment.

DisorderFirst-line approach
Anxiety (cat)Enrichment, alternative housing, or foster first; if a fearful cat keeps hiding (even in the litter box) despite hide and perch boxes, a benzodiazepine, with alprazolam preferred at 0.125 mg per cat once or twice daily
Anxiety (dog)Fluoxetine or paroxetine once daily
Compulsive disorderClomipramine or fluoxetine, plus BMOD stressing consistency and activity
SRPShort-departure program plus fluoxetine or clomipramine
Urine markingFluoxetine (dogs and cats); buspirone gives about a 50% reduction in cats

Compulsive disorder detail

  • Repetitive, sustained, out-of-context behaviour: tail chasing, pacing, weaving, self-licking, leg or foot chewing, self-directed aggression, cage licking, persistent barking; in cats, tail chasing or chewing and overgrooming. your notes
  • Genetic predisposition exists (German Shepherd and Staffordshire to tail chase, Doberman to flank suck), and signs appear with prolonged confinement and during transitions. your notes
Exam trap: duration and weaning for compulsive disorder Continue drug therapy for at least 3 weeks after a satisfactory reduction is seen, then wean gradually over 3 to 4 weeks while continuing behaviour modification. your notes

Pheromone therapy (Feliway, Adaptil) is a useful adjunct; details are in the Adjuncts tab. your notes

Neurotransmitters

Most behaviour drugs act on one of a handful of neurotransmitter systems. Knowing the system explains the indication, the side effects, and the dangerous combinations.

The systems

  • Glutamate: the primary excitatory transmitter; NMDA is one receptor type. NMDA blockers (ketamine, memantine) give analgesia and address pain-associated anxiety; high doses cause a dissociative or cataleptic state. your notes
  • GABA: synthesised from glutamate; the major inhibitory transmitter; increasing GABA is sedating and controls vigilance, anxiety, muscle tension, seizures, and memory. Benzodiazepines act here; flumazenil reverses them. your notes
  • Dopamine: reward, motivation, bonding, attention, voluntary movement. Excess gives stereotypy and compulsion; deficiency gives reduced alertness, anxiety, depression, and parkinsonian signs. your notes
  • Noradrenaline (NE): from dopamine; mood and arousal. Alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine, xylazine) inhibit NE release for sedation, analgesia, and (at low doses) anxiolysis. your notes
  • Adrenaline (epinephrine): the acute stress response. Beta-blockers (propranolol) block the physical signs of fear; alpha-2 agonists reduce fear by blocking NE. your notes
  • Serotonin (5-HT): from tryptophan; controls mood, impulsivity, anxiety, panic, satiety, nausea, cognition, aggression, sleep, pain, and thermoregulation. Low serotonin gives depression, anxiety, and aggression. your notes
  • Acetylcholine: the only transmitter not made from an amino acid (choline plus acetyl-CoA); supports memory, arousal, and attention. TCA anticholinergic effects give dry mouth, dry eyes, urine retention, constipation, mydriasis, and increased heart rate. your notes

Core Drug Classes

The five classes you will be tested on most: baseline SSRIs and TCAs, and the situational benzodiazepines, alpha-2 agonists, and trazodone.

SSRIs

  • Fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram. They block serotonin reuptake; full effect takes 4 to 8 weeks. Fewer cardiac effects and may be safer in seizure-prone pets, but can reduce thyroid hormone. your notes
  • Indications: sertraline (panic, generalised anxiety, compulsive); fluvoxamine and fluoxetine (hyperactivity, aggression, compulsive); paroxetine (impulsivity, aggression, ritualistic). your notes
  • Fluoxetine: separation anxiety, aggression, feline urine marking, compulsive disorder; dogs 1 to 2 mg/kg SID, cats 0.5 to 1 mg/kg SID; can alter blood glucose (caution in diabetics); wean if used over 4 weeks. your notes
Exam trap: the fluoxetine washout Fluoxetine has a very long half-life in dogs (at least 5 days), so switching from fluoxetine to an MAOI (selegiline) needs a 5-week washout to avoid serotonin syndrome. your notes

TCAs

  • Clomipramine, amitriptyline, doxepin. They inhibit NE and serotonin reuptake and carry anticholinergic, antihistaminic, and alpha-1 effects, so side effects are broader; they also lower the seizure threshold and are contraindicated in seizure disorders. your notes
  • Clomipramine (Clomicalm): the strongest serotonergic TCA; licensed for canine separation anxiety with a behaviour plan; 2 to 4 mg/kg q24h (q12h may work better); a 2-week washout when switching to an MAOI; ineffective for owner-related aggression. your notes
  • Amitriptyline: traditionally used for cats urinating outside the box (cost and familiarity) and may help feline interstitial cystitis via NE-mediated pain reduction. Doxepin: high antihistaminic effect, used mainly for pruritus. your notes

Benzodiazepines

  • Act on GABA-A; onset 1 to 2 hours, lasting a few hours up to 8 to 10. Mild sedative at low doses, anxiolytic at moderate, hypnotic at high. Indicated for intense anxiety or panic (storms, noise phobia, separation, car rides, feline spraying). About 67% effective for anxiety cases. your notes
  • Specific agents: alprazolam for acute fear and panic (and with clomipramine for noise phobia); clonazepam and clorazepate for chronic generalised anxiety; oxazepam as a feline appetite stimulant; chlordiazepoxide for feline urine spraying. your notes
  • Drug-specific doses from your formulary: alprazolam 0.02 to 0.1 mg/kg PO PRN or q8 to 12h (cat 0.125 to 0.25 mg per cat PO q12h); lorazepam 0.02 to 0.1 mg/kg PO PRN or q8 to 24h (cat 0.25 mg per cat PO PRN). Oral diazepam is generally avoided in cats because of the rare risk of fatal acute hepatic necrosis. your notes
  • Disinhibition can increase exploration and appetite and reduce avoidance, but can also increase aggression, so use caution and basket-muzzle or crate training in animals with an aggression history. Paradoxical excitation is best discovered with owners present. your notes
  • Rare acute hepatic necrosis can occur in cats after oral diazepam, so screen liver function at baseline and recheck 3 to 5 days after starting. Always wean (10 to 25% per week) to avoid anxiety, anorexia, tremors, or seizures. your notes

Alpha-2 agonists

  • Clonidine, dexmedetomidine, detomidine. They inhibit NE release in the locus ceruleus; anxiolysis can occur at doses below those needed for sedation. Clonidine is given 1.5 to 2 hours before an event, up to twice daily; reversible with atipamezole. your notes
  • Dexmedetomidine oromucosal gel (Sileo) is labelled for canine noise aversion and still helps once fear has begun (onset at least 30 minutes, more like 1 to 2 hours); useful for poor intake coping and noisy transport. your notes
  • It is not suitable for severe fear with handling because it must be given oromucosally; the site goes pale from vasoconstriction, and if swallowed, high first-pass metabolism makes the effect low and the overdose risk low. Contraindicated with cardiac disease or NE-raising drugs. your notes

Trazodone (SARI)

  • A serotonin 2A antagonist and reuptake inhibitor that also blocks histamine and alpha-1; a fast-acting situational drug and a bridge while a baseline takes effect. Used for generalised and separation anxiety, storm phobia, and calming hospitalised pets. your notes
  • Avoid before a neurologic examination, and use caution with impaired primary haemostasis (it decreases platelet aggregation). Dog 2 to 5 mg/kg PRN or q12h (3 to 4 mg/kg q12h for kennel stress, generally under 10 mg/kg daily); cats 12.5 to 50 mg per cat (onset about 3 hours in cats versus 1 to 2 in dogs, so trial the dose first). your notes
  • Low-dose intake trazodone in the first 48 hours has been associated with lower respiratory illness, shorter stays, and higher adoption. std ref
Exam trap: trazodone is dose-dependent Trazodone gives targeted anxiety reduction at the lower end of its range. At higher doses the effect at serotonin receptors becomes less specific, so sedation, hypnotic effect, and side effects increase. Do not just raise the dose to chase the hypnotic effect. your notes

Other Agents and Serotonin Syndrome

The secondary agents, the hormonal and lithium options, and the one toxicity you must recognise instantly.

Buspirone (azapirone)

  • A partial serotonin 5-HT1A agonist; a mild anxiolytic for taking the edge off and building the confidence of an overly fearful pet, useful alongside an SSRI or clomipramine. your notes
  • It does NOT impair motor function or cognition (a good first choice for mild chronic fear), gives about a 50% reduction in feline urine marking, but can disinhibit and increase aggression. Dose about 0.5 to 1 mg/kg BID (2 mg/kg for pretravel); weeks to effect. your notes
Exam trap: where buspirone fails Buspirone is NOT indicated for panic disorders and is not proven for intense fear, phobias, compulsive disorders, or canine aggression. your notes

Gabapentin

  • An alpha-2-delta-1 ligand that reduces release of glutamate, NE, and substance P. It is structurally similar to GABA but does NOT act at the GABA receptor. Used for neuropathic pain and seizures, with anxiolytic properties; dog 10 to 30 mg/kg PRN or TID; sedation usually resolves. your notes
  • Effective for feline fear and anxiety before transport or vet visits. your notes
Exam trap: xylitol in human gabapentin Human oral liquid gabapentin commonly contains xylitol, which is toxic to dogs. Always use a xylitol-free suspension for canine patients. your notes

MAOIs and selegiline

  • MAOIs prevent breakdown of NE, dopamine, and serotonin. Selegiline selectively inhibits MAO-B in the dog, used for canine cognitive dysfunction, some pituitary-dependent hyperadrenocorticism, and fearful dogs with strong behavioural inhibition; dog 0.5 to 1 mg/kg SID in the morning. your notes
  • Mandatory washout before an MAOI: 5 weeks from fluoxetine, 2 weeks from clomipramine, and avoid all other serotonergic agents. your notes

Mirtazapine

  • A post-synaptic tetracyclic that enhances NE and 5-HT1A serotonergic transmission via alpha-2 antagonism; a serotonin receptor antagonist, so less likely to cause serotonin syndrome. Antiemetic and appetite-stimulating. your notes
  • Cats 1.88 to 3.75 mg per cat q72h; dogs about 0.6 to 1.3 mg/kg q24h, equivalent to 3.75 mg (under 10 kg (22 lb)) up to 15 mg (over 35 kg (77.2 lb)), to a maximum of 30 mg total daily. your notes
Corrected per your approval: mirtazapine maximum Your notes read "0.6 mg/kg up to a max of 30 mg/kg." The maximum is 30 mg TOTAL daily, not per kilogram; the per-kg figure was a transcription error (verified against veterinary dosing references, where the canine dose is about 0.6 to 1.3 mg/kg q24h to a maximum of 30 mg daily). The dog tablet range of 3.75 to 15 mg is consistent with this cap. corrected

Antihistamines, hormonal therapy, lithium

  • Antihistamines (hydroxyzine, diphenhydramine, others) for mild anxiety, overactivity, or sleep; anticholinergic, so dry mouth and constipation, contraindicated with glaucoma, urine retention, or hyperthyroidism. Cyproheptadine is an antihistamine with antiserotonergic effects and is an appetite stimulant. your notes
  • Hormonal therapy (progestins) for feline inappropriate urination and some anxiety or aggression, but serious side effects (diabetes mellitus, adrenocortical and bone-marrow suppression, mammary disease). your notes
  • Lithium for some forms of severe unpredictable aggression; highly toxic with a narrow window (therapeutic 0.8 to 1.2 mEq/L). your notes

Serotonin syndrome

Exam trap: serotonin syndrome triggers and antidote It occurs when serotonergic drugs stack (SSRI plus TCA plus MAOI, or with tramadol, amitraz, St John's wort, buspirone, and others). Signs resolve within 48 hours if the dose is halved or stopped; the specific antagonist is cyproheptadine (1.1 mg/kg every 1 to 4 hours), with diazepam for seizures and IV fluids for cardiovascular support. your notes

Adjuncts (Administered Non-Pharmaceutical Agents)

Lower-risk agents that are given to the animal to support, and sometimes substitute for, drug therapy.

PheromonesAct via the vomeronasal organ; cat F3 (cheek) and F4 (appeasing) and dog-appeasing pheromone, with weak to moderate evidence. Spray lasts about 6 hours, a diffuser covers 50 to 70 square metres (538.2 to 753.5 sq ft) for 30 days; good for small shelter rooms with negative-pressure airflow

The Use of Psychoactive Medications and Non-medication Alternatives for Cats and Dogs in North American Animal Shelters and Rescues
The Use of Psychoactive Medications and Non-medication · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. Introduction: Animals in shelters commonly experience fear, anxiety, and stress. Psychoactive medications and non-medication alternative options are recommended and used in shelters to manage these negative affective states, yet little is known about the current practices, perceptions, and barriers surrounding their use. Methods: This study investigated the use and perceptions of psychoactive medications and non-medication alternative options in animal shelters and rescues across Canada, the United States, and Mexico. An online survey collected responses from 233 participants representing 148 unique shelters, focusing on the frequency of use, comfort administering, and efficacy, as well as barriers and considerations influencing psychoactive medication and non-medication alternative use decisions. Results: Results showed that dogs experiencing fear were more frequently treated with psychoactive medications than cats experiencing fear. Gabapentin and fluoxetine were the most commonly used medications for fearful cats, while trazodone and gabapentin were the most commonly used for fearful dogs, with respondents reporting high ratings for both comfort in administering these medications to animals, and perceived efficacy of these medications at reducing anxiety in animals. Pheromones and nutraceuticals were the most commonly used non-medication alternative options. There was high agreement with the statement that admittance to an animal shelter is the main cause of behavior and welfare problems, and that welfare concerns are a key justification for providing psychoactive medication. Cost and uncertainty about the efficacy of treatments emerged as leading barriers to psychoactive medication administration. Specific characteristics, including the respondent’s role and type of shelter, influenced perceptions about psychoactive medications and alternatives, and barriers to their use. Conclusion: These findings highlight the importance of further understanding the efficacy and safety of psychoactive medication and alternatives, as well as addressing the barriers to their administration, to improve overall welfare outcomes for cats and dogs in shelter and rescue environments. Effiicacy of a pheremone-impregnated collar in coltrolling feline problem behavior, and an assessment of adverse events associated with collar use
Endersby et al. · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2024

Abstract. The aim of this study is to assess a pheromone complex-impregnated collar in the control of feline problem behaviors, and to assess the ease of use and tolerance of the collar compared with a regular collar. Six hundred and twenty-four cats from 459 households with one or more of four problem behaviors (problem urination, scratching, fear, or inter-cat conflict) were recruited to a 28-day study. Households were randomly assigned so that each cat received either a pheromone-impregnated polymer collar (containing 13% FELIWAY® Optimum) or a control regular silicone collar. Caregivers completed online questionnaires at recruitment and on days 7, 14, and 28 documenting the frequency and intensity of the problem behavior in the previous 7 days, and documenting any loss, problems and tolerance of the collar (pheromone-impregnated or control). Complete data was available for 491 cats for assessment of efficacy. Compared with the control collar, the pheromone collar produced significantly better improvement in problem urination (P = 0.0172), scratching (P = 0.0013), and inter-cat conflict (P = 0.0029). There was also a greater, but non-significant improvement in problem fear scores (P = 0.063). Collars had been removed definitively or lost from 12.1% of cats, for various reasons, by the end of the study, and potential adverse reactions were reported in 27.2% of cats, but again, with no difference in the overall frequency reported between the two collar groups. In a controlled study, a FELIWAY® Optimum-impregnated collar was shown to be effective in helping to manage a range of problem feline behaviors. The use of the pheromone collar was not associated with a higher level of adverse reaction reporting, but caregiver removal or loss of collars may present an obstacle for effective therapy through this means.

Use of mirtazapine in the treatment of canine behaviour problems: A review of 32 cases
Arguelles et al. · Veterinary Record · 2024

Abstract. Background

Canine behaviour problems seen by speciality behavioural medicine services often involve chronic anxiety disorders that have resulted in maladaptation of the individual to its environment. Common stressors include the presence of other individuals (other dogs or people), noise and being alone. The treatment of these behavioural problems usually includes a combination of behaviour modification, environmental modification and biological therapies. Within the latter, anxiolytic drugs such as clomipramine or fluoxetine have proven useful. Methods

Here, we present a retrospectively analysed series of 32 cases that were treated with the anxiolytic drug mirtazapine, which is widely used in human medicine but has not previously been reported for the treatment of behavioural problems in dogs (although it is marketed as an appetite stimulant in cats). Cases included dogs with a range of anxiety-related behavioural problems. Results

Eighty-one percent of dogs that presented with a behavioural problem showed improvement and suspected adverse effects were mild and tolerable. Limitations

Further studies are required to isolate this result from the other therapeutic measures and to compare its efficacy with other drugs. Conclusion

Mirtazapine appears to be a suitable and safe option for the treatment of anxiety-related behavioural problems in dogs.

Gabapentin: Clinical Use and Pharmacokinetics in Dogs, Cats, and Horses
Dicesare et al. · Animals · 2023

Abstract. Gabapentin is an anticonvulsant drug, which presents an established clinical efficacy in human patients for the management of refractory partial seizures, secondarily generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and for the control of chronic neuropathic pain. Gabapentin was synthesized as a structural analogue of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, with GABA-mimetic effects, able to cross the blood–brain barrier. In veterinary medicine, is extra-label used in combination with other treatments to control seizures when other drugs are no longer effective or become toxic or for neuropathic pain treatment and anxiety. This review aimed to clarify gabapentin use and pharmacokinetic aspects to promote conscious use in dogs, cats, and horses. In dogs, gabapentin was beneficial in the treatment of epilepsy, as well as chronic, neuropathic, and post-operative pain, as well as anxiety. In cats, it showed efficacy in post-ovariohysterectomy-related pain and in anxiety management. In horses, gabapentin has been administered as an analgesic for chronic pain management. In conclusion, when used in combination with other drugs, gabapentin can be considered an interesting therapeutic option for the treatment of neuropathic diseases and analgesia in postoperative and chronic pain. However, despite its beneficial use in different clinical settings, further trials and pharmacokinetic studies are needed for the definition of an effective dosage regimen through proper pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic correlation in dogs, cats, and horses.

An Interdisciplinary Approach for Compulsive Behavior in Dogs: A Case Report
d'Angelo · Frontiers · 2022

Abstract. Compulsive disorder is a debilitating condition affecting both humans and animals, characterized by intrusive thoughts and recurring out-of-place behaviors. Among them, tail chasing might represent one of the most common traits in compulsive dogs. Herein, we reported the case of a 7-year-old intact male German Shepherd mixed-breed dog, presenting with tail chasing behavior. He underwent a first behavioral evaluation 1 year before (at the age of 6), when he injured himself with severe wounds at level of the tail and left thigh. To avoid any specific suffering and increase his physical health, of course, the study was carried out through an interdisciplinary approach, employing a veterinary behaviorist and a rehabilitating dog instructor. Three months after pharmacological treatment with fluoxetine and α-s1 casozepine, associated with a behavioral recovery program, the owner reported an improvement of compulsive events in his dog, in terms of intensity and frequency. Interestingly, over the following 3 months, the dog did not experience any new tail chasing episodes.

Double-blind, placebo- controlled trial of venlafaxine to treat behavioural disorders in cats: a pilot study
Metz D et al. · · 2022

Abstract. Objectives Venlafaxine, a specific inhibitor of both noradrenaline and serotonin, is commonly used in human medicine to treat depression, anxiety and social phobia. Its formulation in small granules renders it interesting to test on cats, which are usually reluctant to take medication. Venlafaxine was administered at 1 mg/kg for 60 days, using a double-blind, placebo-controlled protocol, to cats aged ⩾6 months exhibiting aggressiveness, fear or house-soiling. Methods After one cat’s withdrawal, 21 cats were included in the study: 11 in the venlafaxine group and 10 in the placebo group. Three consultations were conducted, on day 0, day 30 and day 60. Each visit consisted of (1) veterinarian- and owner-based scoring of the cat’s behavioural improvement; (2) scoring of the cat’s compliance with removal from its carrier and compliance with manipulation; and (3) owner scoring of the ease of administration and recording of the potential adverse effects of the treatment. Results Improvement was significantly higher in the venlafaxine group; as early as day 30, according to the veterinarian scoring, and at day 60, according to both the veterinarian and owner scoring. In contrast, neither the removal nor the manipulation scores were significantly different between the two treatment groups. Venlafaxine seemed to improve all three studied signs, as early as day 30 for fear and aggressiveness, and at day 60 for house-soiling. The adverse effects of venlafaxine were limited to drowsiness in one cat. All cats tolerated the treatment well during the 60-day period. Conclusions and relevance These results suggest that venlafaxine is efficient in treating several behavioural problems and is easy to administer. More studies should be conducted to explore its effects at different dosages on specific diagnoses.

Effects of a Novel Gel Formulation of Dog Appeasing Pheromone (DAP) on Behavioral and Physiological Stress Responses in Dogs Undergoing Clinical Examination
Puglisi et al. · Animals · 2022

Abstract. The veterinary visit is necessary for safeguarding the health of dogs, but it can be stressful and threaten both the welfare of the patient and the accuracy of the examination. This randomized, triple-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study aims at evaluating how dog appeasing pheromone (DAP) in a novel gel formulation influences the behavioral and physiological stress responses of 28 dogs undergoing a standardized clinical examination, while staying in the waiting room (WR) and visited in the examination room (ER). Behavioral responses were studied through behavioral categories and subjective scales (WR and ER). Autonomic response considered heart rate (WR and ER), blood pressure (WR and ER), respiratory rate (ER), and rectal temperature (ER). Neuroendocrine response considered salivary cortisol (WR and ER). In the waiting room, the use of DAP was associated with a significant reduction of lip licking (p = 0.0189), an increase in panting (p = 0.0276), and a reduction close to significance (p = 0.0584) of low body postures. No significant differences were observed within the physiological responses. In the examination room, neither behavioral nor physiological differences were found.

Effects of a Nutritional Supplement (DìRelaxTM) on Anxiety in Dogs in a Randomized Control Trial Design
Scandurra A · Animals · 2022

Abstract. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy of DìRelaxTM, a nutraceutical formulated to reduce anxiety in dogs, using a randomized controlled trial (RCT) design. The C-BARQ questionnaire, some clinical investigations, and the impossible task test were performed in dogs before and after treatment. The C-BARQ questionnaire is particularly useful for assessing the frequency and severity of problematic behaviors. The impossible task paradigm provides insight into the decision-making processes in the realm of expectancy frustration. Results showed an ameliorative effect on the performances of treated dogs during the solvable phases, with a significant decrease in the time needed to solve the task. No behavioral difference was found between treated and untreated anxious dogs during the unsolvable phase. According to the results from the C-BARQ questionnaire, some of the behaviors appeared to improve. Clinical investigations, including a complete blood cell count and blood chemistry, showed no difference between groups, thus suggesting the safety of the product. In general, this study suggests that DìRelaxTM can be safely administered with no adverse effects and can exercise a beneficial effect on anxious dogs by enhancing their cognitive abilities, but further studies should investigate the best method of administration.

Effects of a single dose of orally administered gabapentin in dogs during a veterinary visit: a double- blinded, placebo-controlled study
Stolar O et al. · · 2022

Abstract. OBJECTIVE To evaluate the effects of a single dose of orally administered gabapentin in alleviating stress at a veterinary visit in privately owned dogs. Animals 22 healthy client-owned dogs (1.5 to 8.5 years old) were enrolled in this study. PROCEDURES Each dog received a 50-mg/kg oral dose of either gabapentin or placebo 2 hours before the beginning of each visit protocol. The dog’s behavioral responses were coded from recorded video clips during a 5-minute-long standardized physical examination and pre– and post–physical examination phases. The veterinary technician separately rated each greeting behavior at each visit. Physiological variables during veterinary visits (ie, eye surface temperature and salivary cortisol concentrations) were also compared between the pre– and post–physical examination phases. The owner was queried 24 hours after a visit to determine the incidence of adverse events. RESULTS The greeting test score, eye surface temperature, and cortisol concentrations did not differ substantially between the gabapentin and placebo treatment groups. Lip licking frequency during the physical examination phase was significantly lower in the gabapentin treatment group than in the placebo group (P = 0.001). Abstract. OBJECTIVE To determine whether a single dose of trazodone administered to dogs before a veterinary visit reduced their behavioral and physiologic signs of stress and owners’ stress during veterinary visits. Psycho-Behavioural Changes in Dogs Treated with Corticosteroids: A Clinical Behaviour Perspective
Notari L · Animals · 2022

Abstract. Arousal and distress are often important factors in problematic behaviours, and endogenous corticosteroids are important mediators in the associated stress responses. Exogenous corticosteroid treatments have been reported to change behaviour in human patients and laboratory animals, with similar changes also noted in pet dogs. These behaviours not only potentially adversely impact the welfare of the dogs, but also the quality of life of their owners. Indeed, corticosteroids can bias sensitivity towards aversion in dogs. In this paper, the possible behavioural side effects of exogenous corticosteroids are summarised to help inform and support veterinarians prescribing these drugs.

Acute trazodone administration reduces behavioral and physiological indicators of travel-induced anxiety in dogs in a road travel model
Landsberg G, Mendes J, Kosziwka W, Keys DA, Gordon A, King A, Araujo JA, de Rivera C · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2026

Abstract. Travel-induced stress and anxiety are prevalent in dogs and result in behavioral changes that negatively affect well-being. This study assessed whether trazodone or gabapentin could reduce stress during road travel.
