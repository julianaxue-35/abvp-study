Intake, Environment and Housing

The ASV Guidelines frame assessment as a continuous process that begins before admission and runs through the whole stay: collect history, manage the environment to minimise fear and stress, house appropriately, keep routines predictable, and treat enrichment as essential, not optional.

Assessment and Decision-Making

Speed
ASV: intake Collecting information before admission lets the shelter offer services that may prevent intake (outpatient behavioural care, rehoming resources, spay-neuter, return to field). Personnel must collect a thorough behavioural history at or near intake, for every animal regardless of source, including the reasons for surrender and previously observed behaviour. Available information about aggression must be recorded with an objective description of the actions and circumstances. ASV
Exam trap: intake communication Intake staff should be trained in communication techniques that reduce social desirability bias: asking open-ended questions, using objective language, and active listening. Expect a stem testing the open-ended plus objective-language pairing. ASV

Environmental management and housing

  • Create an environment that minimises stimuli that induce fear, stress, and frustration; shelters must have written policies supporting mental wellbeing. ASV
  • Housing has a large impact on welfare; novel environments are especially stressful for shy, under-socialised, or geriatric cats and dogs. ASV
  • Prey species must be housed away from predatory species at all times (olfactory, auditory, and visual separation). Note that cats are predators but may also be prey to dogs. ASV

Daily routine and predictability

A consistent, structured environment that minimises reassignment of enclosures, caregivers, and schedules protects against chronic fear and anxiety. ASV

Exam trap: drivers of unpredictability The three named sources are lack of routine in daily care, frequent disruption of enclosure set-up, and irregular patterns of continuous light or darkness. The light or darkness item is the one most often omitted. ASV

Enrichment and socialisation

Enrichment provides social interaction, physical and mental stimulation, opportunities for species-typical behaviour, and choice and control over the environment. ASV

  • Successful programs promote emotional wellbeing and reduce undesirable behaviours. ASV
  • Enrichment must be given the same significance as nutrition and medical care, and is never optional. ASV

Dog Behaviour Assessment and Information Sources

Behaviour assessment is the process of collecting information about an individual animal's behaviour to understand its needs, address welfare concerns, and match it to the right outcome. Over the past two decades, formal evaluation tests have been shown to predict future behaviour poorly, so the current recommendation is a mixed, longitudinal model: objective history plus objective observation across many contexts.

Why formal testing for every animal is no longer recommended Behaviour evaluation tests fail to reliably predict future behaviour, particularly aggression, in a new home. Running one stressful subtest after another can worsen results and harm welfare, and formal testing consumes time and resources and can raise length of stay. Named test batteries: SAFER, Assess-a-Pet, Match-up II. your notes A pilot 9-week shelter training program found a standard temperament test insensitive to real training-related change (no difference in Human Sociability or Playfulness scores despite genuine skill gains), while a purpose-built basic-education test correctly detected the improvement — a standardised test is not automatically fit for every measurement purpose. (de Castro, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below) Generalisability is also a problem: a standardised puppy behaviour test that predicted guide-dog success in its original population provided no predictive value at all when applied to a different guide-dog organisation's puppies. (Feng, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below)

(Hare, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below)
Exam trap: post-adoption correlations over time At 2 weeks, surrender reports correlate with aggression toward unfamiliar people, urination when left alone, and destructive chewing. At 6 weeks there are fewer correlations overall. Dogs reported by owners NOT to guard resources were similarly unlikely to guard at home. your notes

Observation across contexts

Medical examination

  • Vet exams are stressful for most dogs: close to 80% of owned dogs show fear during a standardised exam, and dogs without their owners are significantly more stressed. your notes
  • A dog reported fearful at the vet (55%) is also likely to show fear in new situations (68%), pain or touch sensitivity (35%), and non-social fear (37%). your notes
  • Exam observation is most useful for learning how the dog responds to stress and for building a handling or behaviour-modification plan, interpreted alongside other contexts. your notes

Daily care staff and volunteers

  • Untrained daily-care observation can be inaccurate: staff correctly predicted only about one third of problems later reported by adopters, while behavioural tests predicted almost 75%. your notes
  • A likely reason is reluctance to disclose negative information that could hurt a dog's adoption chances. your notes
  • Close to half of staff mistook aggression for excitement or frustration, but rarely the reverse, so people tend to judge dogs positively. Fearful dogs were recognised well by experts and non-experts alike. your notes
  • Experts and non-experts were equally poor at telling play from aggression. Non-experts focus on the head; trainers attend to the body and read motivation and emotion better. Training improves non-expert proficiency, and video improves learning more than photos and text. your notes

Walks, socialisation, in-kennel, playgroups, foster

  • Walks and field trips reveal kennel-exit enthusiasm, leash and harness tolerance, interest in handlers and environment, and reactions to passers-by, children, joggers, cyclists, and skateboarders. your notes
  • In-kennel: even friendly dogs may show aggression when confined. Aggression toward people is HIGHEST when the dog is in its kennel; aggression toward dogs tends to be lowest there. Monitoring in-kennel behaviour gives early warning. your notes
  • Playgroups: dogs that joined a playgroup before evaluation reacted more positively to a stimulus dog, and regular adult interactions reduced aggression in evaluation. your notes
  • Foster and returns are the most accurate reflection of home behaviour. Dogs returning from foster were about 20 times more likely to be adopted or transferred, and about 85% of dogs returned within 30 days were quickly re-adopted. Sleepovers reveal little, since most dogs just sleep. your notes

Behaviour Evaluation Science

If a shelter chooses to use a standardised evaluation, it should fare well on feasibility, reliability, and validity, and the results must be read against the prevalence of the behaviour of concern. The mathematics of low-prevalence testing is the single most testable idea in this section.

Three criteria of a good evaluation

CriterionWhat it means
FeasibilityBalance information against throughput: no longer than 30 minutes, no more than 2 handlers and a stimulus dog, a documented step-by-step protocol, and an easy scoring system.
ReliabilityIntra-rater, inter-rater (IRR), test-retest, and internal consistency. Good IRR implies good intra-rater reliability. Evaluator sex and position (standing, kneeling, sitting) change the dog's response.
ValiditySensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values, all of which depend on prevalence in the tested population.
Exam trap: choosing predictive value by cost of error For aggression, favour a test with higher NEGATIVE predictive value, because adopting out a missed aggressive dog is unacceptable. For low-cost problems (mild fear, mouthiness), balance positive and negative predictive value. At low prevalence most errors are false positives; at high prevalence most are false negatives. your notes
Reference: the coin-flip paper Patronek and Bradley simulated that as many as 78% of dogs flagged aggressive on an evaluation are likely false positives, and even at 85% sensitivity and specificity the false-positive share is about 48%. The paper recommends discontinuing standardised behaviour evaluations and underlines that aggression must be safely corroborated in additional contexts. std ref

Subtests

  • Handling: no procedure proven superior. Begin with friendly, non-invasive touch (shoulders, side, rump), then advance to less pleasant contact (paw lift, toe-webbing pinch, muzzle hold, mouth open, thigh pinch) to mimic grooming or a vet visit. your notes
  • Play: short bursts of tag or tug reveal whether arousal becomes unruly play, mouthiness, sexual behaviour, or aggression. Dogs never kept as pets may not know how to play. your notes
  • Food guarding (assess-a-hand): controversial. The test predicts ABSENCE better, with 78% of non-guarders in the test also non-guarding at home. Many shelter food-guarders did not guard in the home, adopters cared little, and suspending the test did not change the roughly 9% return rate. your notes
  • Doll test: a toddler-sized doll simulates a child approaching. Controversial; mimics tethering and child bites. your notes
  • Threatening stranger: sensitive to tester sex, and most informative with a MALE tester, since dogs tend to be more reactive to men. your notes
  • Dog-dog: the stimulus dog must be moderately social and tolerant; watch for a friendly stimulus dog turning defensive after repeated tests. your notes
  • Model (fake) dog: good agreement for friendliness, moderate for fearfulness, POOR for aggressiveness. Useful as an initial screen, not a sole predictor. your notes

Cat Behaviour Assessment

For cats the first problem is telling a frightened pet cat from an unsocialised one, then predicting how a socialised cat will behave at home. Several frameworks exist, each with trade-offs between data quality and resource cost.

The discrimination problem

  • Minimum hold times to decide feral versus frightened range from 1 day to more than 7 days, with potential euthanasia for feral cats after the hold. your notes
  • A good predictive assessment could cut animal-care days, improve welfare, and raise live-release rates. your notes

Exam trap: structured feline test metrics A structured test yields a rating of severity (1 to 5), frequency, duration, proportion, and latency (for example, meowed 4 times, hid in the box for 60% of the period, approached after 37 seconds). your notes

Hiding box

Providing a hiding box facilitated a quicker reduction in stress after arrival; cats with boxes were more likely to approach the cage front and spent less time behind the litter box. Optimising shelter conditions is one of the most impactful ways to improve cat wellbeing. your notes

Risk, Behaviour Modification and Outcomes

When behaviour or welfare concerns arise, shelters develop an individualised plan, provide behavioural support, and make timely outcome decisions. Training and modification must follow least-intrusive, minimally-aversive principles, and risk to people must be addressed promptly.

ASV: training and behaviour modification Training MUST follow Least Intrusive, Minimally Aversive (LIMA) principles and the Humane Hierarchy of Behaviour Change. Positive reinforcement improves health, welfare, and adoptability; punishment-based methods can increase fear, anxiety, and aggression and compromise safety and welfare. It is unacceptable to use physical force as punishment. ASV

Behaviour medication

  • Must be strongly considered for emotional-health welfare concerns, for immediate intake or housing stress, or for long-term problems (separation-related, fear of people, chronic shelter stress). ASV
  • Concerns must be objectively assessed and diagnosed, with clear treatment goals, under veterinary advice or written protocols, and dispensed per regulation. ASV
  • Goal: improve welfare, reduce stress and anxiety, and support the behaviour plan, as part of a comprehensive plan (assessment, environment, routine, foster, enrichment, training, monitoring). ASV
ASV: risk assessment of aggression Shelters MUST respond promptly to behaviour posing a significant safety risk and must have protocols to identify and manage animals at high risk of harm. Dogs surrendered with credible reports of severe aggression toward people or other dogs should be euthanised as quickly as legally possible, since there is no benefit to holding them. Euthanasia is the appropriate outcome for animals at high risk of serious harm to people, and legal consultation can help when writing these protocols. ASV
Exam trap: single-incident euthanasia and thin slicing A euthanasia decision should rest on a single aggression incident only if it is truly egregious (a severe attack on a person or dog); otherwise confirm the behaviour safely in other contexts. Experts can decide accurately from a few observations (thin slicing), but non-experts who thin-slice are prone to error. Fast-track dogs are those with a good surrender report and high sociability to people and dogs. your notes

Most common shelter behaviours

  • Anxiety or fear about unfamiliar people, handling, or other dogs, often shown as avoidance including aggression. ASV
  • Frustration about other dogs, withdrawal of attention or play, or limited activity and stimulation. ASV
  • Anxiety or fear about resources (toys, food) or about separation from people. ASV

Assessment timing

Rehoming, adoption criteria, and follow-up

  • Provide resources and a safe, gradual, controlled introduction to children and resident pets, and communicate any behaviour concerns to reduce returns. your notes
  • Basic adoption criteria: the animal must be desexed; no adoption to anyone with a known history of animal or child abuse, anyone suspected of being intoxicated at application, or for use as a food source. your notes
  • Guiding placement values: the match suits the animal and family, appropriate veterinary care, social and companionship needs met, a liveable environment, and the pet respected and valued. your notes
  • Follow-up: more than one behaviour problem was reported by about 51% of adopters at 1 week and 1 month; house training was the top dog issue and chewing or scratching the top cat issue. Conversation-based adoptions and good interpretation reduce mismatches. your notes

Bite history: documentation, placement, and legal considerations notesASV

Dunbar Bite Scale

LevelDescriptionShelter implication
1Snap or air bite — no skin contactCommunication signal; evaluate context. Low placement risk for otherwise stable dog.
2Teeth contact skin but no puncture — may leave redness or bruisingEvaluate triggers and recovery speed. Most dogs are adoptable with appropriate household guidance.
31–4 punctures from a single bite, none deeper than half the canine lengthSignificant; requires bite history detail, trigger mapping, and placement into household without young children unless circumstances are clear.
41–4 punctures, at least one deeper than half canine length, or wounds from head shakingHigh risk; requires intensive evaluation. Adoption criteria are narrow; placement in homes with children or other animals is rarely appropriate.
5Multiple-bite attack with Level 4 wounds OR multiple attacks in a single incidentASV position: a credible surrender history of multiple Level 4–5 bites is grounds for serious euthanasia consideration.
6Fatal attack on a humanEuthanasia.

Documentation requirements

  • Record: date of incident, identity and age of victim (adult, child, infant), severity (Dunbar level), location on victim's body, circumstances (feeding, sleeping, handling, trigger), whether warning signals were present, and what happened immediately before and after.
  • Source and credibility of the report: owner surrender statement, animal control report, veterinary record, or witness account. Document the source clearly — information weight depends on it.
  • US shelter context: most states impose a legal duty to disclose a known bite history to adopters. Failure to disclose known Level 3+ history can create shelter liability. Document that disclosure was made and signed off by the adopter.

Placement criteria by level

  • Level 1–2: placement suitable with standard counselling and household guidance; no automatic exclusion of children if context is benign.
  • Level 3: no placement with infants or toddlers; household must have no accidental trigger scenarios; signed informed consent required.
  • Level 4: narrow placement criteria; experienced owner only; no young children; no homes with less secure physical management of the dog.
  • Level 5: ASV: a credible report of multiple Level 4–5 bites makes the animal a serious public safety risk; euthanasia is a supported outcome and may be the only defensible decision.
Exam trap · credibility of surrender bite reportsA surrender form stating a Level 4 bite is not automatically discounted because the owner "might be exaggerating." The ASV position is that a credible surrender report of a severe bite carries significant weight and must be documented and acted upon — not buried. Conversely, a vague rumour or unattributed claim without corroboration is insufficient grounds for euthanasia on its own. Evaluate specificity: date, victim, circumstances, source.

Welfare and Ethical Decision Making

Welfare is multidimensional and no single measure is a gold standard, so shelters combine input-based and animal-based measures within a recognised framework, monitor over time, and use structured ethical reasoning to make and defend outcome decisions.

Three viewpoints of welfare

FrameworkComponents
Five FreedomsFrom thirst and hunger; from distress and fear; from injury and pain; from physical discomfort; to express normal behaviour
Welfare Quality (4 principles)Good feeding, good housing, good health, appropriate behaviour
Five DomainsNutrition, environment, health, behaviour, mental state

std ref Input-based measures (sound, air quality, temperature, humidity, housing, resources, management) sit alongside animal-based measures. Qualitative Behaviour Assessment (QBA) rates the expressivity of the whole animal as it engages with its environment and shows high inter-rater agreement.

Cortisol and monitoring caveats

Exam trap: cortisol and behaviour as welfare markers Plasma cortisol is highest in the first 3 days; urinary cortisol peaks around day 17; dogs with prior kenneling experience have LOWER urinary cortisol than naive dogs. A dog's behaviour cannot be used as a sole predictor of stress, since behavioural stress is expressed many ways and correlates poorly with plasma cortisol. Some dogs performing stereotypies had better welfare than those that did not, and stereotypy can persist as a behavioural scar. your notes

Daily rounds

Daily rounds are a systematic monitoring process and one of the most effective tools for protecting welfare, helping create urgency, move animals through the system, reduce LOS, and prevent crowding. your notes

  • Team includes shelter operations, medical, and behaviour perspectives, led by a population manager, with protected time. your notes
  • Conduct rounds before cleaning and feeding to catch inappetent animals; for group housing, sometimes during feeding to confirm access. your notes
  • Six questions: Who are you? How are you? Are you where you should be? What can we provide today? Where are you going? Are you meeting the Five Freedoms or domains? your notes

Ethical decision making and welfare threats

  • Respond to welfare threats by addressing fundamental causes (reduce stressors, change the environment) rather than ad-hoc treatment of signs; pharmacology should not replace environmental change. your notes
  • Behavioural restriction is a powerful psychological stressor; increasing an animal's ability to predict and control its environment protects wellbeing. your notes
  • A structured ethical approach makes decisions inclusive, transparent, and evidence-based, and easier to justify to those with opposing views. your notes
  • Moral stress, the stress of being unable to do what one believes is right, can drive medical error, compassion fatigue, and burnout; organisational structures help more than individual resilience techniques alone. your notes

Advancing canine welfare assessment in kennels: evaluating the validity of Field Instantaneous Dog Observation (FIDO) scoring in commercial breeding kennels
Mugenda, Appl Anim Behav Sci · Appl Anim Behav Sci · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106770

Abstract. Accurate assessment of social fear in dogs housed in commercial breeding kennels is necessary to inform their behavioral management, including interventions needed to support their welfare. The Field Instantaneous Dog Observation (FIDO) tool, which was developed to quickly identify areas of welfare concern for dogs in commercial kennels, includes a behavioral measure of social fear that has been examined for reliability, but its validity has yet to be established. We therefore investigated: 1) if dogs' responses to stranger approach changed when they were locked into the inside portions of their pens (to avoid loss of data) versus when free access to the outdoor portions of their pens allowed them to avoid such interactions, and 2) if the dogs' responses to a stranger's approach in their home pens were associated with their behavioral responses to an unfamiliar person in an outdoor arena. Eighty pair-housed dogs from four USDA-licensed commercial breeding kennels were tested. Using the FIDO scoring, the dogs' responses were categorized as red, indicating a fearful response to approach, green, indicating an affiliative or neutral response, and yellow, indicating an ambivalent response. Percentage of agreement and weighted kappa statistics were calculated for the FIDO scores of the dogs when locked indoors or when allowed free outdoor access. Behavioral measures from the arena test, such as latencies to approach the stranger and proximity to the stranger were captured from video, and variables were reduced by applying a principal component (PC) analysis. Multivariate multiple regression analysis was applied using the PCs as outcome variables, and FIDO scores as predictors. Moderate to substantial agreement was found for dogs' red and green FIDO scores between locked in and free access scoring conditions (all ps <= .0023, 95 % CI). Dogs scored as red in their pens using FIDO showed more stranger avoidance in the arena test than dogs scored as green (p = 0.039), indicating the validity of the tool's assessment of fear in response to approach by an unfamiliar person. This finding suggests that the FIDO tool may be useful in helping to identify dogs whose responses to unfamiliar people suggest a need for intervention to reduce fear of them, a crucial element in supporting their welfare, especially for those intended to be rehomed at the end of their breeding careers.

Beyond Cortisol! Physiological Indicators of Welfare for Dogs: Deficits, Misunderstandings and Opportunities
Cobb, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · 2025 · 10.1080/10888705.2025.2572616

Abstract. This paper initiates new conversations about using physiological indicators to assess dog welfare. There are significant concerns about construct validity - whether measures used accurately reflect welfare's complexity. We propose evidence-based frameworks to advance canine welfare assessment through improved physiological measurement protocols, challenge over reliance on isolated cortisol measurements, and present actionable recommendations for research methodologies that better consider individual variation. Current evidence demonstrates that animal welfare assessment requires multiple physiological indicators reflecting distinct biological systems. In practice, indicators are frequently used in isolation with limited salient dog descriptors, failing to reflect the canine experience adequately. Using a systems approach, we explore various physiological systems and alternative indicators It is essential to consider factors like age, body weight, and sex when interpreting biomarkers, and researchers should report these. This discussion identifies indicators for both positive and negative experiences. We advocate for a practical, evidence-based approach to assessing indicators of canine welfare, including noninvasive collection methods. We acknowledge the complexity of evaluating experiential responses in dogs and the need for continued work to improve practices, ultimately advancing welfare assessment and improving dogs' lives.

Dog Guardian Interpretation of Familiar Dog Aggression Questions in the C-BARQ: Do We Need to Redefine “Familiar”?
Pellowe S, Walsh C · Animals · 2025 · 10.3390/ani15192876

Abstract. The C-BARQ familiar dog aggression (FDA) subscale contains four items relating to threatening responses towards familiar dogs in the same household (i.e., dog rivalry). In a recent study, we noticed that 92 of 157 guardians who owned only one dog completed the FDA items, generating an unexpected score. We followed up with participants to explore whether lifestyle factors influenced their completion of the FDA items. Singleton dogs with FDA scores were more likely to regularly participate in social activities with other dogs, with many scores based on such interactions with non-household dogs. The singleton dogs with FDA scores also had marginally lower fear-related C-BARQ scores compared to singletons with no FDA score and dogs living in multi-dog households. We then conducted a scoping review of articles using English versions of the C-BARQ and found wide variation in whether or not FDA scores were reported. Studies that reported significant FDA findings often did not indicate the proportion of scores in their data that came from singleton dogs, raising issues of accuracy and interpretation of the subscale. We discuss ways to clarify the interpretation of the FDA questions by dog guardians and hope to promote further consideration of practices to improve replicability across studies.

Effects of maternal care on detection dog behavioral outcomes
Montgomery LI, Krichbaum S, Smith JG, Haney P, Lazarowski L · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106818

Abstract. This study characterised maternal behaviour over the first three weeks post-partum in 27 detection-dog litters, identified factors shaping maternal care, and related it to offspring outcomes. Maternal behaviours declined across the three weeks, though individual differences among dams were stable. Counterintuitively, less maternal care predicted greater offspring success in the detection-dog program - consistent with other working-dog research showing that more maternal care can be associated with lower selection. Maternal care was shaped by an interaction of delivery type and parity, and by dam temperament (assessed with the C-BARQ). The findings highlight the early maternal environment as an influence on working-dog development, with effects that vary across populations.

Evaluation of the factorial structures of the canine behavioral assessment and research questionnaire (C-BARQ) in France
Besegher A, Rebout N, Bovet D, Jeannin S, Bedossa T, Serpell JA, Hoummady S · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106816

Abstract. This study aimed to evaluate the factorial structure of the French version of the C-BARQ by analyzing the behaviour of 246 dogs, based on responses from 248 participants, in order to confirm its potential applicability in France. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identified 13 primary factors comprising 63 items, explaining 54.1 % of the total variance and a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) revealed a good fit with the original US model. The French version of the C-BARQ mirrors the core structure of other validated versions, confirming its cross-cultural robustness, while also highlighting certain specificities. The emergence of the combined factor "Stranger-directed aggression/fear" suggests that non-expert respondents may have difficulty distinguishing between these two dimensions. Two new factors—"Passerby-directed aggression " and "Compulsive-like behaviour"—emerged, likely reflecting the characteristics of the study sample and the inclusion of the full set of items. Other notable differences include the clustering of certain attachment-related items with those related to energy, forming a new factor labeled "Social excitability/energy," as well as the absence of the "Dog rivalry" factor due to insufficient responses. Overall, the results indicate that the French version of the C-BARQ is a valid tool for assessing canine behaviour. However, cultural, contextual, and demographic differences should be considered when interpreting the findings. This study paves the way for future research on the factors influencing perceptions of canine behaviour and on the adaptation of psychometric instruments across cultural contexts.

Initial psychometric evaluation of the European Portuguese version of the Lincoln Canine Adaptability Resilience Scale
Alves, PLoS One · PLoS One · 2025 · 10.1007/s11165-016-9602-2

Abstract. OBJECTIVE: Dogs face a variety of stressors in their lives, either as companion or as working animals, from novel situations and challenges, to visits to the vet, kenneling, or transportation. Some individuals have personal characteristics that allow them to cope with adversity and develop positive adaptations. The Lincoln Canine Adaptability Resilience Scale (L-CARS) was developed to assess this ability. This study aimed to validate a European Portuguese version of the Lincoln Canine Adaptability Resilience Scale. PATIENTS AND METHODS: Information from 182 dogs was collected. The English version of the L-CARS was translated into Portuguese, and this version was back-translated until a unified version was obtained. Canine handlers, native Portuguese speakers, completed a copy of the translated Portuguese version of the L-CARS. Validity testing was performed with the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy. Extrated values were assessed based on Eigenvalue and scree-plot analysis. Internal consistency was tested with Cronbach's α. The correlation between items was determined using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. RESULTS: Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin factor analysis was 0.83. The factor analysis model identified five factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1, accounting for 67.56% of the variance. Commonalities ranged between 0.86 and 0.29. Cronbach's α was 0.81. All components showed positive correlation with the overall score (p < 0.01 for both). CONCLUSIONS: The study presented criterion and construct validity of the European Portuguese version of the L-CARS. It is a step in providing a broader Portuguese-speaking population and clinicians with a validated and accessible tool to evaluate patients. Further studies are required.

Investigating the ability of standard tests to detect improvements in behavior after a 9-week training program in shelter dogs – A pilot study
de Castro, J Vet Behav · J Vet Behav · 2025 · 10.1016/j.jveb.2024.11.002

Abstract. Annually, millions of dogs enter shelters worldwide. As such, it is vital to develop effective methods for assessing adoptability and promoting successful rehoming that can be easily implemented by organizations with minimal resources. Many shelters use temperament testing and training programs to facilitate these goals, but there is limited research on the effects of training programs on shelter dog behavior, and no standardized tests for assessing the skills imparted by those programs. Therefore, the primary goal of this study was to evaluate the sensitivity of temperament and basic education tests to detect changes in dog behavior after training in a shelter context. For dogs, Vas and colleagues developed a 13-item questionnaire to measure inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity (Dog ARS; 2007). We re-assessed, in a large sample of dogs (N = 319), psychometric properties of the Dog ARS, to identify possible limitations as a basis for further development. We examined the cross-study stability of factor structure and 40-day temporal stability of item and subscale scores and compared owner-report with expert (dog trainer)-report (n = 86), paralleling human parent/teacher assessments. To identify ambiguous items, we administered a modified version (including “I don’t know” options, N = 520) to a different sample. We could replicate the factor structure with evidence of good internal consistency and test–retest reliability of both subscales. Agreement between owner and trainer ratings was fair (inattention) and moderate (hyperactivity/impulsivity). Three ambiguous items were identified. Overall, we claim that the Dog ARS is a reliable tool to assess ADHD-like behaviour in dogs, but in its current form, it is not suitable to detect diagnosable individuals, as it does not comprise items assessing functional impairment, and also, the inclusion of owner-expert ratings in the evaluation process would be necessary.

Temperament Assessment Algorithm in Dogs
Karpinski, M · Animals · 2022

Abstract. The aim of this study was to evaluate the temperament of dogs on the basis of behavioral observations, with emphasis on 24 selected traits and behaviors. From the observations, the temperament of the dogs was determined and the animals were assigned to one of two personality groups: introvert or extrovert. The study involved 46 dogs. The agglomeration method, Pearson’s 1-r distance, and Ward’s binding method were used. As shown by the statistical analysis, 18 dogs (39%) were assessed as introverts and 28 dogs (61%) exhibited extrovert traits. To construct a model for the assessment of canine temperament using the identified traits, logistic regression was performed with the independent variables, number of extrovert traits (ETs) and introvert traits (ITs), and a dichotomous dependent variable (1 = extrovert, 0 = introvert), reflecting the assessment of the temperament of the dog based on the observations and results of the original questionnaire.

The effects of owner and domestic cat (Felis catus) demographics on cat personality traits
Leech, L · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2022

Abstract. Personality has been researched in many companion animals and is described as differences in behavioural traits of individuals that often remain consistent over time. In domestic cats many factors have been discovered to influence personality, including breed, coat colour, gender, rearing experience, number of cats within a household, owner age, owner gender and owner personality. However, research is limited for certain factors, including owner demographics, so the aim was to demonstrate that a simple survey could be used to infer personality traits and identify domestic cat and owner demographical factors that influence certain traits. An online personality survey with 34 traits was sent out to cat owners in the UK, Europe and North America, containing cat and owner demographical questions. Housing type, total number of cats in household and owner animal preference all had significant effects on many of the personality trait scores. Unexpectedly, cat breed, owner age, neutering status and country of residence showed distinct clusters in the multifactor analysis individual model but did not have any significant effects on any of the personality traits, along with coat colour, owner gender and cat gender which were initially considered of importance to the study, contradicting some of the previous research. This study highlights the importance of considering demographical factors that influence personality traits, to predict cat personality based on these factors to cater for specific husbandry practices and to improve the chances of successful adoption for those within shelters.

What Will Happen to This Dog? A Qualitative Analysis of Rehoming Organisations' Pre-adoption Dog Behaviour Screening Policies and Procedures
Griffin, K. · Frontiers in Veterinary Science · 2022

Abstract. Rehoming organisations often undertake some type of behaviour evaluation to determine dogs' suitability for rehoming and/or the type of suitable home. Assessments can carry considerable weight in determining dogs' fates. Although evaluation of the validity and reliability of any test is important, a more fundamental consideration is if the nature of the information sought and the weight given to this in organisations' decision making is of more than anecdotal value. Therefore, this study's aim was to conduct a qualitative analysis of organisations' pre-adoption dog behaviour screenings and potential justifications, comparing this with the available scientific evidence. A written enquiry was sent electronically to rehoming organisations in the UK and US from February 2016-July 2017. Of 73 respondents, the majority conducted assessments for all dogs. Using a thematic analysis, nine themes and 71 sub-themes emerged concerning the types of information respondents aim to gather from assessments. The majority of respondents used, at least partially, pass/fail scoring, i.e., certain outcomes would lead to dogs being deemed unadoptable. Forty-one sub-themes and one theme were identified as potentially leading to a dog being deemed unadoptable. The evidence base for these factors was identified from the scientific literature relating to: increased risk for relinquishment, impact on a dog's quality of life, and human safety risk. Evidence supported 10 factors: “aggression towards people”, “aggression towards cats or other animals”, “aggression towards dogs”, “biting or snapping”, “resource guarding”, “activity level or exercise needs”, “destructiveness”, “housetrained”, “fearfulness”, and “knowledge of basic commands and/or general training”. Of those, seven were associated only with relinquishment risk, two (“resource guarding”, “knowledge of basic commands”) with human safety risk, and one (“fearfulness”) with both. Thus, for >85% of characteristics organisations deemed important for dogs' adoptability, scientific evidence to support this is lacking. More research is needed to investigate the value of behaviour assessments, especially concerning the assessment of factors that could pose a public safety risk. However, given the current lack of scientific support for many decisions regarding dogs' rehoming suitability and recognised pressure on resources, it is suggested that organisations should focus on pre-adoption adopter education and post-adoption support.

Do you see the same cat that I see? Relationships between Qualitative Behaviour Assessment and indicators traditionally used to assess temperament in domestic cats
Travnik I · Animal Welfare · 2021

Abstract. This study into the behaviour of domestic cats (Felis catus) aimed to assess the relationship between behavioural expressions obtained via the Qualitative Behaviour Assessment (QBA) and quantitative outcomes of temperament tests. Four behavioural tests were used: Unfamiliar person (UP); Novel object (NO); Conspecific reaction (CR); and Food offering (FO) tests. Tests were filmed and assessed using an ethogram that included 25 discrete behavioural categories, generating quantitative information (coding method) on the existing temperament dimensions. Videos were also assessed by another observer using the QBA method, based on a list of 20 adjectives rated in visual analogue scales (rating method). Data were analysed using Principal Component Analysis. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were used to relate the principal components (PC) of QBA to the temperament dimensions obtained with the coding method. The QBA allowed us to identify three PC, explaining 76.63% of the total variance. PC1 ranged from ‘calm/relaxed/friendly’ to ‘tense/fearful/alert’, reflecting the valence of cats’ behavioural and emotional expressions towards the stimuli tested. PC2 ranged from ‘indifferent’ to ‘agitated/active’, indicating the level of emotional arousal, and PC3 ranged from ‘aggressive’ to ‘suspicious’ and could be interpreted as an axis of ‘aggressiveness — caution’ in response to the stimuli. The first PC obtained for each test by using the coding method was significantly correlated with the PC1 of QBA, suggesting that the variations in cats’ behavioural and emotional expressions identified by QBA were correlated with the main quantitative outcomes of temperament tests traditionally applied for domestic cats. QBA could be a promising tool for identifying and differentiating cat temperament profiles. Further research is required to assess the potential use of QBA as a feasible and practical method for use in shelters.

Reliability and Validity of Seven Feline Behavior and Personality Traits
Mikkola S · Animals · 2021

Abstract. Domestic cats are popular pets, and they have personalities, with stable behavior differences between individuals. Lately, feline behavior and personality have been studied with different approaches, for example, with owner-completed questionnaires. The majority of these studies, however, lack a sufficient validation and reliability assessment of the questionnaires used. We designed an online feline behavior and personality questionnaire to collect cat behavior data from their owners. Then, we ran a factor analysis to study the structure of personality and behavior in a dataset of over 4300 cats. For validation, we studied the internal consistency, test–retest reliability, inter-rater reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity of this questionnaire and extracted factors. In addition, we briefly examined breed differences in the seven discovered factors: fearfulness, activity/playfulness, aggression toward humans, sociability toward humans, sociability toward cats, excessive grooming and litterbox issues. Most of the rank ordering of breeds within each trait paralleled what has been found in previous studies. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire and factors were good, strengthening owner-completed questionnaires as a method to collect behavioral data from pet animals. Breed differences suggest a genetic background for personality. However, these differences should be studied further with multidimensional models, including environmental and biological variables.

Unanswered Questions and Hypotheses about Domestic Cat Behavior, Ecology, and the Cat–Human Relationship
Turner D · Animals · 2021

Abstract. After recent publication of several reviews covering research results from the last 35 years of domestic cat studies, a number of important unanswered questions and hypotheses have arisen that could interest active researchers, especially those beginning their academic careers. Some sections of this paper concern methodologies that have yielded new insights and could provide more in the future; other sections concern findings and interpretations of those that need further testing. First, hypotheses arise from combining subjective (or psychological) assessments of cat and human personality traits and observational (ethological) studies of cat–human interactions: e.g., do owners with high attachment to their cats interact differently with them than owners with low attachment levels? New analytical methods of dyadic interaction observations open the door for testing further hypotheses. In particular, the Theme® (Noldus bv, NL) program could be used to determine if there are differences between cat breeds in interaction patterns with people, which is not only of interest to owners but also therapists employing cats in their practices. Cat breed differences have been found using subjective ratings, but these need to be corroborated by direct observational data from the home setting and/or non-invasive colony observations, since ratings based on anthropomorphic projections might not be reliable. This should be done before searching for the genetic basis of such differences. Reliable information on breed differences is also needed before prescribing certain breeds for animal- assisted interventions. A model has predicted that the degree of socialization as a kitten affects cats’ responses to positive and negative experiences with unfamiliar humans and their formation of feline–human relationships later on. This needs to be tested in an ethically approved manner on cats of known socialization status and has enormous consequences for cat adoptions from animal shelters. Observations of human–cat interactions have yielded many correlations, which can be tested by non-invasive manipulations of human behavior in the home setting. Examples of these will be given and are of general interest to the cat-owning public. A review of first findings on social cognition in cats has resulted in further unanswered questions and hypotheses. Finally, two aspects of domestic cat ecology will be considered (effects on wildlife and space utilization), which are of great interest to the public and conservationists alike.
