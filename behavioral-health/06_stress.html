Stress Physiology

Stress triggers two neuroendocrine cascades: the fast SAM system for immediate fight-or-flight and the slower HPA axis for sustained cortisol release. The exam tests the timeline, the hormones, and the chronic-dysfunction paradox.

Stress

Speed

Cortisol kinetics

  • Cortisol takes 20 to 30 minutes to peak following a stressor and remains elevated for 90 minutes or more. your notes
  • Low to moderate acute stress is considered beneficial (helps the animal cope). Extreme acute stress or chronic stress has detrimental effects on physical and emotional health. your notes

Chronic HPA dysregulation

Exam trap: low cortisol does not equal low stress Chronic stress causes adrenal hypertrophy and increased glucocorticoids, but ACTH remains unchanged. The HPA axis becomes dysregulated and stops responding appropriately, so a normal or low resting cortisol in a long-term resident may indicate a malfunctioning stress-response system, not a relaxed animal. Kennelled dogs in this state still remain responsive to acute stressors such as restraint and venipuncture. Cortisol also depends on when it was collected, neuter status, sampling method, and only indicates activation of stress, not the type (good, tolerable, or toxic). your notes

Systemic effects of stress

Stress increases maladaptive behaviour and displays of aggression, impairs social behaviour, memory, and cognition, increases disease susceptibility, slows healing, and shortens lifespan. The HPA effects include increased energy availability, increased oxygen intake, decreased blood flow to non-critical areas, and inhibition of digestion, growth, immune function, reproduction, and pain perception (memory and sensory functions are enhanced). Acute stress can enhance memory for threatening events. Chronic stress can lead to structural and functional brain changes. your notes

Stress Taxonomy and Dynamics

Three categories of stress, two types of stressor, and the shelter-specific concepts (trigger stacking, context learning, the first-week rule) that the exam tests.

Categories of stress

CategoryDefinition
Good stressThe animal faces a challenge and copes successfully with no long-term harm
Tolerable stressThe challenge is distressing at the time, but the animal can cope and does not suffer long term
Toxic stressDistress exceeds the animal's ability to cope, causing long-term consequences to emotional and physical health

Types of stressor

  • Physical: going for a run, hit by a car, catching a virus, hunger, thirst, pain, extreme temperatures, disease, sleep deprivation. your notes
  • Psychological: social or environmental factors with no direct physical perturbation (meeting a new person or dog, being attacked without injury, social isolation, exposure to novelty, unpredictable environments, chronic frustration). your notes
Exam trap: most potent psychological stressors Novelty, the withholding of an expected reward, and the anticipation of punishment are the most potent psychological stressors. your notes

Trigger stacking and context learning

  • Context learning: an aversive experience in a specific environment shapes the animal's future behaviour in that same context. your notes
  • Trigger stacking: multiple stressors in rapid succession accumulate with no window to return to baseline, producing an amplified response or a behavioural explosion. your notes
  • A brief positive pre-session experience (walk, play, human interaction) did not improve dogs' subsequent learning performance and, unexpectedly, was followed by higher stress signs and more owner-proximity-seeking during a disruption task — a caution against assuming a good experience buffers the next stressor (Miller et al., PLoS One, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

The first-week rule

  • Stress is highest during the first week. Owner-relinquished dogs show intense psychological stress without adaptation, whereas strays and return dogs show a lower baseline. your notes
  • Defer non-essential stressful activities during the first week to allow adaptation and relationship building. your notes
  • Prior kennelling experience reduces the activated stress response when the dog enters a new kennel. your notes
  • Petting interaction over 15 minutes per day does not change stress levels in owner-relinquished dogs, but it does reduce stress in strays. your notes
  • Research on wild animals shows translocation stress via four routes: capture, captivity, transport, and release. your notes
  • Sensor-based nocturnal activity monitoring found no distinct "first-night effect" (activity did not differ between night 1 and night 2), but shelter dogs were more active at night and had higher urinary cortisol:creatinine ratios than pet dogs through at least the first 12 days, with dogs losing about 5% body weight over that habituation period (van der Laan et al., Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Maternal and early-life stress

Experiences during the first weeks of life profoundly affect the animal's ability to cope. Maternal stress during gestation can alter pup behaviour, and offspring often show learning impairment and increased susceptibility to conditioning of fearful responses, especially to auditory stimuli. your notes Dogs from known adverse early-life backgrounds (puppy mills, unlicensed commercial breeders, hoarding situations) show significantly higher salivary cortisol and more fear behaviour with an unfamiliar human even after removal to a shelter, regardless of how long they spent in either environment (Buttner, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Measuring and Identifying Stress

Cortisol is the most-tested stress marker but it comes with caveats. The exam also tests the stress leukogram and the sampling-matrix decision.

Exam trap: sampling decision To evaluate a shelter's housing over months, faecal or hair cortisol metabolites are correct; blood or saliva cortisol would be a diagnostic error due to the acute stress of collection. your notes

Other endocrine and haematological markers

  • ACTH and luteinising-hormone-releasing-hormone stimulation tests measure adrenal and pituitary sensitivities. Increased HPA responsiveness with reduced pituitary sensitivity suggests HPA dysfunction from chronic stress. your notes
  • Stress leukogram: decreased peripheral lymphocyte numbers and increased neutrophil counts. In a long-term shelter animal with a normal cortisol, a persistent lymphopenia and neutrophilia on CBC provides evidence of chronic stress and HPA dysregulation. your notes
  • Cardiovascular parameters (heart rate, systolic blood pressure) are also used as stress indicators, though confounded by many factors; in hospitalised dogs, dog-appeasing pheromone delivered by diffuser produced no significant change in these cardiovascular stress parameters over 24 hours (Minuzzo, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). Heart-rate variability is another candidate marker: in pet dogs, the specific dimension of the owner relationship (emotional closeness, shared activities, perceived costs) differentially predicted heart-rate variability and stress-related behaviour (Somppa, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • The buccal micronucleus assay, a measure of genomic damage, found significantly higher micronuclei frequency in shelter-housed dogs and cats than in home-housed dogs and cats, suggesting chronic shelter stress may increase genomic damage (Santovito, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Cortisol elevation is not stress-specific: a review of measurement methods and clinical correlates notes that abnormal cortisol also occurs with endocrine disease such as Cushing's, so a raised result in a shelter animal should prompt consideration of underlying pathology, not just psychological stress (Marza, Animals, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). Despite 128 reviewed studies, no universal quantitative or qualitative stress indicator or standardised assessment protocol exists for veterinary patients, reinforcing that an individualised approach combining behavioural observation, history, and multiple markers is the most reliable path to a diagnosis (Kartashova, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • A scoping review of 27 studies on canine-assisted interventions found consistently positive changes in human stress biomarkers with no negative impact on the studied dog stress biomarkers, though results were often inconsistent within a study (e.g., improved cortisol without a matching change in self-reported stress) (Gandenberger, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Serum serotonin and tryptophan did not differ across behavioural stress-response categories in shelter dogs undergoing a stressful procedure, and the two did not correlate with each other, arguing against their use as a stand-alone stress marker (Riggio, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Across 224 shelter dogs, in-kennel behavioural observations (e.g., time spent standing with paws on the cage, tail wagging, panting) were more predictive of adoption, return-to-owner, transfer, and euthanasia outcomes than cortisol or immunoglobulin A (Powell et al., Physiology & Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). In shelter dogs undergoing veterinary health checks, urinary oxytocin correlated negatively with cortisol and body temperature, particularly in relaxed dogs, suggesting oxytocin may buffer HPA-axis activation (Gazzano et al., Veterinary Sciences, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Fecal cortisol metabolites are higher in recently sheltered free-roaming dogs than in pet dogs and can keep rising rather than settling, and counter-intuitively, dogs later given a good rehoming prognosis had higher fecal cortisol than those with a poor prognosis (moderated by sex) — a reminder that a higher level does not automatically mean a worse outcome (Chen, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • The gut–brain axis is an emerging non-endocrine biomarker source: in a Beagle population, fecal short-chain fatty acids and specific bacterial genera tracked with age, alongside behavioural indicators (activity, exploring, play) that were higher in younger dogs, though causal pathways linking gut health and behaviour remain unclear (Fernández-Pinteño et al., Animals, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Feline Stress

Cats mask stress with feigned sleep and defensive displays. The five pillars, the FURI link, and the trapped-tame-versus-feral distinction are all high-yield.

Signs of stress in cats

  • Hypervigilance, feigned sleep, constant hiding, activity depression, and loss of appetite. your notes
  • Defensive behaviours: front-of-pen aggression, arched back, ears back, dilated pupils, vocalising. your notes
Exam trap: feigned sleep Feigned sleep is a coping mechanism in highly stressed cats, not evidence of relaxation. Recording "the cat is sleeping and relaxed" is an anthropomorphic error. your notes
Exam trap: trapped tame versus feral Even the tamest house cat, when trapped and stressed, can exhibit defensive behaviours indistinguishable from truly feral cats. Allow frightened cats to calm before assessing personality. Some cats may never adjust to the shelter. your notes

The five pillars of feline environmental welfare

  • 1. A safe place. 2. Multiple and separated key environmental resources. 3. Opportunities for play and predatory behaviour. 4. Positive, consistent, and predictable human-cat interaction. 5. An environment that respects the cat's sense of smell. std ref

Stress, disease, and adaptation

  • High stress score is linked to high rates of FURI. your notes Fear of unfamiliar people specifically is associated with feline idiopathic cystitis recurrence: 94% of cats with a recurrence were reported fearful of strangers versus 59% of cats with only a single episode (Caudron et al., Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Stress levels generally reduce within the first two weeks, but about 4% of the feline population maintain a high stress level and may not be suited to shelter housing. your notes
  • Feral cats require the same minimum floor space as friendly cats (11 sq ft (1 sq m), per ASV) in short-term housing, though they may use hiding spaces only at night. ASV
  • Species-relevant auditory stimuli (recordings of cat purring and cat eating sounds) reduced visual scanning behaviour in cats exposed to a novel environment, but did not produce broader stress relief across other measured behaviours (Bian et al., Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Nail cortisol concentration is significantly higher in cats housed in unfavourable shelter conditions than in favourable shelter or home conditions, and correlates positively with dog exposure and negatively with space allowance, litterbox number, free-roaming time, and enrichment variety (Pham Quoc Anh Minh et al., Applied Animal Behavior Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Hair cortisol is significantly higher in cats whose owners report house soiling (eliminating outside the litter box) or aggression toward household members, linking a common non-invasive marker to specific behaviour problems (Wojtas et al., Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Urinary output is significantly suppressed on the first day after a stressful in-shelter move (e.g., to the adoption floor), measurable non-invasively by weighing litter clumps as a proxy for urine output (Andrukonis, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Owner tactile and vocal interaction frequency correlates positively with urinary oxytocin in domestic cats (Nagasawa, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below), and both urinary cortisol and oxytocin are significantly higher when cats have no social contact with a person than when they do, with the two hormones correlating under the no-contact condition (Nagasawa, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Vocalisation during a brief standardised stressor (confinement in a carrier) is a stable, repeatable individual difference from before weaning through adulthood and may be a useful indicator of individual stress reactivity; facial thermography was not a reliable indicator in the same test (Urrutia, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Cats show a Secure Base Effect: stress-related behaviours are reduced when the owner is physically present in an unfamiliar environment, but an owner-scented object alone was not sufficient to produce the same calming effect (Behnke, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Hair and nail cortisol concentrations correlate with each other; cats with litterbox issues have significantly higher hair and nail cortisol, and cats with dander or matted coats have higher nail cortisol, linking coat condition to chronic stress (Contreras, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • In pet cats, Cat Stress Score was significantly higher in single-cat households than multi-cat households regardless of reported agonistic interactions between housemates — though the authors caution this could partly reflect inactivity from a negative affective state rather than true relaxation (Ozgunay, Animals, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
Overlaps: feline housing as stress mitigation The detailed feline micro and macro environment specifications (8 to 11 sq ft (0.7 to 1 sq m) individual floor space, 18 sq ft (1.7 sq m) per cat group minimum, perch 12 inches (30.5 cm) wide and raised 13 to 14 inches (33 to 35.6 cm), cages at least 18 inches (45.7 cm) off the floor, fill top cages first, temperature per ASV 18 to 26.6 C (64.4 to 79.9°F), quiet latches, horizontal bars, colour palette favouring blues, greens, and violets over fluorescing whites, hiding boxes for all cats at entry, group-housing selection and removal criteria) are built in full in the Facility and Environment hub. The key principle here is that each specification is a stress-mitigation tool: hiding lowers Cat Stress Score without increasing length of stay or reducing adoption rates; elevated cages reduce stress and improve monitoring; less cage space directly increases FURI risk and behavioural concerns; and small groups are preferred over warehouse-style rooms for welfare, stress, and disease reasons. your notes
UV vision clarification The notes state cats see in the UVB spectrum. Cats do transmit some short-wavelength and ultraviolet light through their lenses and are dichromatic (sensitive to blue-violet and green-yellow; reds and oranges appear greyish), which is why the practical advice (avoid bright white fluorescing plastics, choose matte blues, greens, and violets) is sound even though the "UVB" label is a simplification. your notes

Adaptation, Deterioration and Management

The first three days see cortisol rebalance, but prolonged confinement drives behavioural deterioration. Tracking whether the animal is sensitising, habituating, or developing a depression-like state is the clinical lever.

Behavioural adaptation

  • Cortisol decreases during the first three days, likely due to the animal regaining homeostatic balance after transport and introduction to a novel environment. your notes
  • By day six, dogs begin spending more time at the back of the kennel and less time active, which may indicate growing comfort. your notes
  • Dogs transferred to short-term foster rested significantly more than in the shelter, likely an artifact of a less stimulating environment rather than sleep disruption per se. However, when humans were absent, shelter dogs were more active than pet dogs during the latter's least active period. your notes A weeklong foster stay was also confirmed to significantly lower urinary cortisol and increase resting time at two shelters, and co-housing with a familiar dog after returning to the shelter was linked to more rest and less high-intensity activity than returning alone (Gunter et al., PeerJ, 2026 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Accelerometer-measured nocturnal activity and urinary cortisol:creatinine ratio are highest immediately after shelter intake and decline over a 12-day habituation period; smaller dogs and dogs with no prior kennelling experience show higher nocturnal activity and cortisol:creatinine ratios, and dogs lose weight over this period (van der Laan, PLOS ONE, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Behavioural deterioration

  • Barrier aggression, high arousal, worsening reactivity, increasing fear, and onset of abnormal behaviour are commonly cited examples. Chronic stressors include spatial and social restriction and a lack of agency and predictability. your notes
  • Increased length of stay may produce escape-related behaviours (door scratching, floor digging, whining) and reduced rest. your notes
  • Age differences: younger dogs tend toward bedding chewing and play bouncing; older dogs toward panting and barking. your notes
  • Shelter dogs show less variation in activity than pet dogs (temporal monotony from the fixed schedule). Boredom initially increases activity but over time can lead to wakeful inactivity, fragmented sleep, and a depression-like affective state characterised by loss of responsiveness to opportunities for reward. your notes
  • Environmental disruption can reshape normal activity patterns: automated video analysis found shelter dog in-kennel activity that was normally lower in the afternoon instead remained constant throughout the day during COVID-19 shelter restrictions, compared with the same shelter before the pandemic (Byosiere, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap: three states to distinguish Track changes from intake to differentiate sensitisation (increased activity), habituation (increased or decreased activity), and depression-like affective states (wakeful inactivity, loss of reward responsiveness). your notes

When you identify a stressed animal

  • Stop and reassess: what is causing the stress? What options exist to alleviate it? Is the procedure absolutely necessary right now? Assess needs versus wants and prioritise the need. your notes
  • If stress cannot be avoided: can it wait? Can the dog undergo an anaesthetic event? Is there a different approach? your notes
  • Allowing the owner to remain during a veterinary consultation did not significantly change the dog's stress-related behaviour or handling difficulty, but dogs were more willing to enter the consult room and appeared more relaxed during exploration with their owner present (Girault, The Veterinary Journal, 2022 — see journal abstracts below); however, an anxious owner's own behaviour can intensify their dog's stress signs during the exam, and dogs increasingly sought eye contact with their owner as stress signs rose (Helsly, The Veterinary Journal, 2022 — see journal abstracts below). Bespoke classical music matched to resting heart rate did not reduce salivary cortisol, IgA, or infrared temperature rises during a mock veterinary visit, though it was linked to lower core body temperature and more relaxed lip posture (King, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Long-term stays

  • Keep length of stay as short as possible. Animals staying more than a few days require daily enrichment. your notes
  • Chronic stress from prolonged stays reduces coping, increases fear, anxiety, and frustration, and underlies social withdrawal, repetitive behaviours, and aggression, jeopardising placement. your notes
  • Options: increased enrichment, additional space and choice, foster care. your notes
  • Reproductive stress (oestrus, sex drive) increases urine spraying, marking, fighting, and emotional stress; animals housed long term should be spayed and neutered. your notes
  • Long-term confinement without basic care (daily enrichment and exercise) or where basic care induces stress or compromises safety is unacceptable. ASV
  • Feral animals unable to express natural behaviour, play, or form social bonds: euthanasia is humane when a live outcome (return to field) is not possible in a timely manner. your notes
  • Very fearful dogs are less likely to acclimate and are at high risk for deterioration. For pro-social animals, group housing should be seriously considered; concerns about disease and aggression are minimal in well-managed programmes, and behaviour stabilises within one to two days of adding a new dog. your notes
  • Dogs housed long term (over a year) in no-kill shelters are more often a "dangerous breed", male, and older at admission, are rated higher on aggression and high arousal and lower on amicability, and show more vocalising and yawning before feeding than short-term dogs — though direct welfare-assessment scores did not differ significantly between groups (Raudies, Animals, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Regular structured human interaction can lower baseline cortisol over weeks: shelter dogs participating in a prison-based animal-assisted intervention programme showed a significant decrease in baseline cortisol by the end of the programme, although transportation to and from the venue significantly raised cortisol each session (d'Angelo, Animals, 2021 — see journal abstracts below). The presumed reciprocal benefit does not automatically hold on the human side: in a similar prison-based dog-training programme, human-animal bond quality did not predict improvements in the detained juvenile handlers' own self-reported stress or self-esteem over eight weeks, a caution against assuming AAI benefits both parties equally (Karkdijk, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Age-Related Changes in Gut Health and Behavioral Biomarkers in a Beagle Dog Population
Fernández-Pinteño A, Pilla R, Suchodolski J et al. · Animals · 2025 · 10.3390/ani15020234

Abstract. The gut and the gut microbiome communicate with the nervous system through the gut–brain axis via neuroimmune and neuroendocrine mechanisms. Despite existing research, studies exploring this link in aging dogs are limited. This study aims to examine multiple blood and fecal biomarkers of intestinal health, along with various behavioral indicators based on saliva, blood, observations, and activity, in different age populations (junior: <2 y.o.; adult: 2–7 y.o.; senior: >7 y.o.) of thirty-seven Beagle dogs. In our study, Bacteroides were significantly higher in senior dogs. The relative abundance of Faecalibacterium and Blautia showed age-related trends, higher in senior and junior dogs, respectively. Fecal short-chain fatty acid concentration, especially acetate, increased with age, while propionate was higher in junior dogs. For the behavioral indicators we considered, blood thyroxine concentration, playing, exploring, and total activity were higher in junior dogs. The differences observed between the biomarkers of gut health and behavior, particularly those significant for the age correlations, emphasize the importance of considering age-related factors when studying the gut microbiome and behavior. However, further research is needed to better understand the mechanisms and specific pathways involved in the relationship between the studied biomarkers and age.

Association between behavioral factors and recurrence rate in cats with feline "idiopathic" cystitis
Caudron I, et al · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2025 · 10.1016/j.jveb.2025.02.005

Abstract. Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC) is a lower urinary tract disease associated with significant morbidity and mortality in cats. The aim of our study was to detect potential associations between behavioral parameters and recurrence rates in cats diagnosed with FIC. Our main hypothesis was that anxiety-related behaviors could be overrepresented in cats that will experience a recurrence, compared to cats with a single episode. For this retrospective study, we recruited cats diagnosed with FIC who presented to the Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vétérinaire (CHUV) from March 2019 to February 2022. Cats with concurrent systemic diseases or with any other condition that could explain the urinary signs were excluded. Cat owners were sent an online survey to collect data on their cat’s behavioral parameters, potential recurrences, and whether they complied or not with provided environmental recommendations. Thirty-three surveys from 23 neutered male and 10 spayed female feline patients were received and analyzed. We found a significant association between fear of unfamiliar people and recurrence of FIC: 94% of cats who had recurrences were reported by their owners to show fear of strangers had recurrences compared to 59% of cats in the single episode group (P=0.04). Cats with reported fearful behavior in the presence of familiar people also tended to be overrepresented in the group with recurrences (P=0.08). Factors that did not differ between the two groups include displaying aggression towards humans and other animals, being a victim of aggression, displaying urine marking, owner compliance with the prescribed environmental modifications (P>0.05). Identifying cats with fear and anxiety-related problems and treating them may help decrease the recurrence rate of FIC and may decrease associated mortality. Further larger scale prospective studies are needed.

Effects of species-relevant auditory stimuli on stress in cats exposed to novel environment
Bian Y, et al · Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · 2025 · 10.1080/10888705.2023.2259803

Abstract. Environmental changes like vet visit could cause stress in cats. Studies have attempted to develop stress management strategies targeting sensory systems. Even though species-appropriate music which includes cat affiliative sound (e.g., cats’ purring and suckling sound) has been shown to relieve stress in cats. Little is known whether the cat sound alone works in stress management. This study was conducted to investigate the effects of species-relevant auditory stimuli on stress in cats exposed to a novel environment. During the 28-day experiment periods, 20 cats received four types of sound treatments which included silence (T1), purr of cats (T2), eating sound in cats (T3), and the mixed sound of T2 and T3 (T4) in a novel environment in random orders with intervals of 1 week between treatments. Cats’ behaviors were recorded during each 10-min test. Results showed that T4 reduced visual scanning (P = 0.017) without significantly affecting other behaviors, compared with other treatments. Together, the two types of cat-specific sounds did not exert pronounced effects of relieving stress on cats exposed to a novel environment.

Exploring the impact of a brief positive experience on dogs' performance and stress resilience during a learning task
Julia Miller, Camila Cavalli, Amin Azadian, Alexandra Protopopova · PLoS One · 2025

Abstract. Learning and stress resilience can be influenced by recent experiences. Research has traditionally focused on the effects of negative situations and stressors on subsequent learning and stress resilience, while knowledge is limited regarding the effects of positive experiences. We aimed to examine the impact of a pre-session brief positive experience on dogs' learning and stress resilience. Pet dogs were quasi-randomly assigned to the experimental (n = 20) or control (n = 20) group, counterbalanced for age, sex, and breed clade. Experimental dogs received a session intended to provide a positive experience, which included a 15 min walk on a long leash, human interaction, exploration, playing, and olfactory-based foraging. Control dogs were kept on leash in an office without being allowed to explore nor interact with their owner or the experimenters for 15 min. After 60 s of habituation to the testing room, all dogs were taught to nose-touch the experimenter's hand. After the Learning phase, there was a 2 min Disruption phase, in which a remote-controlled car moved inside of a tub at a distance. Measures included the number of hand touches in each phase, the latency to return to the task, and general stress and affiliative behaviours. No differences were observed in the Learning phase. Surprisingly, experimental dogs exhibited higher stress levels than control dogs during the Disruption phase. These dogs also spent a significantly higher proportion of time in proximity to their owners, which could be interpreted as reassurance-seeking behaviour. Contrary to our predictions, exposure to a brief positive experience did not impact learning and, surprisingly, seemed to have made dogs more susceptible to stress during the Disruption phase. Several possible explanations are discussed, including the possibility of an unintentional induction of a negative emotional state by the termination of the positive experience, as well as differences in arousal, or habituation to the indoor environment.

Insights into shelter dog outcomes using behavioral and biological stress markers
Powell L, Graham K, Grant L, Lenz O, Reinhard CL, Yost H, Anderson E, Watson B · Physiology & Behavior · 2025

Abstract. The research team investigated whether behavioral observations and stress biomarkers could help predict outcomes for shelter dogs. Over nine months, they studied 224 dogs from three Pennsylvania shelters, collecting fecal samples and recording behavioral videos at two timepoints (within 48 hours and 7-9 days after entry). Key findings indicated that at shelter entry, behavior patterns differed significantly by outcome. Dogs returned to owners demonstrated increased panting while those euthanized or transferred showed less time sitting than adopted dogs. After one week, human-directed behaviors became predictive; dogs that were adopted or transferred spent more time standing with their front paws on the cage and wagging tails compared to euthanized animals. Cortisol levels were higher in adopted versus transferred dogs at baseline but showed no significant difference by week one. Immunoglobulin A levels did not meaningfully correlate with outcomes. The authors concluded that in-kennel behavioral observations may provide the most useful information for shelters to assess stress, while biological markers warranted further investigation into how dogs adapt to shelter environments.

Physiological and Behavioral Evaluation of Shelter Dogs During Veterinary Routine Health Checks
Gazzano V, Curadi MC, Baragli P, Mariti C, Cecchi F, Cavallo S, Sacchettino L, Gazzano A · Veterinary Sciences · 2025

Abstract. The researchers evaluated stress responses in 26 shelter dogs during routine veterinary examinations, analyzing behavioral scores alongside physiological and hormonal parameters including heart rate, body temperature, cortisol, oxytocin, serotonin, tryptophan, and interleukin-6. A significant negative correlation was observed between oxytocin and cortisol, particularly in dogs exhibiting relaxed behavior. Oxytocin was also negatively correlated with body temperature, supporting a potential role in modulating stress-induced hyperthermia. No significant associations were found between tryptophan, serotonin, interleukin-6, or other physiological measures and behavioral scores. The absence of correlation between tryptophan and serotonin may be due to blood-brain barrier regulation, while interleukin-6's lack of association suggests further investigation is needed. These findings highlight oxytocin's possible buffering effect on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and suggest that behavioral assessment may offer a more sensitive measure of canine stress than hormonal or physiological parameters alone.

Behavioral, Physiological, and Pathological Approaches of Cortisol in Dogs
Marza · Animals · 2024

Abstract. Cortisol, an essential glucocorticoid hormone, is crucial in regulating the stress response and maintaining physiological and behavioral homeostasis in mammals, including dogs. This review explores cortisol’s physiological and behavioral role in canines, focusing on its effects on stress, immune function, and metabolism. Various methods of measuring cortisol levels in dogs, invasive (blood, saliva, urine) and non-invasive (hair, fecal assays), are discussed regarding their accuracy and practical applications. The review also highlights the influence of different environmental factors, such as shelter conditions, human interaction, and music, on cortisol levels in dogs. Furthermore, the clinical and behavioral implications of abnormal cortisol levels are examined, with particular attention to conditions like Cushing’s disease and stress-related behavioral issues. The findings emphasize the importance of cortisol monitoring in veterinary practice and animal welfare, proposing future research directions to improve canine health and stress management.

Cardiovascular effects of dog-appeasing pheromone in the hospital environment
Minuzzo · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2024

Abstract. Recent research has emphasized the health implications of acute or chronic stress in animals. Hospitalized dogs are susceptible to stress due to various factors, including exposure to a novel environment, invasive procedures, and separation from their primary caregiver. Due to the narrow sample population and broad scope of husbandry conditions, the potential for confounding variables limits the degree by which results can be used to inform causation of the relationships identified. Further research is warranted to replicate this work and explore potential confounders.

Abstract. Background: Chronic stress is implicated in behavioral and health issues in cats, but methods for recognition, evaluation, and measurement of stress are lacking. Cortisol concentration is typically used as an indicator of stress. Objectives: To evaluate use of an enzyme immunoassay to quantitate hair and nail cortisol concentrations (HCC and NCC) in cats and evaluate associations between HCC and NCC and behavioral, physical, and environmental correlates of chronic stress in cats. How to evaluate and manage stress in dogs – A guide for veterinary specialist

Abstract. Stress has a significant impact on the health and well-being of dogs and can seriously affect the quality of daily life, veterinary clinics procedures and shelters routine. That means veterinary specialists need to be armed with valid and convenient tools to assess their patients’ stress levels, both behavioral and physiological. For this review we analyzed 128 articles in order to summarize methods of stress assessment in different clinical and experimental environments, as well as methods to alleviate stress. We have also identified the most common forms of stress-related behavior in various situations. Restless nights? Nocturnal activity as a useful indicator of adaptability of shelter housed dogs.
van der Laan, Vinke, van der Borg, Arndt · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Shelter dogs face the challenge of adapting to a kennel environment. Individual differences in adaptation are known to exist. Resting patterns might be indicative of adaptability to such a novel environment, but need to be evaluated for its usefulness, like every potentially physiological and behavioural parameter. Here, we evaluated nocturnal activity patterns of dogs as indicators of adaptability to novel environments. We measured nocturnal activity (3-axial accelerometer, the Actical®) and two physiological stress parameters, i.e. urinary cortisol/creatinine ratio (UCCR) and body weight in 29 dogs relinquished to a shelter (SD group) in the first two days after intake at the shelter (n = 29), after a 12-day habituation period in the shelter (n = 28) and >6 weeks post-adoption (n = 17). A control group of 29 pet dogs kept at home (CPD group), matching the SD group characteristics, was also assessed for its nocturnal activity and UCCR. Linear mixed model analysis, t-tests and Friedman tests were used to analyse the data. The main findings are: 1) the SD group exhibited higher nocturnal activity (total activity counts, activity duration and number of rest bouts) the first two nights after intake than on night 12, with decreasing inter-individual variances. Compared to the CPD group they showed higher nocturnal activity on night 1 (all p < 0.001) and night 12 (all p ≤ 0.001) except for total activity counts on night 12. We found no ‘first-night effect’, where sleep is disturbed during the first night; nocturnal activity in the shelter did not significantly differ between nights 1 and 2 in the shelter. 2) In line with literature findings, SD group UCCRs were higher shortly after intake than after a 12-day habituation period and after adoption, and higher than in the CPD group. 3) An interaction was found between weight class and both nocturnal activity and UCCR levels: in their first days in the shelter, smaller dogs showing higher levels than larger dogs. 4) Dogs in the SD group lost, on average, 5% of their body weight between intake and the two-week habituation period. In conclusion, nocturnal activity, as measured by an accelerometer, may be a valid parameter to monitor adaptability of dogs to a kennel environment. Monitoring nocturnal activity in this way can be a useful and cost-effective additional indicator for assessing dog welfare.

The effect of owner presence and scent on stress resilience in cats
Behnke A · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. The Secure Base Effect (SBE) refers to a human or non-human animal’s ability to use the presence of a bonded caretaker, or other familiar stimulus, as a source of comfort that facilitates stress reduction and exploration in novel or stressful contexts. Recent research has shown that some pet domestic cats (Felis catus) display SBE in the presence of their human owner, a finding that could be utilized to improve cat welfare. In applied settings, cat owners are often encouraged to leave behind items holding their scent when leaving their cat in a novel location (e.g. boarding facility), so that these items can be provided to the cat if they show signs of separation distress. Although this practice has not be studied scientifically in cats, scent objects have been found to produce SBE in human research under similar conditions. Olfaction is thought to play an important role in the social behavior of domesticated cats, even in early life, as exposure to nest scent has been found to reduce stress in kittens. Thus, the possibility that owner scent might be sufficient to elicit SBE in cats is an important empirical question with applied implications. In the current study we asked whether owner scent would reduce cat anxiety when in an unfamiliar environment, and to what extent this might be related to whether the cat showed evidence of using their owner as a Secure Base. Forty-two adult cats underwent a counterbalanced Secure Base Test that examined the cat’s behavior with the owner present, without the owner present, and with a scent object present. On average, cats displayed a lower frequency of stress-related behaviors when the owner was present, providing support for the presence of SBE. However, this effect was not seen when the cat was alone with the scent object. The results of this research can be used to deepen our understanding of feline social behavior and welfare. These findings also highlight the importance of using evidence based practices to address cat anxiety in applied settings.

Abstract. Research to assess the relationship between cats and humans is in a nascent stage. Some studies have assessed the stress status in cats using physiological indicators, such as the cortisol hormone, but have not focused on the social interaction with humans. Moreover, the role of oxytocin secretion in the relationship between cats and humans remains unclear. In this study, we determined the possibility of quantifying the urinary concentration of oxytocin in cats and assessed the effects of social contact with humans on the levels of urinary oxytocin and cortisol metabolite. Four cats were subjected to two conditions, namely, social (control), and non-social (no social contact with humans) conditions. The urinary concentrations of cortisol and oxytocin under the non-social condition were significantly higher than those under the social condition. The implications of weeklong fostering and co-housing on shelter dog welfare
Gunter LM, Platzer JM, Reed JL, Blade EM, Gilchrist RJ, Barber RT, Feuerbacher EN, Wynne CDL · PeerJ · 2026

Abstract. Meeting the needs of dogs in a typical animal shelter can be challenging. Negative environmental inputs such as excessive noise, restrictive kenneling, and social isolation contribute to compromised welfare. Human-animal interaction, such as a temporary stay outside the shelter in a caregiver's home, has been shown to reduce dogs' cortisol levels and increase rest. The authors investigated the impacts of weeklong fostering on dogs' urinary cortisol and activity at two shelters (one open admission, one managed admission), and at the open admission facility a sub-study explored the effects of co-housing before and after foster care. Dogs' urine was collected for cortisol:creatinine analysis and activity monitors were worn for 17 days (five days in the shelter, seven days in a caregiver's home, five days back in the shelter). In total, 84 dogs participated, yielding 1,385 cortisol:creatinine values and 1,205 activity totals. At both shelters, dogs' cortisol decreased and they spent more time resting during weeklong fostering. No significant differences in cortisol or activity were found pre- versus post-fostering, except more mid-intensity activity in the shelter afterward. Housing with versus without another dog did not affect cortisol pre/post foster, but upon reentry to the shelter dogs living with a familiar dog rested more and engaged in less high activity, indicating a positive welfare effect. Significant cortisol and activity differences were also found between the two shelters. A weeklong reprieve from the shelter, and co-housing with a familiar dog upon return, are two evidence-based interventions that can improve shelter dog welfare.
