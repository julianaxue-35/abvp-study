Canine Development

Behaviour starts before birth and is shaped through windows that close. The exam tests the sequence (prenatal, neonatal, transition, socialisation, juvenile, senior), the peak sensitivities and fear periods, and the consequences of getting the windows wrong.

Exam trap: what is learned in the socialisation window Attention, care-soliciting, bite inhibition, and primary social attachment are all learned here. A pup not socialised in this window is socially handicapped for life and can become neophobic. Livestock guarding dogs housed with sheep in this period form a lifelong social bond with sheep. your notes

Fear periods, play, and bullying

  • During fear periods, reduce novel exposure to a level the pup can recover from quickly, and avoid scheduling traumatic events (adoption, vaccination) inside them. your notes
  • Puppy play needs supervision: a pup bullied even a few times can become fearful, and a pup that bullies successfully can learn to bully more efficiently. your notes
  • Early weaning is linked to destructive behaviour, possessiveness, excessive barking, and fearfulness later. your notes
AVSAB: socialisation before 12 weeks takes precedence over completing the vaccine series The AVSAB position statement (often tested on the ABVP) is that the benefits of careful socialisation in controlled, low-risk environments before 12 weeks outweigh the small infectious-disease risk of attending well-run puppy classes before the vaccine series is complete. std ref

Canine Communication and Body Language

Read the dog in clusters, not in single signs. The exam tests whether you can tell offensive aggression from defensive aggression and submission from a submissive grin, and whether you know the autonomic correlates of "hard eyes" and tail lateralisation.

Visual signals

ChannelWhat to read
PostureLean forward shifts weight onto the front, signalling offensive intent; lean back unloads the front and signals defensive intent. Limbs and weight distribution drive movement.
PiloerectionHackles up anywhere on the body indicates AROUSAL, not a fixed emotion; can occur in play, conflict, or fear.
Tail heightHigh tail signals arousal or excitement; low tail signals fear, submission, appeasement, or affiliation.
Tail movementLong and wagging is engaged; long and still is absent of communication; short tail length adds little.
Tail speedLoose side-to-side is greeting and excitement; low and quick suggests nervousness or timidity; high and fast signals arousal (excitement, interest, or aggression).
Tail-side lateralisationWagging more to the dog's RIGHT side reflects positive valence (processed in the left hemisphere); more to the LEFT side reflects negative valence or withdrawal (right hemisphere).
MouthOpen relaxed mouth is comfortable; tight mouth is emotional or physical discomfort; yawns can be soporific or stress-related.
Commissure"Long lip" pulled back signals fear, distress, or appeasement. "Short lip" pushed forward with a C-shaped mouth and wrinkled muzzle signals aggression.
Submissive grinLips retracted, teeth visible, eyes squinty, forehead smooth, ears pulled back. A subthreshold appeasement gesture, NOT aggression.
EyesHard eye (direct, prolonged gaze with dilated pupils) activates the sympathetic nervous system and signals threat and arousal. Whale eye (sclera visible) signals discomfort or nervousness.
EarsRead the BASE: pressed back means fear, submission, defensive, or aggression depending on the rest of the cluster; forward means interest, attention, or approach.

Emotional state clusters

StateCluster (read together, not in isolation)
Offensive aggressionLean forward; fixed hard eye; raised tail; stiff or frozen; closed mouth; offensive pucker (short lip, C-shape, wrinkled muzzle)
Defensive aggressionPosture pulled back; ears back; tail tucked; increasing distance
Timidity / fearfulnessLow tail (may wag low and quick); tight mouth or yawns; panting or whining; long-lip commissure; whale eye; ears back; trembling; lowered body; cowering or hiding; barking, howling
Alert / attentionEars forward; tail down but not tucked; prolonged focus on the person or object
ArousalPiloerection; tail high and fast (often left-biased); tight mouth and short-lip commissure; panting with hard eyes locked; ears back
Exam trap: submissive grin versus offensive pucker A submissive grin is teeth-visible but with SQUINTY eyes, smooth forehead, and ears back. An offensive pucker is teeth-visible with a wrinkled muzzle, C-shaped mouth, and HARD open eyes. The eyes and the muzzle settle the question. your notes

Acoustic

  • Whine, yelp, growl, howl, bark. Barks are short-range communication and meaning varies with context. your notes
  • Low-pitched, harsh, low-amplitude barks signal aggression (low frequencies are distance-increasing across mammals). your notes
  • High-pitched barks signal fear or desperation. your notes

Olfactory and the vomeronasal organ

  • The vomeronasal organ (VNO) sits below the nasal cavity and is a chemosensory organ specialised for pheromones in faeces, urine, and saliva. Dogs collect samples via the head or anogenital area and route them through the VNO to the olfactory bulb. your notes

Modified-signal breeds: reading dogs with altered anatomy notesstd ref

Selective breeding has altered or eliminated key visual communication signals in some breeds. These animals are not communicating less — their signals are constrained or absent, and the reader must compensate by weighting intact channels more heavily.

ModificationSignals lost or alteredWhat to use instead
Brachycephalic faces (Bulldogs, Pugs, Boxers, French Bulldogs)Lip and muzzle position is permanently compressed. Offensive pucker vs. submissive grin distinction is unreliable; C-shape muzzle and lip tension are masked.Focus on eye expression (hard eye, whale eye, blink rate), ear base position, body weight distribution, and tail. Read the cluster more broadly, giving more weight to non-facial cues.
Docked tails (Dobermans, Boxers, Rottweilers, some working breeds)Tail height, lateralisation, and wag speed are eliminated or severely reduced. Tail-based social signalling is functionally absent.Compensate with full body posture, ear position, piloerection extent, and head orientation. Studies show docked-tail dogs receive more aggressive approaches from intact-tail dogs, confirming the signal loss is real and bidirectional.
Cropped ears (Dobermans, Cane Corsos, some Boxers)Natural ear mobility is surgically fixed in an erect position, eliminating pressed-back (fear/appeasement) and lateral (relaxed) ear signals.Rely on forehead tension, eye expression, and overall body posture. Cropped ears often create a more alert or threatening appearance regardless of the animal's actual emotional state.
Exam trap · body language requires knowing the breed anatomyAssigning an emotional state based on muzzle or lip position in a brachycephalic dog, or based on tail lateralisation in a docked-tail dog, is unreliable. On exam scenario questions involving these breeds, the correct answer will privilege eye expression, weight distribution, and body posture over the absent or reduced signal channel.

Feline Development

The feline socialisation window closes early (2 to 7 weeks), which is the single highest-yield fact in this tab. Genetics, queen nutrition, and queen handling all leave permanent imprints.

Genetics

  • Genetics influence neurodevelopment and neurotransmitter systems, shaping emotional and motivational states. your notes
  • Personality is a confluence of environment and genetics, but the SIRE'S personality is a strong independent predictor: kittens sired by outgoing, friendly toms show more approach and affiliative behaviour with people, less stress around strangers, and more exploration of novel objects. your notes

Prenatal (gestation 63 days)

  • Kittens show preferences for flavours fed to the queen in pregnancy. your notes
  • Protein and calorie restriction (even in the second half of gestation) produces offspring with elevated emotional reactivity and impairments in social interaction, environmental exploration, and learning. your notes

Neonatal (0 to 14 days)

  • Kittens suckle within an hour of birth; they suckle and sleep. They cannot thermoregulate. your notes
  • With eyes and ears closed, kittens orient to the queen and littermates by TACTILE, THERMAL, and OLFACTORY cues. Forelimb paddling predominates; hindlimbs are weak. your notes
  • Hearing comes in by about day 5; eyes open in week 2 (earlier in kittens born to young queens, and earlier in females than males). your notes
  • Kittens hand-reared from 2 weeks (separated from queen and littermates) are more fearful and aggressive toward people and cats, more sensitive to novel stimuli, with poor social and parenting skills and poorer learning. your notes

Socialisation (2 to 7 weeks)

Exam trap: the feline socialisation window closes at 7 weeks Kittens handled by humans between 3 and 14 weeks were more likely to approach humans and accept handling for longer than kittens handled only after 7 weeks or not at all before 14 weeks. Exposure to human handling by 7 weeks is critical for a cat to enjoy human interaction in the future. your notes
  • Shelter-born kittens handled for more than 5 minutes per day were more likely to approach novel toys and unfamiliar people than kittens not handled. your notes
  • Full motor coordination is reached by 7 weeks. your notes
  • Kittens have ultrasonic vocalisations; pitch falls with age. your notes
  • Visual acuity and binocular vision continue to develop into this period. your notes

Feline Communication and Body Language

Cats use vocal, scent, and visual channels and signal differently to humans than to each other. The clusters worth knowing: amicable approach, distress, and the "polite decline" of the wrapped tail.

Vocalisation

  • Used for agonistic, sexual, and queen-kitten encounters, and cats vocalise much more to humans than to other cats. your notes
  • Purr: a care-soliciting behaviour heard in kittens at the nipple, in friendly interactions, AND in mildly anxious or ill cats. The dual meaning matters clinically. your notes
  • Chirr or trill: a closed-mouth meow used as a friendly greeting (often to preferred associates). your notes
  • Agonistic communication is usually OPEN-mouth: hiss, spit, growl, snarl, yowl, shriek. your notes

Scent

  • Urination, defaecation, and scratching deposit chemical information. your notes
  • Sebaceous glands on the head, perianal area, and between the digits leave rich information. Head rubbing (allorubbing) of objects or social targets uses the glands on the temporal head, under the chin, and around the lips. your notes
Social terms worth knowing Allorubbing (rubbing one cat's body or head against another, including humans), allogrooming (grooming another cat), and preferred associates (cats that consistently exchange these affiliative behaviours and rest near each other) are the formal terms used when assessing multi-cat household tension. std ref
Exam trap: marking versus normal voiding Stress-related urine MARKING is usually a VERTICAL surface, in a standing posture, with a quivering tail and little to no scratching. Normal voiding is in a HORIZONTAL squat with substrate scratching afterwards. std ref

Pain-related behavioural indicators notesstd ref

Pain produces specific, observable changes in posture, movement, and behaviour. Recognising them prevents misclassifying pain-driven defensive behaviour as a primary behavioural problem.

Postural and movement signs

  • Hunched or rounded back: abdominal or spinal pain. Often paired with reluctance to move and a hindquarter tuck.
  • Guarded posture: the animal holds a specific body region away from contact — a painful limb held up, a flank held tightly, or the head and neck kept stiff. Attempting to touch the guarded area provokes rapid withdrawal or snapping.
  • Altered weight-bearing: shifting weight off a painful limb; reluctance to jump, climb, or use stairs; hesitation before sitting or rising; short-strided asymmetric gait.

Facial and ocular signs

  • Reduced eye aperture (squinting or partial eye closure): a characteristic indicator of pain in both dogs and cats, particularly head, ear, or dental pain. Validated in the Feline Grimace Scale and the Canine Grimace Scale.
  • Whisker position (cats): whiskers pulled flat or held rigidly back toward the cheeks are a pain indicator included in the Feline Grimace Scale.
  • Ear position in pain (cats): ears rotated outward and slightly lowered — distinct from the fully flattened fear ear — is included in the Feline Grimace Scale.
  • Orbital tightening: squished periocular soft tissue appearance present in both feline and canine grimace scales.

Behavioural signs

  • Sudden onset of aggression or snapping in a previously non-aggressive animal should always prompt a pain workup before a behavioural diagnosis.
  • Decreased appetite, reduced voluntary activity, withdrawal from social contact, and reluctance to interact overlap with stress — if onset is acute or linked to a medical event, pain is the priority differential.
  • Self-directed licking, biting, or scratching at a specific site indicates local pain or discomfort at that location.
Exam trap · pain mimics behaviour problemsA cat presented for "aggression that started last week" whose physical exam reveals dental disease, a fractured tooth, or chronic otitis is not showing a primary behavioural disorder — it is showing pain-elicited defensive aggression. Treating the behaviour without treating the pain will fail. Rule out pain, metabolic disease, and neurological abnormality before attributing behaviour change to a primary behavioural disorder.

Human-dog communication: How body language and non-verbal cues are key to clarity in dog directed play, petting and hugging behaviour by humans
Walsh · Applied Animal Behavior Science · 2024

Abstract. Clarity of communication between humans, intended to convey a message, may be misunderstood, or ambiguous, depending on many influencing variables, including impaired vision/hearing, neuro-divergence, culture, native language accent and gestures. Intraspecific communication in animals has been problematic to define in absolute terms, however, species such as dogs have a robustly researched and documented repertoire of behavioural body language, signalling and vocalizations. Humans tend to exhibit human communicative gestures of affection to their dogs, including hugging, touching, kissing and restraining them. Play is an essential element of the ontogenesis of any puppy/dog; however, it appears that intra and interspecies play in dogs differs in form, function and motivation. This study investigated, compared, and considered, the behaviours/communication which humans exhibit towards their dogs, and those exhibited by dogs towards their humans, during “play, petting and hugging behaviours”. Video-sharing platforms facilitate studying human-dog hugging, petting, and playing behaviour and comparing dogs' non-verbal communication with the comments “like/dislike clicks” of viewers. The current study was conducted on google, investigating media sharing platforms. Study (1) “play” reviewed the 30 most popular videos, and studies (2) “hugging” and (3) “petting”, reviewed the 80 most popular videos on media platforms. Our results showed that many human behaviours may be concerning as humans tend not to necessarily understand the body language or vocalizations shown by dogs, when interacting with them. A high number of stress behaviours were observed in the dogs involved which may have occurred due to misunderstanding of the information a dog is communicating during human-dog play, petting and hugging behaviour. This can cause feelings of discomfort, anxiety, fear, distress, and confusion in a dog, which may result in a fractured human/dog bond, welfare issues for the dog, and the possibility of a dog escalating to exhibit defensive/agonistic behaviour should communication fail. The high prevalence of videos in which stress-related behaviours were identified was concerning, although the expression of a specific behaviour may sometimes depend on the kind of interaction and/or other characteristics of the dyad engaging in it. We advocate for increased education for both adults and children in canine communication, context and in co-operative ways of respecting agency in human-dog interactions.

Online Training Using an Educational Video Improves Human Ability To Identify and Rate Kitten Fear Behavior
To Identify and Rate Kitten Fear Behavior · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2024

Abstract. Introduction: Fear is a negative emotional state that can influence early behavioral development and lead to impairments in animal welfare. For young kittens, excessive fear early in life can instill lasting fearfulness leading to behavioral issues such as aggression, negative impacts on the human–animal bond, potential mistreatment, relinquishment to shelters, and/or euthanasia. Therefore, it is crucial that caretakers can accurately identify when their kittens might be experiencing fear in order to respond appropriately. Methods: We used an online survey to determine: (1) whether members of the general public (n = 761) can accurately identify and rate different severities of fear behavior in young kittens in short video clips (i.e. no fear requiring no intervention, mild fear requiring awareness of possible intervention, and moderate fear requiring immediate intervention); and (2) whether an educational video offering specialized training on identifying kitten behavior can improve human ability to correctly rate kitten behavior in comparison to a general kitten care video. Results: Using mixed logistic regression models, we found no difference at baseline in correct ratings between the two video groups across all fear categories. However, participants who received the specialized behavior training had significantly greater odds of being correct after video training for all three categories compared to participants who received the general kitten care video. Additionally, previous experience with cats and participant personality impacted ratings. Conclusions: Overall, the current study demonstrates that concise and specialized training in identifying kitten behavior is a useful tool for improving human ability to identify and rate fear levels in kittens. This training approach can help strengthen caretaker understanding of kitten behavior to ensure interactions with potentially fear-provoking stimuli are properly mitigated to reduce related welfare impacts. Thus, these types of educational resources are encouraged within shelters, veterinary clinics, research settings, and foster and adoptive homes to improve the welfare of kittens. Multimodal Communication in the Human–Cat Relationship: A Pilot Study
de Mouzon · Animals · 2023

Abstract. Across all species, communication implies that an emitter sends signals to a receiver, through one or more channels. Cats can integrate visual and auditory signals sent by humans and modulate their behaviour according to the valence of the emotion perceived. However, the specific patterns and channels governing cat-to-human communication are poorly understood. This study addresses whether, in an extraspecific interaction, cats are sensitive to the communication channel used by their human interlocutor. We examined three types of interactions—vocal, visual, and bimodal—by coding video clips of 12 cats living in cat cafés. In a fourth (control) condition, the human interlocutor refrained from emitting any communication signal. We found that the modality of communication had a significant effect on the latency in the time taken for cats to approach the human experimenter. Cats interacted significantly faster to visual and bimodal communication compared to the “no communication” pattern, as well as to vocal communication. In addition, communication modality had a significant effect on tail-wagging behaviour. Cats displayed significantly more tail wagging when the experimenter engaged in no communication (control condition) compared to visual and bimodal communication modes, indicating that they were less comfortable in this control condition. Cats also displayed more tail wagging in response to vocal communication compared to the bimodal communication. Overall, our data suggest that cats display a marked preference for both visual and bimodal cues addressed by non-familiar humans compared to vocal cues only. Results arising from the present study may serve as a basis for practical recommendations to navigate the codes of human–cat interactions.

The Power Is in the Word—Do Laypeople Interpret Descriptors of Dog Emotional States Correctly?
Heritier · Animals · 2023

Abstract. A basic understanding of dog behaviour and emotion is relevant not only for professionals, such as veterinary personnel or dog trainers, but also for dog owners and for people with little contact with dogs. Information about dog behaviour and emotions is mostly conveyed verbally. This study explores whether definitions of dog behaviour and emotion are understood in such a way that they can be allocated to a descriptor (i.e., a label such as “fearful”), even by people with low background knowledge. If people can match descriptors to definitions, this suggests that the definitions are distinct enough and elicit mental representations of behaviour that can fit the label. Good agreement on the definitions is a prerequisite for the validity of the descriptors used; however, no study to date has tested this. A sample of 236 adults was asked to match descriptors of Qualitative Behaviour Assessment (QBA) for veterinary and shelter situations to their correct definitions, e.g., the descriptor “fearful” to its definition “dog may try to flee, hide or freeze; ears back”. Matching was substantially above chance; nonetheless, the mean proportion of correct responses was only 50% (SD ± 16.6%) for the veterinary QBA set and 33% (SD ± 14.3%) for the shelter QBA set. Performance in the matching task was positively correlated with measures of experience with dogs. Taken together, the results suggest that descriptor–definition pairs used to describe dog behaviour need to be clearly defined to avoid misinterpretations when teaching laypeople how to interpret canine behaviour.

An exploratory study on dogs’ vocalizations towards their owner and food in an unsolvable task
Gaunet, F · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2022

Abstract. Despite ongoing research, many aspects of dogs’ vocal communication are not yet fully understood, including how they convey information about items. The aim of the present exploratory study was to determine whether dogs vocalize differently toward their owner and food in an unsolvable task where food is inaccessible and to characterize the acoustic composition of canine vocalizations. In Savalli et al. (2014; 2016), food was placed in an inaccessible location with the dog present. Next, with the owner present, the communicative modalities of the dogs were recorded, including their vocalizations. Only 21.6% of the fifty-one dogs vocalized (11 dogs out of 51; 189 vocalizations); 32.27% of the vocalizations were whines and whine-related, and 67.72% were barks and bark-related. Vocalizations may thus be a secondary modality of communication or, more likely, most of the dogs had previously learned from their owners not to vocalize in food-request situations by lack of reinforcement. We characterized four types of whines and five types of barks, respectively making up 1/3 and 2/3 of the vocalizations recorded. No other type of vocalization was found. We found firstly that the majority of the vocalizations (67.7%) were directed at the owner. Secondly, whines were rarely directed at food. Thirdly, though fundamental frequency (F0) and duration did not statistically differ for each type of vocalization, whether directed at the target or at the owner, we found a tendency of specific sub-unit of a bark to have a higher F0 and lower duration when dogs gazed at the target compared when the gazed at the owner. This type of vocalizations was thus not used similarly according to the target viewed, indicating that dogs employ different types of vocal coding to call for attention. Given the small number of dogs who vocalized in this study, future studies will involve a larger group of dogs who will be tested outdoors with an inaccessible toy to enable more vocalizations. This exploratory study opens perspectives in understanding needs expressed by dogs.

Student veterinarians’ ability to recognize behavioral signs of stress in dogs
Menor-Campos · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2022

Abstract. Veterinarians’ perceptions of animal stress influence their practice. Therefore, the aim of this research was to evaluate how veterinary students perceive stress in dogs. Two hundred and eighteen fourth year veterinary students of the University of Cordoba (Spain) participated in the study. An online questionnaire measuring veterinary students’ perceptions of behavioral indicators of stress in dogs, their attachment to pets, and demographic measures (e.g., pet ownership, student gender) was administered to participants in university classes. Data show that veterinary students easily identified some stress indicators such as stereotypical behaviors, excessive barking and aggressiveness, but they were less likely to identify yawning, low activity and paw raising as indicators of stress. Understanding the basis of stress and having a companion animal influenced stress identification but participants’ gender, and attachment level to their pets, had no effect on ability to identify canine stress indicators. Misunderstandings about canine behavior might influence veterinary students’ ability to recognize subtle stress signs in dogs. However, a good understanding of the psycho-physiological basis of stress and personal experiences of dog ownership were associated with greater ability to identify behavioral stress signs in dogs, suggesting that additional training in canine stress might be beneficial for student veterinarians.

Canine Olfaction: Physiology, Behavior, and Possibilities for Practical Applications
Kokocinska-Kusiak, A · Animals · 2021

Abstract. Olfaction in dogs is crucial for gathering important information about the environment, recognizing individuals, making decisions, and learning. It is far more specialized and sensitive than humans’ sense of smell. Using the strength of dogs’ sense of smell, humans work with dogs for the recognition of different odors, with a precision far exceeding the analytical capabilities of most modern instruments. Due to their extremely sensitive sense of smell, dogs could be used as modern, super-sensitive mobile area scanners, detecting specific chemical signals in real time in various environments outside the laboratory, and then tracking the odor of dynamic targets to their source, also in crowded places. Recent studies show that dogs can detect not only specific scents of drugs or explosives, but also changes in emotions as well as in human cell metabolism during various illnesses, including COVID-19 infection. Here, we provide an overview of canine olfaction, discussing aspects connected with anatomy, physiology, behavioral aspects of sniffing, and factors influencing the olfactory abilities of the domestic dog (Canis familiaris).

Factors associated with sniffing behaviors between walking dogs in public places
Holcova, K · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2021

Abstract. Although body sniffing is the most frequent canine interaction in public places, little is known about olfactory behaviors between walking dogs. The aim of this study was to examine the association of dog, human and behavior characteristics with the number of sniffing dogs within a dyad, with the initiation and termination of sniffing, with the first and last area sniffed on the recipient’s body and with the number of areas sniffed on the recipient’s body in walking dogs in public places. We observed 538 dyadic encounters between sexually intact dogs, each led by one owner. We randomly selected one dog from each of 538 dyads, yielding 486 focal dogs actively involved in sniffing for analysis. Interacting dogs were more likely to engage in mutual than single dog sniffing when the owners communicated with each other than when they did not. Dogs more likely initiated sniffing than not when a male dog encountered a female than vice versa. Dogs more likely sniffed the rear than the head as the first area on the recipient’s body when only one dog sniffed the other than when both dogs sniffed each other; and also when they initiated sniffing than when they did not. Dogs more likely sniffed more than one area than only one area on the recipient’s body when they sniffed the head or abdomen than the rear as the first area on the recipient’s body; when both dogs sniffed one another than when only one dog sniffed the other; and also when they initiated sniffing than when they did not. Dogs more likely sniffed the abdomen or rear than the head as the last area on the recipient’s body, when only one dog sniffed the other than when both dogs sniffed one another; and also when they sniffed more than one area on the recipient’s body than when they sniffed only one area. Dogs more likely terminated sniffing than not when an adult dog encountered a puppy than vice versa. In conclusion, dyadic sniffing behaviors between sexually intact dogs, each walking with one owner, were mainly associated with the owners’ communication, the number of sniffing dogs within a dyad, the dog’s sex and age and with the initiation of sniffing.

Heads and Tails: An Analysis of Visual Signals in Cats, Felis catus
Deputte B · Animals · 2021

Abstract. Visual communication involves specific signals. These include the different positions of mobile body elements. We analyzed visual configurations in cats that involve ears and the tail. We aimed at deciphering which features of these configurations were the most important in cats’ interactions with other cats and with humans. We observed a total of 254 cat–cat interactions within a sample of 29 cats, during a total of 100 h of observation scheduled with the “Behavioral dependent onset of sampling” method and using the “All occurences” sampling method. In addition, we sampled 10 interactions between cats and humans. In cat– cat interactions, we noted the positions of ears and tail of both protagonists, as well as the outcome of the interaction, which was either positive/neutral or negative. In a great majority of the 254 interactions sampled, both cats held their tail down. On the contrary, ear position was a critical element in predicting the outcome. When both partners held their ears erect, the outcome was significantly positive, such as rubbing or close proximity. In all other cases of the position of ears in both cats, the outcome was negative, with increased distance of the partners. Although the tail did not seem to play a significant role in visual configurations in cat interactions, the “tail-up” display was important when a cat approached a human being. In the vast majority of cases the cat rubbed itself on a human’s leg(s). Thus, we may conclude that the presence of a human has a specific meaning in the cat’s world, probably as the result of a long period of commensalism. It is important for pet owners to understand the signals that cats use with other cats and with humans in order to promote the welfare of cats.
