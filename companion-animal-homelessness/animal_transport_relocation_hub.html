Relocation Framework, Goals and Regulations

Relocation programs transfer animals from a source organisation (typically a community with excess pet population) to a destination organisation (typically one with unmet adopter demand). Every design decision is judged against the welfare of all affected animals and against length of stay (LOS).

Core principles

  • Intentionally designed relocation programs weigh the health, behaviour and safety risks and benefits for all affected animals and minimise negative impacts through careful selection and planning.
  • Decision making must prioritise a decrease in LOS. Holding animals for relocation when live outcomes are available locally pushes shelters beyond their Capacity for Care (C4C) and damages community relationships.
  • When destination shelters accept more animals than they have capacity to care for, welfare is compromised for both relocated and resident animals, and LOS increases.
  • It is UNACCEPTABLE to transport an animal when the transport itself is likely to be harmful to its immediate or long-term health or welfare.

Relocation program models: risk and cost gradients textbook Fig 20.2

Risk and operating cost both rise along three independent axes: how far the animal travels (geographic), how many organisations are involved (population), and how the animal moves (transport mode). A program can sit at a different point on each axis.

Responsibilities of the program ASV 2022 notes

Regulatory and legal

  • All participants must follow federal transport regulations plus local and state regulations for both source and destination.
  • Departments of Agriculture and Departments of Health commonly require health certificates (CVIs), specific vaccinations, and age or health restrictions for imported animals. For commercial air, the airline must be consulted.

Emergency planning (made before transport)

  • Emergency contact information; safe locations to stop; protocols for vehicle problems; a plan for animal and human medical emergencies. Travelling animals must have contact information for both source and destination.

Agreements, records and metrics

  • Written agreements between all parties, reviewed regularly, addressing at minimum medical and behavioural selection criteria plus transportation and destination requirements.
  • A contact person must be identified at each transfer point, and a travel record kept from source to destination to allow tracing of source and contacts along the route.
  • Track standard metrics: animal demographics, behavioural and medical conditions, and outcomes.
Exam trap · the no-return resource paradox Unless there are extenuating circumstances, animals should NOT be returned to the source, even when unexpected medical or behavioural concerns appear at the destination. Transport is a major physiological stressor and a heavy resource investment. Programmatic failures are corrected by revisiting selection criteria and goals, not by re-shipping the animal.

Data collection and reporting ASV 2022 notes

  • At minimum, both source and destination track the number of animals relocated, the incidence of unanticipated medical and behavioural concerns, and outcomes.
  • Tracking total LOS (source plus destination) captures the animal's overall time in the system and complements outcome metrics.
  • Collect non-urgent health and outcome data within 14 days of relocation and perform a quarterly analysis of aggregate data to catch the majority of individual, partner and programmatic concerns in time to respond.
Exam trap · 14-day data collection and quarterly analysis are two distinct timelines Non-urgent individual animal data must be collected within 14 days of relocation (not 24 hours, not 30 days). Aggregate programmatic data is reviewed on a quarterly basis (not monthly, not annually). An exam question asking about either interval expects the precise value.

International transport considerations ASV 2022 notes

  • Before accepting transport from an international source, organisations should weigh the needs of partners closer to home and the impact of the international partnership on their own mission.
  • Selection criteria must account for suitability for adoption, disease management, long-term quality of life, and the effects of extensive travel.
  • Shelters accepting international transports should have a written policy statement explaining the decision.
  • Transparency gaps are common among international rehoming organisations: a homepage content analysis of 241 organisations rehoming dogs from southern and eastern Europe to Germany found many lacked information on the dogs' origin, transport method, or health status, blurring the line between rescue and illegal trade; behavioural descriptions were included in only about two-thirds of adoption advertisements, raising the risk of post-adoption relinquishment for behavioural unsuitability; and some organisations failed to use the mandatory EU TRACES transport-tracking system (Graf, Animal Welfare, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Canada has restricted commercial imports over zoonotic risk: the Canadian government placed a temporary suspension on all commercial dog imports from non-rabies-free countries because of growing concern about zoonotic disease risk from international rescue dogs (von Rentzell et al., PLOS ONE, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Falsified rabies documentation is the leading cause of denied entry into the US: CDC data show 458 dogs were denied entry in 2020, a 52% increase over the 2018–2019 average, primarily (56% of denials) for falsified rabies vaccination certificates; just three countries of origin accounted for 74% of all denied-entry dogs (Pieracci et al., PLOS ONE, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
Gap filled · named regional zoonoses (ASV 2022, 11.2) The ASV chapter specifically names plague, rabies and leptospirosis as regionally distributed zoonoses requiring special consideration, alongside aggressive behaviours. Heartworm (Dirofilaria immitis) is a relevant vector-borne consideration in relocation but is not a classic zoonosis — human infection is rare and aberrant. Keep heartworm separate from the named zoonoses on the exam.
Gap filled · the manifest must survive an accident (ASV 2022, 11.3) Beyond the travel record, ASV requires that a copy of the transport manifest (identifying every animal on board) is kept accessible and separate from the vehicle itself, so it survives if an accident destroys the copy travelling with the animals. A cloud-based digital manifest shared in real time with source, transporter and destination satisfies this.

Source and Destination Responsibilities

The source organisation carries the bulk of preventive and screening duties before transport. The destination carries the intake assessment and housing responsibilities on arrival. Both share accountability for animal selection, communication and outcomes.

Vaccination, parasites and heartworm ASV 2022 notes

  • All eligible animals in the source population must be vaccinated at or before intake. Vaccinating only the animals selected for relocation is insufficient because it leaves the majority of the population unprotected.
  • It is not recommended to hold animals back from transport just to allow a vaccine response or a booster.
  • Treatment for fleas, ticks and internal parasites is strongly recommended.
  • Ideally, all dogs 6 months of age and older are tested for heartworm disease prior to relocation.
Exam trap · vaccinate the population, not just the travellers A tempting wrong answer is "vaccinate only the animals selected for transport." The standard is population-wide vaccination at or before intake. Selective vaccination of only the transport pool is explicitly inadequate.

Records, certificates and identification ASV 2022

  • Health and behaviour records must be shared with the destination. When required, a valid CVI and proof of rabies vaccination must accompany each animal (requirements vary by state).
  • Visual identification is required: collars or tags routinely, with alternatives such as marking the inner ear or painting a claw on a neonate. Microchipping before transport is ideal because it provides permanent identification.
  • Each primary transport enclosure must be marked with the animal's unique identifier.

The fit-for-transport examination ASV 2022 notes

  • Animals must be examined by trained staff within 24 hours prior to travel and deemed fit for transport.
  • Purpose: look for evidence of infectious disease and evaluate the animal's ability to tolerate the physical and emotional experiences of travel (prolonged confinement, handling by different people, direct exposure to other animals).
  • A veterinarian must confirm fitness for transport for any animal with medical concerns or recovering from surgery.
Who clears whom Trained staff can screen and clear apparently healthy animals within the 24-hour window. A licensed veterinarian is specifically required to clear animals with medical concerns or post-surgical recovery. Exam options naming the driver, an agriculture inspector, or a foster coordinator are wrong.

Animal selection criteria ASV 2022 notes

At minimum, animals considered for relocation should be:

  • Easily handled by a variety of people.
  • Able to cope with prolonged periods of confinement.
  • Not exhibiting overt aggression toward any species of animal that may also be participating in the transport.

Animals with known behavioural health concerns should NOT be considered for relocation unless: their safety AND the safety of human personnel can be ensured during the journey, AND their best opportunity for continued management and improved health and welfare lies at the destination.

Destination responsibilities ASV 2022 notes

  • Sufficient trained personnel ready to receive and evaluate animals on arrival.
  • Each animal receives a brief health assessment at intake to identify infectious disease and problems needing emergency or follow-up care.
  • Adequate housing prepared without displacing the existing population.
  • Isolation or quarantine need is informed by regulatory requirements, animal health status, and infectious disease risk. Routine quarantine for all transferred animals is not mandated — unnecessary holds increase LOS and harm animal health and organisational goals.
Key recommendation · spay/neuter at the destination, not the source Research shows that performing S/N at the destination shelter balances resources such as surgical capacity, LOS, and community needs. Data demonstrate that travel does not impact the postoperative complication rate, which is 2% within 2 weeks of relocation regardless of when S/N is performed. Performing S/N at the source adds length of stay there without reducing complications.
Gap filled · aggregation of sources (ASV 2022, 11.4.5) If animals from different sources cannot travel on separate vehicles, they should ideally be housed in separate compartments, with protocols in place to minimise exposure and cross-contamination between populations.
Gap filled · importing animals under 8 weeks (ASV 2022, 11.4.2) Importing animals less than 8 weeks old may be prohibited in some states. This is a regulatory constraint to check at the destination jurisdiction before planning pediatric transports.

Sedation and behaviour medications ASV 2022 notes

  • Should be considered when an animal is likely to have emotional welfare concerns during transport.
  • Psychopharmaceuticals must only be used under veterinary direction, and clear communication between partners is essential when behaviour medications are used.
  • Safe programs do not use sedatives to compensate for poor transport practices.
Exam trap · sedation to the point of incapacity is unacceptable It is UNACCEPTABLE to transport animals sedated or anaesthetised to the point they are unable to swallow, walk or thermoregulate. These animals face severe risk of: choking, aspiration pneumonia, hypothermia, and cardiac or respiratory arrest without continuous monitoring by trained medical personnel.

Vehicles, Climate Control and Safety

Engineering controls protect animals from heat, exhaust and injury. The high-yield numbers cluster here: temperature and humidity bounds, ventilation geometry, and the reasons factory cargo climate units are rejected.

Vehicle climate parameters ASV 2022 notes

Memorise both the absolute safety bounds and the narrower comfort target. Ground and air limits are different — a common exam trap.

Vehicle engineering rules

  • Factory-installed cargo climate control units must be avoided. They are designed for static, non-heat-producing loads (the classic example is flowers) and lack the airflow, heat distribution and power to clear the metabolic body heat of live animals.
  • The recommendation is a roof-mounted climate unit, plus insulation of sides, roof and floor, and an insulated floor mat (covered with bedliner or diamond-plate flooring) to block road heat reaching the lowest tier of enclosures.
  • Control over heating and cooling in the animal compartment is essential; alarms help when drivers and animals are in separate compartments.
  • Temperature and humidity sensors must be placed at animal level where kennels are located — not just at driver cab level.
  • A dual-control remote monitoring unit with programmed alarms at predetermined setpoints is required.
  • A battery-operated carbon monoxide (CO) detector is placed in the animal compartment to protect all occupants from an undetected exhaust leak.
  • Interior temperatures in direct sunlight can rapidly exceed safe levels even when it is comfortable outside.
  • Small vehicles carrying few animals per trip are preferred.
  • Scented air freshener sprays and essential oils are NOT recommended in transport programs.
Exam trap · ground vs air temperature limits are different values Ground transport: 7.2°C (45°F) minimum, 29.5°C (85°F) maximum. Air transport (cargo hold on ground): -7°C (19°F) minimum, 27°C (81°F) maximum; loading is banned if interior exceeds 29°C (84°F). These are distinct numerical sets. Do not apply ground transport limits to air transport questions or vice versa.

Kennel selection notes

  • Sound, appropriately sized, and matched to the length of transport; must not collapse; minimises injury or escape; easy to clean and disinfect; durable for repeated use.
  • Use a disinfectable barrier (e.g. roll-down outdoor solar shades) to reduce visual stimulation and act as a physical barrier to respiratory droplets.
  • Choose kennels that can be removed without taking the animal out of the safety of the kennel; built-in cargo control bars help achieve this.

Enclosure Standards and Species-Specific Protocols

Enclosure size, ventilation geometry and the unfamiliar-animals rule are non-negotiable engineering standards. Species-specific protocols layer additional behavioural and physiological requirements on top.

Primary enclosure standards ASV 2022 notes

Sizing requirement

Large enough for the animal to: stand and sit erect, turn around normally while standing, and lie in a natural position without lying on another animal.

Structural requirements

  • No sharp edges; flooring must prevent injury, discomfort and leakage of fluids into other enclosures. Absorbent bedding must be provided unless it poses a risk to the individual animal.
  • Vent openings on at least 3 sides, with at least 1 inch (2 cm) of unobstructed space between vent openings and adjacent structures.
  • If an enclosure is permanently fixed so only a single door ventilates, that door must face an unobstructed aisle.
  • Enclosures must be secured against movement, doors secured against accidental opening, and operators must be able to remove animals swiftly in an emergency.
  • Use a disinfectable visual barrier (such as roll-down outdoor solar shades) to reduce visual stimulation and act as a physical barrier to respiratory droplets.
Exam trap · unfamiliar animals in the same enclosure Unfamiliar animals MUST NOT be transported together in the same primary enclosure. There are no exceptions based on size, age, temperament assessment, or duration of travel. This is a hard rule.

The two-driver rule exists because fatigue is a major crash risk: as little as 1–2 hours of lost sleep can raise crash risk 2 to 10-fold, a magnitude comparable to driving under the influence. Avoid odd-hour or overnight transports for this reason, and never treat two drivers as optional on a long haul just because both animals and cargo appear settled.

Overnight and unattended care

  • When feasible, an overnight facility that houses cats and dogs off the vehicle is preferred (foster homes, shelters, hotels, or transport hubs).
  • Animals should never be left unattended unless monitoring is sufficient and attendants can immediately respond to needs.

Behavioural Stress, Sedation Evidence and Outcomes

Transport is a measurable physiological and behavioural stressor. This tab covers the stress-biomarker studies, the evidence for anxiolytics, and disease and adoption outcome data after relocation.

Transport stress physiology study data notes

  • Beagles after 22 hours of long-distance ground transport took 2 weeks to return serum cortisol and total bilirubin to normal.
  • Beagles travelling 9.5 hours had elevated cortisol and corticosterone during ground transport that returned to baseline overnight.
  • Beagles flown showed increased plasma cortisol after ground transport, increased salivary cortisol after both ground and air, and higher neutrophil with lower lymphocyte counts after both modes. Heart rate was significantly higher during loading than during take-off, flight, landing or turnaround; the largest heart-rate increases were during loading and unloading.
  • In flight, dogs were lying down about 50% of the time and inactive about 75% of the time; the most common behaviours were sitting and lip licking.
  • 44% of owned dogs that travelled were reported to show anxiety, fear and motion sickness. Nausea-consistent behaviours were ameliorated by a dog-appeasing pheromone collar in one report.
  • Commercial-breeding-kennel puppies show measurable transport distress by ~48 hours post-arrival: in 383 eight-week-old puppies tested pre- and post-transport, arrival was followed by decreased exploration, increased locomotion and escape attempts, and increases in both fecal glucocorticoid metabolites and fecal secretory IgA; affiliative behaviour also increased post-transport, suggesting puppies used social contact as a coping strategy despite good physical health at both time points (Romaniuk et al., Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · heart rate peaks during loading, not flight The intuitive wrong answer is that heart rate peaks at take-off or during turbulence. The data show the largest heart-rate increases occur during loading and unloading. The flight itself was relatively stable, with animals mostly inactive.

Sedatives and anxiolytics: evidence ASV 2022 study data

  • Psychopharmaceuticals must only be used under veterinary direction, and clear communication between partners is essential when behaviour medications are used.
  • Safe programs do not use sedatives to compensate for poor transport practices.
  • Clomipramine evidence: dogs given clomipramine twice daily (BID) starting 3 days before transport had a significantly smaller rise in plasma cortisol, a lower neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, lower average heart rate during transport, and drooled less often than placebo.
  • Gabapentin in cats (van Haaften et al. 2017): reduced stress scores, aggression, and heart rate versus placebo, with increased sedation scores. About 30% of cats had mild side effects observed at the clinic, but 90% had side effects noted at home (ataxia, vomiting, hypersalivation) — owners should be warned these are more common than the in-clinic picture suggests.
  • Trazodone in cats (Stevens et al. 2016): improved handling and reduced anxiety signs for transport-related anxiety, with minimal side effects (one transient report of sleepiness).
  • Carrier-acclimation training (Gruen et al. 2013) is a non-pharmaceutical alternative: a structured protocol for acclimating cats to handling and carriers, reducing reliance on medication where time allows.
  • Air travel exception: sedation is prohibited by commercial airlines, and altitude can amplify sedative effects — over-sedation is implicated in roughly half of canine air-travel deaths. An animal that requires sedation to travel safely should not fly; pursue an alternative adoption or transport pathway instead.
Exam trap · clomipramine protocol: BID x 3 days BEFORE transport The key details: clomipramine is given twice daily (BID), starting 3 days before transport — not on the day of transport, not as a single dose, not as acepromazine. An exam option describing acepromazine at loading or a single-dose administration is a distractor.

Key finding · CIRDC is the highest post-transport disease concern in dogs While most disease incidences were below 5% in dogs at destination and 30-day follow-up, canine infectious respiratory disease complex (CIRDC) reached 17% at 30-day follow-up. This is significantly higher than other conditions and should inform post-transport monitoring protocols and destination shelter infection control practices.
Key clinical finding · FURI risk factors in cats In the feline outcome study, three factors were associated with increased FURI prevalence: younger age, longer transport duration, and longer length of stay at the destination. These factors inform selection criteria and transport planning for feline populations.
Exam trap · spay/neuter timing and complications The recommendation is to perform S/N at the destination, and transport does not raise the baseline 2% complication rate. An option claiming travel increases S/N complications, or that S/N must be done at the source 14 days before loading, is wrong.
Key finding · CPV incidence stays low with basic biosecurity — and the predictors are counterintuitive In a large-scale structured relocation program (4,088 puppies, 8 to 19 weeks old), the post-transport canine parvovirus (CPV) diagnosis incidence was only 2.3%. The number of pre-transport vaccinations, length of stay at the source shelter, and the time between pre-transport vaccination and transport were not associated with CPV diagnosis count. CPV incidence was instead lower in puppies aged 13 to 19 weeks, in puppies transferred into the source shelter (rather than owner-surrendered/stray directly), and in puppies transported during summer. When basic biosecurity and vaccination protocols are in place, transport timing should weigh these factors rather than simply maximizing the number or timing of pre-transport vaccine doses (DiGangi et al., Animals, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
Key finding · internationally rehomed dogs are not a behavioural liability, but have a distinct fear profile Using the C-BARQ behavioural questionnaire, dogs rehomed to Germany from southern and eastern Europe scored lower on excitability than Western European dogs, but higher on non-social fear and stranger-directed fear (Eastern European dogs scoring higher than Southern European dogs on the latter). Southern dogs were more prone to chasing; Eastern dogs showed more stranger-directed aggression. Dogs raised in shelters (versus as strays) were more trainable but had higher stranger-directed fear, and dogs under one year old at transport scored higher on trainability. Overall severe behavioural disorder rates were low: 6.7% anxiety, 2.1% aggression, 3.4% abnormal repetitive behaviours (Graf, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). Separately, in two Canadian owner surveys (n=803 and n=878), owners of imported dogs did not report a poorer owner-dog relationship than owners of domestically-born dogs — if anything, owners of Canadian-born dogs used harsh training methods more often and held higher behavioural expectations (von Rentzell et al., PLOS ONE, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Data collection and reporting ASV 2022 notes

  • At minimum, both source and destination track the number of animals relocated, the incidence of unanticipated medical and behavioural concerns, and outcomes.
  • Tracking total LOS (source plus destination) captures the animal's overall time in the system.
  • Collect non-urgent health and outcome data within 14 days of relocation; perform a quarterly analysis of aggregate data to catch programmatic concerns in time to respond.
  • These findings then inform selection and preparation policies, improving short-term success and long-term program viability.

Animal welfare organisations that rehome dogs from southern and eastern Europe to Germany: A homepage content analysis
Graf · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.3390/ani2020144

Abstract. Each year, over 100,000 dogs are imported into Germany from other EU countries by animal welfare organisations, mainly from Romania. This study conducted a systematic content analysis of websites belonging to 241 animal welfare organisations that rehome dogs from southern and eastern Europe to Germany. Assessment included transparency, legal compliance, and availability of educational and medical information for adopters. The study shows that many organisations lacked information regarding dogs' origins, transport methods, or health status which sometimes makes it difficult to distinguish between dog rescue and illegal dog trade. Photos were mainly used in adoption advertisements and behavioural descriptions were only included in two-thirds of the dog advertisements which poses a risk of increased dog relinquishment post-adoption due to behavioural unsuitability. Information on vector-borne diseases and typical behaviour of imported rescue dogs was not provided comprehensively. Few organisations violated legal standards, offering underage or banned breeds and failing to use the TRACES transport system. Most organisations relied upon private foster homes, while few had no temporary housing available in Germany. Although most claimed to conduct pre-adoption checks, comprehensive contract details were rarely published. The number of existing animal welfare organisations that rehome dogs from southern and eastern Europe to Germany is unable to be determined due to high fluctuation and the lack of central registry. Inadequate health disclosures and behavioural descriptions risk poor adoption matches and increased returns. Lack of legal compliance may endanger both animal and public welfare and opens the door to illegal dog trade. Sustainable animal protection requires better adopter education, reliable medical testing, and local engagement in source countries to reduce reliance upon transnational rehoming.

Behavioral traits of rescue dogs from Southern and Eastern Europe rehomed to Germany
Graf, J Vet Behav · J Vet Behav · 2025 · 10.1016/j.jveb.2025.01.005

Abstract. Over the past twenty years, dogs from Southern and Eastern Europe have been increasingly frequently rehomed to Germany and other Western European countries by animal welfare organizations. The aim of this study was to assess behavior and temperament of these dogs using the Canine Behavioral Assessment and Research Questionnaire (C-BARQ©) and to compare them with dogs from Western Europe. The study shows that foreign rescue dogs can be suitable pets and owners are often satisfied with their behavior. They show differences in all behavior scores. The greatest difference was found for ‘excitability’, which was lower in the non-Western European dogs. They also showed significantly higher scores for ‘non-social fear’ and ‘stranger-directed fear’, with Eastern European dogs scoring higher than Southern European dogs. Due to the study’s limitations (e.g. different median age, limited information on the dog’s origin in the native group, owner-derived behavioral information) the comparison is impaired and further investigation is required. While Southern dogs are more prone to chasing, Eastern European dogs display more ‘stranger-directed aggression’. Dogs that grew up in shelters are more trainable than stray dogs, but also have higher ‘stranger-directed fear’. Dogs that were less than one year old at the time of transport scored higher in ‘trainability’. Overall, the number of dogs with severe behavioral disorders was 6.7% for anxiety, 2.1% for aggression and 3.4% for abnormal repetitive behaviors. These dogs are considered to be enormously welfare compromised. In conclusion, dogs from Southern and Eastern Europe can be rehomed as pets if carefully selected and prepared. • Rescue dogs from Southern and Eastern Europe are suitable as pets for German owners. • The primary behavioral problems are stranger-directed and non-social fear. • Behavioral differences depend on origin, individual history and age at transport.

“It’s my calling”, Canadian dog rescuers’ motives and experiences for engaging in international dog rescue efforts
von Rentzell · PLOS ONE · 2024

Abstract. The importation of rescue dogs has become an increasingly common occurrence in recent years, often involving industrialized countries as the ultimate destination. However, international dog rescue activities have attracted considerable criticism from the veterinary community and the public due to the associated zoonotic disease and public health risks, as well as the potential poor behaviour of international rescue dogs. The Government of Canada has also recently placed a temporary suspension on all commercial dog imports from non-rabies free countries due to the growing concerns of the zoonotic disease risks. To understand the perspectives and experiences of stakeholders involved in dog import activities in Canada, we interviewed nine members in leadership positions of Canadian-based international dog rescue organizations. Thematic analysis of interview dialogue yielded three themes: 1) Motive, which described the reason behind participants’ involvement in international dog rescue; 2) Challenge, which described the major difficulties faced in participants’ dog rescue work; 3) Duty, which described participants’ beliefs on responsible dog rescue practices. Members of international dog rescue organizations described being driven by strong desires to provide animal and humanitarian aid. However, local dog rescue efforts were constrained by logistical and societal barriers unique to the Canadian context. Additionally, the current study revealed both similarities and differences in occupational experiences between international dog rescue organizations and other animal care professions. Specifically, difficulties with the emotional burden associated with caregiving professions was also present within dog rescue work. However, international dog rescue members also experienced additional challenges due to the stigma surrounding international dog rescue operations. Further research on attitudes held by other stakeholders involved in dog import activities, as well as members of Canadian communities needing dog rescue aid may provide meaningful inputs on how to better support and facilitate local and international dog rescue efforts.

Investigation into owner-reported differences between dogs born in versus imported into Canada
von Rentzell · PLOS ONE · 2023

Abstract. Over 1 million dogs are imported into the United States and roughly 340,000 dogs into the United Kingdom yearly. Although the official number of dogs arriving to Canada is currently unknown, local animal professionals estimate that thousands of dogs are imported into Canada each year. Dog importation may be increasing globally while regulation and surveillance are still limited, resulting in concerns for the health and welfare of imported dogs. To date, few studies have investigated how the source location of dogs influences the owner-dog relationship. The current report presents two independent studies that were conducted to assess whether owners of imported dogs reported a poorer owner-dog relationships compared to owners of Canadian-born dogs. In both studies, an online survey was distributed to dog owners (Study 1: n = 803; Study 2: n = 878) in British Columbia, Canada, containing questions on various aspects of the owner-dog relationship. The first study included questions from the Lexington Attachment to Pets Scale, Canine Behavioral Assessment and Research Questionnaire, Human-Animal Bond questionnaire, Monash Dog Owner Relationship Scale, and constructed questions about training methods, expectations, and health. The second study was comprised of original questions assessing difficult behaviour, training practices, health, attachment, and perceived level of burden of owning a dog. Both studies found no evidence of a poorer owner-dog relationship in non-Canadian-sourced dogs. In fact, owners of Canadian-sourced dogs used harsh training methods more frequently and had higher expectations for their dog. While no signs of poorer owner-dog relationship in non-Canadian-sourced dogs were found, future research should continue the investigation of age, health, and backgrounds of incoming dogs.

The Effect of Transportation on Puppy Welfare from Commercial Breeding Kennels to a Distributor
Romaniuk · Animals · 2022

Abstract. Many puppies from commercial breeding kennels (CBKs) are transported by ground from their kennels of origin to a distributor. This experience may elicit fear and stress during a sensitive developmental period, which may in turn negatively impact the puppies’ short- and long-term welfare. This study aimed to measure short-term effects of transportation on puppy welfare metrics. Eight-week-old puppies (n = 383) from 12 CBKs were tested at their kennels (pre-trans) and ~48 h after arriving at a distributor (post-trans). At each location, puppies underwent an isolation test, a stranger-approach test, and a physical health assessment. Behavioral responses to testing were scored from videos. Fecal glucocorticoid metabolites (FGM), fecal secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), and presence of intestinal parasites were also analyzed. Linear mixed-effects models identified decreased exploration (p < 0.001), and increased locomotion (p < 0.001) and escape attempts (p = 0.001) during the post-trans isolation test. Increased affiliative behavior (p < 0.001), FGM (p < 0.001) and sIgA (p = 0.014) were also observed post-trans. Findings support good physical health both pre- and post-trans, while behavioral and physiological changes suggest increased puppy distress post-trans. Higher post-transport affiliative behavior may indicate that puppies sought social support as a coping strategy after experiencing transport-related distress. Future studies should explore the efficacy of transportation-related interventions to mitigate puppy distress.

Incidence and Predictors of Canine Parvovirus Diagnoses in Puppies Relocated for Adoption
DiGangi, Craver, Dolan · Animals · 2021

Abstract. Animal relocation programs seek to balance the animal population and resources between source and destination communities to promote positive outcomes, though little objective evidence has been reported on their physical and behavioral implications. The objective of the current report is to describe the incidence and predictors of canine parvovirus (CPV) diagnoses in 8- to 19-week-old puppies reported by destination shelters participating in a large scale, long-distance, structured animal relocation program. The incidence of post-transport CPV diagnoses in the study population of 4088 puppies was 2.3%. The number of pre-transport vaccinations, length of stay at the source shelter, and time between pre-transport vaccination and transport was not associated with the expected difference in count of post-transport CPV diagnoses (p > 0.05), and was lower in those 13–17 weeks of age (IRR = 0.08, 95% CI = 0.02–0.34, p = 0.001), 18–19 weeks of age (IRR = 0.11, 95% CI = 0.02–0.80, p = 0.029), transferred in to the source shelter (IRR = 0.31, 95% CI = 0.10–0.96, p = 0.043), and transported in the summer season (IRR = 0.07, 95% CI = 0.01–0.53, p = 0.010). When basic biosecurity practices and vaccination protocols were in place, post-transport CPV cases in puppies were few, suggesting that the timing of transport should take into account factors other than the number or timing of pre-transport vaccinations.

U.S. dog importations during the COVID-19 pandemic: Do we have an erupting problem?
Pieracci · PLOS ONE · 2021

Abstract. Dog importation data from 2018–2020 were evaluated to ascertain whether the dog importation patterns in the United States changed during the COVID-19 pandemic, specifically with regard to denial of entry. Dog denial of entry reports from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2020, stored within the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Quarantine Activity Reporting System (QARS), were reviewed. Basic descriptive statistics were used to analyze the data. Reason for denial, country of origin, and month of importation were all examined to determine which countries of origin resulted in the largest number of denials, and whether there was a seasonal change in importations during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), compared to previous years (2018 and 2019). During 2020, CDC denied entry to 458 dogs. This represents a 52% increase in dogs denied entry compared to the averages in 2018 and 2019. Dogs were primarily denied entry for falsified rabies vaccination certificates (56%). Three countries exported 74% of all dogs denied entry into the United States, suggesting that targeted interventions may be needed for certain countries. Increased attempts to import inadequately vaccinated dogs from countries with canine rabies in 2020 may have been due to the increased demand for domestic pets during the COVID-19 pandemic. Educational messaging should highlight the risk of rabies and the importance of making informed pet purchases from foreign entities to protect pet owners, their families, and the public.
