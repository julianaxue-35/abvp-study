Overview: Adoption and Placement

The conversational adoption philosophy notes

Over the past two decades, animal sheltering has shifted toward removing barriers to adoption. The contemporary model, known as conversational adoptions, is built on two foundational assumptions:

  • Every adopter is an individual with personal strengths and weaknesses.
  • Adopters inherently mean well and are capable of gathering knowledge for appropriate animal care.

Role of the adoption counsellor

  • Create a partnership with the adopter, not a gatekeeping relationship.
  • Focus on aligning the adopter's expectations with the characteristics of the specific animal.
  • Even low-barrier adoption processes remain vulnerable to unconscious bias: a survey of 484 organisations running off-site adoption events found that 39.4% of respondents described relying on "vibes" — intuition or gut feeling about a prospective adopter — as part of their selection method, a documented pathway for implicit bias to creep in even when no formal screening barriers (home visits, income checks) exist (Ly, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · returns are not failures A return should be viewed as a learning opportunity about that specific animal, making the next placement match easier. A returned animal can be adopted to the same person as a better match, or that person can become a foster carer or shelter advocate. Framing returns as screening failures or blacklisting returners is inconsistent with conversational adoption philosophy.

Adoption fees and income requirements notes shelter research

Historically, high adoption fees and income requirements were justified by the flawed assumption that high family income was necessary to guarantee a good quality of life for the adopted animal. Research refutes this:

  • No differences in owner-pet attachment were found in adult cat adopters when adoption fees were reduced.
  • No change in return rates when adoption fees were reduced.
  • A low advertised price was an important predictor of a visitor choosing to come to a specific shelter (visitor survey data).
Exam trap · income thresholds are not evidence-based Imposing income requirements or premium fees as a welfare safeguard is not supported by research. Lowering fees does not degrade attachment or increase returns. These barriers simply reduce access without improving animal welfare outcomes.

Visitor dynamics and geographic catchment notes

  • Most adopters search within a 30-mile (48.3 km) radius. Some are willing to travel up to 90 miles (144.8 km), but this is the exception.
  • Shelters should understand local community demographics to tailor programs and ensure no sectors are neglected.
  • Track visitor geography by collecting zip codes at the door.

The 2/3 rule: visitors who do not adopt

  • As many as 2/3 of visitors have no intention of taking an animal home that day.
  • These visitors still matter: a positive experience can convert them into future adopters, foster carers, or volunteers.
  • Deploy trained volunteers to proactively engage with "just looking" visitors.

Spontaneous adoption

Studies show no difference in long-term retention between spontaneous (unplanned) adoptions and planned adoptions. Spontaneous adopters are not higher-risk; do not discourage or slow them down.

Faster decisions were linked to higher satisfaction, not lower, in a survey of 933 adopters representing 1,537 dog-owner pairs: adopters who took less than a week to decide on an adoption, or who were primarily motivated by companionship and affection, reported higher eventual satisfaction than adopters who deliberated longer. Prioritising personality over physical appearance as a selection criterion was also linked to greater satisfaction (Dinwoodie, Animals, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Exam trap · spontaneous vs. planned adoptions Spontaneous adoption retention rates are statistically equivalent to planned adoption retention rates. Restricting or discouraging spontaneous adoptions is not evidence-based.

Decision Making at the Shelter

Adoption decisions are driven by a sequential process: initial browsing is morphology-driven, the meet-and-greet phase is behaviour-driven. Understanding both phases separately is high-yield for the exam.

Canine adoption decision sequence notes shelter research

Canine morphology preferences (browsing phase)

  • Small body size
  • Puppies
  • Certain breed types
  • Long coat length
  • Lighter coat colour (tentative evidence)
Exam trap · in-kennel training does not drive canine adoption Studies confirm that in-kennel training has no effect on adopter browsing behaviour. Only the morphology of the dog while in the kennel predicted visitor stopping behaviour. Training resources should be invested in meet-and-greet skills (lying down, calm proximity), not in-kennel behaviours.

Meet-and-greet: the experimental evidence

  • An experimental study found that when dogs were encouraged to stay in proximity and lie down next to the adopter, and only the dog's favourite toy was present in the meet-and-greet room, adoptions nearly doubled.
  • Training goal: teach dogs to lie down next to a bench or chair (where adopters will subsequently sit). Teach relaxation, not play performance.
  • Excessive play and attention before the meet-and-greet may work against adoption by over-arousing the dog.
  • Behaviour is the dominant decision factor overall, not just at the meet-and-greet: a survey of 517 potential adopters across eight Mid-Atlantic shelters found dog behaviour was the single most-cited decision factor (58.9% of respondents, significantly above chance), ahead of age, size, and lifestyle fit, consistent across adopter age, household type, and prior pet-ownership experience (Minnis, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Pair-housing benefits shelter dogs' welfare and adoption speed: compatible pairs of shelter dogs showed significantly fewer stress indicators (lip licking, whining, ears back) and greater reductions in urinary cortisol:creatinine ratio than singly-housed dogs, and were adopted approximately 4 days faster (Hecker, PLoS ONE, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Feline adoption: environmental and labelling factors notes shelter research

Housing environment effects

  • Frequency of viewing the same cat (not duration) predicted ultimate adoption.
  • Top-tier cage placement + toy = more adopter interest.
  • Active cats with lower stress and human-directed social behaviour had shorter length of stay (LOS).
  • Singly housed cats in traditional horizontal cages had the fewest adoptions and most stress behaviour.
  • Re-orienting a cage from horizontal to vertical and adding a shelf and towel (hiding area): directly reduced stress and improved adoption outcomes.
  • Multi-cat adoption requirements lengthen LOS: cats adopted as part of a multi-cat outcome spent 3 days (42%) longer on the adoption floor than cats adopted individually; this rose to 13 days (185%) longer when the cat was flagged as requiring a co-adoption. Paired cats did show more affiliative behaviour with each other, but the shelter's own multi-outcome classifications often did not match the pairing that behavioural observation would have supported (Engebrecht, Animal Welfare, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Adopter preferences

  • Kittens preferred over adults.
  • Exotic breed status preferred.
  • Unique coat patterns preferred.
  • Lighter coat colours preferred.
  • Black cats: longer time to adoption; judged as less friendly, less playful, and more aggressive than light-coloured cats (without behavioural evidence). This is a documented cognitive bias. A parallel "Black Dog Bias" is described for dogs, who are likewise reported to face discrimination in adoption centres and higher euthanasia rates; interestingly, one courtroom-based study found this bias could reverse into a positive "stereotype violation" effect when a black dog was placed in an unexpectedly sympathetic role (Kaluk, Anthrozoos, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Subjective, not objective, cuteness drives LOS: in an Irish shelter study, participant-rated ("subjective") cuteness was the only variable that influenced cats' length of stay — objectively measured facial features (a proxy for infant-like "baby schema") were not as influential as hypothesised (Jack, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · the owner-surrender vs. stray label bias Cats labelled as "owner-surrendered" are adopted significantly faster than cats labelled as "stray," even when both groups have identical latency to approach people and the only difference is the label. This is a cognitive labelling bias documented in peer-reviewed research. It is not explained by actual behavioural differences. This is one of the most exam-tested concepts in this subdomain.

Socialization status and placement pathway (feline)

  • Cats identified as undersocialized using the Feline Spectrum Assessment (FSA) were more fearful, less affectionate, and less amenable to approach and petting (by strangers and by their own adopters) than control cats, and their adopters reported slightly lower satisfaction; the authors suggest the least-socialized (FSA-1 and FSA-2) cats should be directed toward TNR or barn placement unless meaningful behavioural progress is made in shelter (Ellis, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
  • More socialization exposure is not always better: in a study of foster kittens, high exposure to cognitive-enrichment non-social stimuli (toys, novel surfaces) was associated with greater odds of fear toward people, and kittens whose foster parent scored high on emotional stability also had greater odds of fear toward people — while high social exposure (to people specifically) did reduce fear as expected. Kittens already fearful at foster intake had significantly greater odds of remaining fearful just before adoption (Graham, Applied Animal Behaviour Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Choice availability: limiting vs. variety

  • Some research recommends limiting choices; other studies show adopters prefer variety and may avoid shelters they perceive as having a limited selection.
  • Allowing the public to view all animals, even those not yet ready for adoption, is likely to increase public trust and establish the public as a partner in the shelter's mission.
  • Kennel viewing measurably increases adoptions, especially for long-stay dogs: when one shelter restored visitor access to dog kennels after a pandemic-era appointment-only period, adoptions during viewing sessions rose 40% to 57% above the two prior years' baselines (P = 0.009 and P = 0.0009) for dogs with a length of stay of 9 days or more (Social Compassion & SCIL, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2025 — see journal abstracts below). An earlier pilot at the same shelter that restored viewing for large dogs specifically found an 82% increase in adoptions during viewing periods versus only a 4% increase during appointment-only periods, relative to the prior year (Mavrovouniotis, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Evidence-Based Marketing

Every element of an animal's public-facing profile, from photo composition to word choice, has been studied for its effect on length of stay (LOS) and adoption interest. The evidence is specific and high-yield.

Online profile best practices notes shelter research

  • Photo: outdoor setting, animal standing, making direct eye contact. For cats, include a toy in the photo.
  • Video: participants who viewed videos of cats rated them as significantly more adoptable than those who viewed photos alone. Video is preferred for feline profiles.
  • Breed labels: removing breed labels from dog profiles did not create adverse consequences. One NYC shelter study found LOS was reduced for all dogs after breed label removal, with no change in return rates. A controlled photo-rating study confirms the mechanism: adding a breed label to a shelter dog's photo reduced perceived adoptability while adding a personality description increased it; specific breed names (e.g. Chihuahua mix, Chow mix, Jack Russell Terrier mix) hurt ratings while "Lab mix" helped, and descriptors like affectionate, calm, and friendly boosted adoptability while "energetic" reduced it (Archer, Animal Welfare, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • No warnings in the public profile: matching concerns, restrictions, and behavioural caveats should be raised during the individual counselling phase, never in the initial marketing profile.
  • Do not blame the previous owner in the animal's profile text.
  • Photo background itself may matter less than assumed: a controlled study varying online photo backgrounds (outdoor, home indoor, in-kennel, plain) found no significant effect of background type on either viewers' click-through behaviour or their perception of a dog's sociability — differences between individual dogs drove engagement far more than the backdrop (Lamb, PLOS ONE, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
  • On social media specifically, an analysis of shelter Facebook posts found that animal type (dog vs. cat) and setting (home vs. other) significantly influenced likes and shares, and engagement patterns shifted from more likes (pre-pandemic) to more shares (pandemic-era) (Morrison, Animals, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · matching restrictions belong in counselling, not marketing The online animal profile is a marketing tool. Behavioural concerns or potential matching restrictions placed in the initial profile reduce adoption interest and increase LOS. These must be reserved for individual counselling conversations with serious prospective adopters.

Language style

Adoption advertisements that use concrete and analytic language, rather than narrative or story-based language, result in higher interest from potential adopters. Avoid emotional vignettes; focus on clear, action-oriented descriptors.

The mere exposure effect

Repeatedly posting the same animal's photo on social media, even without interaction, makes viewers perceive that face as more attractive over time (mere exposure effect). Frequent and consistent social media posting is evidence-based, not just good practice.

Training to improve adoption metrics notes

Dogs

  • Focus training on meet-and-greet skills, not in-kennel performance.
  • Teach the dog to lie down next to a bench or chair (where an adopter will sit).
  • Teach calm relaxation in the presence of a person, rather than high-energy play.
  • Avoid over-stimulating the dog with excessive play and attention prior to the meet-and-greet.
  • Brief outings and temporary fostering are high-yield interventions: across 51 shelters, dogs given a brief outing or a short-term foster stay were roughly 5 times and 14 times more likely to be adopted, respectively, than matched control dogs, with post-intervention shelter stays averaging just 10 days before adoption (Gunter, Animals, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Cats

  • Clicker training with food treats to approach the front of the kennel.
  • Increases time spent at the front of the cage.
  • Increases exploratory behaviour; decreases inactivity.
  • Cats who approached an unfamiliar experimenter had a shorter time to adoption.
  • Adopters consistently reported preferring cats who were friendly toward them.

Matching programs: benefits and trade-offs notes

Matching programs conduct counsellor-led conversations before potential adopters browse any animals, showing only dogs assessed as a good match for that individual.

  • Benefit: Better positive placement outcomes and higher adopter-animal compatibility.
  • Trade-off: Results in a longer average length of stay (aLOS) for the animals.
  • A survey of 370 US shelters and rescues found that programs, not resources, predict outcomes: organisations that avoided breed designations for mixed-breed dogs, maintained active foster networks, and ran matching systems had higher live-release rates and lower return rates for dogs, independent of the shelter's financial or staffing resources (Reese, Animal Welfare, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · matching programs and aLOS Matching programs produce better outcomes but increase, not decrease, average LOS. An exam option stating matching programs "compress" LOS is incorrect.

Post-Adoption: Returns, Counselling, and Support

Behavioural problems are the leading cause of non-retention. The timing of returns is predictable, and targeted counselling at the time of adoption measurably reduces them. This section is statistic-dense and high-yield.

Return rates and timing notes shelter research

Overall return statistics

  • Return rate: 7% to 24% across published studies.
  • 20% of all adopters no longer have their pet within 1 year of adoption.
  • Of those lost from homes, only 49% are returned to the shelter. The remainder use alternative rehoming methods.
  • Key reason for not returning to the shelter: fear of being judged as a failure by the organisation. This underscores the importance of shelters positioning themselves as a non-judgmental resource.

Behavioural reasons for return

  • Problematic behaviour is the leading cause of non-retention of adopted animals.
  • 64% of animals were no longer in the same home one year post-adoption due to behaviour problems.
  • 54% to 74% of returned dogs were returned for behavioural reasons.
  • 38% of returned cats were returned due to behavioural reasons.
Key board statistic · first-week returns 41% of returned dogs came back within the first week of adoption. Separate studies report 54% and 39% of returned animals within the first 2 weeks. Approximately 50% to 70% of adopters globally report a behavioural concern within the first week. These statistics establish that the highest-risk period for placement failure is immediately post-adoption, justifying intensive early support.

Aggression and return risk notes shelter research

Aggression toward people is the adopter's primary concern. The provision of management advice at the time of adoption has a dramatic, quantified effect on return risk:

  • Caregivers who received no behavioural advice regarding a dog's human-directed aggression were 11 times more likely to return the dog.
  • Caregivers who received basic management advice regarding the same behaviour were 5.5 times more likely to return (approximately half the risk of no advice).
High-yield statistic: 11x to 5.5x The management advice exactly halves the return risk: from 11 times more likely (no advice) to 5.5 times more likely (with advice). An exam option stating advice "eliminates" return risk is incorrect. The correct figure for "with management advice" is 5.5 times, not 1 times.

Allow adopters to play an active role in learning training skills by interacting with the animal during counselling, rather than receiving passive verbal instruction only.

At-adoption counselling: the 5-minute housetraining study notes

A study showed that adopters who received a targeted 5-minute counselling session on housetraining at the time of adoption reported significantly greater success one month post-adoption than adopters who received no such information. Short, focused counselling is effective.

For separation-related problems (SRP): 30% of adopted dogs showed signs of SRP. Providing an SRP prevention handout reduced prevalence. The two strategies with highest adopter compliance from the handout were:

  • Do not interact with the dog immediately before leaving the house.
  • Provide a food-filled enrichment toy when the dog is left alone.
Key principle · less is more in counselling Compliance drops when counselling covers too many topics. The core principle is to focus on only a few high-priority issues and keep information short and simple. This maximises retention and adopter follow-through.

Post-adoption support programs notes

The primary goal of post-adoption support is to be available to provide assistance to adopters who may be experiencing problems with their new companion. Shelters must do more than simply saying "call us if you need help." Proactively reaching out, even by email, meaningfully reduces silent relinquishment to non-shelter pathways.

  • Animal health concerns
  • Animal behavioural concerns

A qualitative exploration of owner experiences following dog adoption
Moyer, Animal Welfare · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2019.05.001

Abstract. The adjustment period - wherein adopted animals transition to their new home - is a critical time for animal welfare and owner satisfaction, yet literature varies in estimates of how long this period lasts in dogs. This study sought to better characterise the adjustment period in relation to owner experience and canine welfare and clarify its duration as perceived by owners. We used a qualitative approach to examine owner perceptions of duration and their experience of the adjustment period. Twenty-seven interviews were conducted and analysed using thematic content analysis. Six themes were described: adjustment period duration; behavioural indicators during adjustment; behaviours indicative of adjustment; factors influencing adjustment; owner change in routine; and adjustment concerns. Over half of participants perceived the adjustment period to last longer than four months. Behaviours used by owners to assess dog adjustment included: moderation of behavioural extremes (e.g. lethargy and restlessness); play; tail wagging; greeting; and learning their routine. Owners reported that prior experiences and medical needs impacted the adjustment duration, that they adjusted aspects of their lifestyle, and raised concerns about being the right fit for their dog. Future research should incorporate a longer time-frame to better understand how and when dogs adjust to a home, and as there is variation in dog behaviour during adjustment, it must also account for individual differences. As we develop a better understanding of how to characterise this period, adopters can be better prepared for the initial months of dog ownership, and interventions can be individualised to improve owner experience and dog welfare.

Challenges and Benefits of Cat Fostering: A Focus Group Study With Volunteer Cat Fosterers in Aotearoa New Zealand
Roseveare, Anthrozoos · Anthrozoos · 2025 · 10.1080/08927936.2025.2476294

Abstract. Cat fostering programs play a critical role in managing and supporting the health and wellbeing of stray, abandoned, or relinquished cats. Most cat fostering programs can only operate with the help of volunteer cat fosterers. Yet, there is comparatively little research on the experiences of cat fosterers. This study aimed to explore the motivations of volunteer cat fosterers, the challenges they experienced in their fostering practice, and what they perceived as the main benefits of cat fostering. We conducted focus group interviews with cat fosterers in Aotearoa New Zealand to answer our research questions. In total, 13 cat fosterers with a range of fostering experience participated in the focus groups. Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis. The primary motivations to become cat fosterers included a general love for animals, wanting an alternative to cat ownership, and the desire to help and make a difference, which was underpinned by altruistic values. Cat fostering is an emotionally challenging role that requires significant time commitment and involves substantial responsibility, which makes it a form of high-stakes volunteerism. Despite the challenges, however, cat fosterers experienced social and emotional benefits along with the satisfaction of making a meaningful impact on their fosters' lives. These benefits fulfill the basic psychological needs of autonomy, relatedness, and competence. The findings have important implications for shelter organizations and for the recruitment, retention, and training of volunteer fosterers.

Investigating the relationship between canine training classes and post-adoption return rates in North American shelters
Ahmed, Appl Anim Behav Sci · Appl Anim Behav Sci · 2025 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2025.106654

Abstract. Although millions of pets are adopted from shelters each year, a significant portion are returned after adoption which can have negative implications for pets and animal shelters. Previous studies indicate a strong correlation between behavioral issues and returned shelter dogs. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between attending post-adoption training classes and return rates. The study also looked at the distribution of return reasons between dogs who attended training and dogs who did not. Retrospective data between April 1st 2023 and March 31st 2024 was collected from three different shelters in North America (Seattle, WA; DeKalb, IL; Edmonton, AB). Of all the adopted dogs (n = 3325), we looked at which dogs attended training (n = 204) and used propensity score matching to generate a matched control group of dogs who did not attend training. The control group was matched based on age, sex, breed, shelter and intake type. The results showed no significant difference in return rates between dogs who attended training (8.3 %) and dogs who did not attend training (9.3 %) as analyzed by Pearson Chi Squared test ( X 2 (1) = 0.122, p = 0.727). However, of all the returned dogs, those who attended training were more likely to be returned for owner-related reasons (58.8 %), whereas dogs who did not attend training were more likely to be returned for animal-related reasons (78.9 %, X 2 (1) = 5.386, p = 0.020). While attending training classes at animal shelters may help to reduce behavioral returns, as shown by the shift in return reasons, they do not appear to reduce return rates significantly for the general shelter population. This finding provides valuable insight for shelters to improve their resource allocation. Shelters can have a more targeted approach by providing behavioral support for dogs that are at high-risk of return, thus setting those dogs up for a greater likelihood of permanent adoption. • Training classes were not associated with return rates among adopted shelter dogs. • The reasons for returned adoptions differed following training classes. • Attending training classes at animal shelters may help to reduce behavioral returns.

New Strategies of Canine Post-Adoption Support: Methods for a Prospective Longitudinal Cohort Study
Buckland, Animals · Animals · 2025 · 10.1136/vr.150.14.434

Abstract. In 2018, Dogs Trust (UK) launched the Post Adoption Support project, the largest known prospective cohort study investigating behavioural and health problems post-rehoming of dogs. Individuals adopting a dog from any UK Dogs Trust Rehoming Centre are eligible to receive phone calls regarding their dog's health and behaviour at specific timepoints after adoption (2 days, 2 weeks, 4 months). Alongside providing an early and proactive framework to support the wellbeing of the adopter and dog, the project collects rich longitudinal data on dog health and behaviour following adoption to assess the development of issues, the impact of support given, and re-relinquishment risk. This paper details the methodology and study design of the Post Adoption Support project and describes a cohort profile of adoptions over an initial 6-month period. In the cohort, behaviour and health data were collected from 5498 adopters, with call success for those eligible ranging from 72% (4 months) to 89% (2 days). Data from these calls will provide significant insights into canine welfare during the first 4 months post-adoption, which can be used to inform strategies for effective and impactful rehoming and post-adoption support.

No Callback Required: Themes and Factors Associated with New Adopters Declining Free Dog Behavior Support
Samet, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science · 2025 · 10.1080/10888705.2025.2572615

Abstract. This mixed-methods study investigated themes and factors associated with recent dog adopters declining free over-the-phone support for dog behaviors of concern. At least one behavior of concern (potentially aggressive behavior [PAB], separation-related behavior [SRB], or other behavior of concern [OB]) was reported across 6,317 post-adoption support (PAS) calls made to 3,782 adopters at 2-days, 2-weeks, and 4-months post-adoption. However, for 68.8% of calls, the behavior support offered was declined. Generalized linear mixed-effects modeling and Tukey post-hoc testing showed declining support was significantly more likely at 2-weeks post-adoption compared to 2-days and when reporting behaviors of concern in the following combinations: PAB or SRB individually > PAB and SRB simultaneously > only OB > OB with PAB +/SRB. Inductive thematic analysis produced four themes from adopters' responses around declining help: "Awareness but no action yet", "managing behaviour", "feels support not needed", and "belief of cause". Findings offer insights into adopter attitudes around dog behavior, support needs, and behavioral management. Future research should explore whether declining this support is associated with using other support types and whether uptake impacts overall adoption.

Not-So-Prestigious: Social Status Sensitivity Reduces the Adoptability Ratings of Non-Pedigree Cats in an Online Task
Kerman, Anthrozoos · Anthrozoos · 2025 · 10.1080/08927936.2025.2463211

Abstract. The decision to acquire a pet is foundational to the emergence of a psychological bond between humans and their companion animals (HAB). Humans report social comfort and belonging as their primary motives for pet ownership. However, the popularity of “pedigree animals” suggests the presence of other factors, such as animals utilized as status symbols or considered as extensions of social identity. We tested this effect by requesting participants (n = 250) to evaluate the adoptability and cuteness of a series of cat images belonging to pedigree and non-pedigree conditions (30 images/condition, n = 60). Participants also completed two online questionnaires assessing their perceived status attainment (PSA) and aspirations for social status (SA). Accounting for the effects of participant age, sex, and ownership status, we found a differential effect of both social status metrics on adoptability ratings. People scoring high on PSA and SA showed a reluctance to adopt cats presented in the non-pedigree condition – though it did not translate into an increased preference for pedigree cats. Cuteness ratings followed a similar pattern with adoptability, as the perception of cuteness of non-pedigree cats declined with heightened status sensitivity. However, while cuteness remained relatively constant across different PSA scores in the pedigree condition, higher SA slightly increased the reported cuteness ratings of pedigree cats. Our study provides tentative experimental support for the role of social status sensitivity in human–animal interactions, and calls attention to other socio-cognitive mechanisms beyond the paradigm of attachment and belongingness.

Observed Benefits of a Rehome Web Application For Harder-To-Adopt Pet Populations
John Cornelison, Sue Neal and Kevin Horecka · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. Introduction: Owner-to-owner rehoming platforms such as Home-Home.org, Getyourpet. com, and Rehome.adoptapet.com (hereafter, Rehome) were launched in 2016 and 2017 as alternatives to traditional shelter intake. Previous research identified characteristics that predict an animal’s likelihood of successful diversion from shelter intake via person-to-person rehoming platforms. These findings suggested that certain populations – specifically senior animals and large dogs – have lower odds of diversion. This study builds on this by evaluating whether harder-to-adopt animals can benefit from being listed on Rehome. Methods: Using data from Rehome and 23 shelters participating in the Human Animal Support Services (HASS) initiative, this study used Cox regression, chi-square tests, and two outcome metrics: median length of stay (LOS) and adoption rate. Analyses focused on harder-to-adopt animals – defined as senior cats, senior dogs, and large dogs – and compared adoption rates between animals listed on Rehome and those surrendered to shelters. Results: Animals on Rehome had longer LOS than animals in the shelter. Cox regression results further indicated that animals on Rehome had lower probabilities of adoption at any given time compared to those in shelters. Overall, adoption rates were higher in shelters, although considerable variability existed among shelters; whereas Rehome’s performance remained relatively stable. In some regions, Rehome had higher adoption rates than the corresponding local shelter. Conclusion: The results imply that harder-to-adopt animals have a better chance of finding a new home in shelters than through an online rehoming website. However, harder-to-adopt animals still find success on Rehome. Considering that Rehome results in efficiency gains for shelter systems and a reduced-stress environment for animals, if properly expanded and utilized by shelter communities, Rehome could serve as a useful complementary tool for shelters. It may also help reduce shelter intakes, especially of animals traditionally classified as harder-to-adopt.
