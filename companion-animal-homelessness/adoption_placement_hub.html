Overview: Adoption and Placement

The field of shelter adoption has shifted decisively away from high-barrier, income-based screening toward a conversational, partnership-based model. Understanding this philosophy and the research underpinning it is central to the ABVP exam.

The conversational adoption philosophy notes

Over the past two decades, animal sheltering has shifted toward removing barriers to adoption. The contemporary model, known as conversational adoptions, is built on two foundational assumptions:

  • Every adopter is an individual with personal strengths and weaknesses.
  • Adopters inherently mean well and are capable of gathering knowledge for appropriate animal care.

Role of the adoption counsellor

  • Create a partnership with the adopter, not a gatekeeping relationship.
  • Focus on aligning the adopter's expectations with the characteristics of the specific animal.
Exam trap · returns are not failures A return should be viewed as a learning opportunity about that specific animal, making the next placement match easier. A returned animal can be adopted to the same person as a better match, or that person can become a foster carer or shelter advocate. Framing returns as screening failures or blacklisting returners is inconsistent with conversational adoption philosophy.

Adoption fees and income requirements notes shelter research

Historically, high adoption fees and income requirements were justified by the flawed assumption that high family income was necessary to guarantee a good quality of life for the adopted animal. Research refutes this:

  • No differences in owner-pet attachment were found in adult cat adopters when adoption fees were reduced.
  • No change in return rates when adoption fees were reduced.
  • A low advertised price was an important predictor of a visitor choosing to come to a specific shelter (visitor survey data).
Exam trap · income thresholds are not evidence-based Imposing income requirements or premium fees as a welfare safeguard is not supported by research. Lowering fees does not degrade attachment or increase returns. These barriers simply reduce access without improving animal welfare outcomes.

Visitor dynamics and geographic catchment notes

  • Most adopters search within a 30-mile radius. Some are willing to travel up to 90 miles, but this is the exception.
  • Shelters should understand local community demographics to tailor programs and ensure no sectors are neglected.
  • Track visitor geography by collecting zip codes at the door.

The 2/3 rule: visitors who do not adopt

  • As many as 2/3 of visitors have no intention of taking an animal home that day.
  • These visitors still matter: a positive experience can convert them into future adopters, foster carers, or volunteers.
  • Deploy trained volunteers to proactively engage with "just looking" visitors.

Spontaneous adoption

Studies show no difference in long-term retention between spontaneous (unplanned) adoptions and planned adoptions. Spontaneous adopters are not higher-risk; do not discourage or slow them down.

Exam trap · spontaneous vs. planned adoptions Spontaneous adoption retention rates are statistically equivalent to planned adoption retention rates. Restricting or discouraging spontaneous adoptions is not evidence-based.

Decision Making at the Shelter

Adoption decisions are driven by a sequential process: initial browsing is morphology-driven, the meet-and-greet phase is behaviour-driven. Understanding both phases separately is high-yield for the exam.

Canine adoption decision sequence notes shelter research

Canine morphology preferences (browsing phase)

  • Small body size
  • Puppies
  • Certain breed types
  • Long coat length
  • Lighter coat colour (tentative evidence)
Exam trap · in-kennel training does not drive canine adoption Studies confirm that in-kennel training has no effect on adopter browsing behaviour. Only the morphology of the dog while in the kennel predicted visitor stopping behaviour. Training resources should be invested in meet-and-greet skills (lying down, calm proximity), not in-kennel behaviours.

Meet-and-greet: the experimental evidence

  • An experimental study found that when dogs were encouraged to stay in proximity and lie down next to the adopter, and only the dog's favourite toy was present in the meet-and-greet room, adoptions nearly doubled.
  • Training goal: teach dogs to lie down next to a bench or chair (where adopters will subsequently sit). Teach relaxation, not play performance.
  • Excessive play and attention before the meet-and-greet may work against adoption by over-arousing the dog.

Feline adoption: environmental and labelling factors notes shelter research

Housing environment effects

  • Frequency of viewing the same cat (not duration) predicted ultimate adoption.
  • Top-tier cage placement + toy = more adopter interest.
  • Active cats with lower stress and human-directed social behaviour had shorter length of stay (LOS).
  • Singly housed cats in traditional horizontal cages had the fewest adoptions and most stress behaviour.
  • Re-orienting a cage from horizontal to vertical and adding a shelf and towel (hiding area): directly reduced stress and improved adoption outcomes.

Adopter preferences

  • Kittens preferred over adults.
  • Exotic breed status preferred.
  • Unique coat patterns preferred.
  • Lighter coat colours preferred.
  • Black cats: longer time to adoption; judged as less friendly, less playful, and more aggressive than light-coloured cats (without behavioural evidence). This is a documented cognitive bias.
Exam trap · the owner-surrender vs. stray label bias Cats labelled as "owner-surrendered" are adopted significantly faster than cats labelled as "stray," even when both groups have identical latency to approach people and the only difference is the label. This is a cognitive labelling bias documented in peer-reviewed research. It is not explained by actual behavioural differences. This is one of the most exam-tested concepts in this subdomain.

Choice availability: limiting vs. variety

  • Some research recommends limiting choices; other studies show adopters prefer variety and may avoid shelters they perceive as having a limited selection.
  • Allowing the public to view all animals, even those not yet ready for adoption, is likely to increase public trust and establish the public as a partner in the shelter's mission.

Evidence-Based Marketing

Every element of an animal's public-facing profile, from photo composition to word choice, has been studied for its effect on length of stay (LOS) and adoption interest. The evidence is specific and high-yield.

Online profile best practices notes shelter research

  • Photo: outdoor setting, animal standing, making direct eye contact. For cats, include a toy in the photo.
  • Video: participants who viewed videos of cats rated them as significantly more adoptable than those who viewed photos alone. Video is preferred for feline profiles.
  • Breed labels: removing breed labels from dog profiles did not create adverse consequences. One NYC shelter study found LOS was reduced for all dogs after breed label removal, with no change in return rates.
  • No warnings in the public profile: matching concerns, restrictions, and behavioural caveats should be raised during the individual counselling phase, never in the initial marketing profile.
  • Do not blame the previous owner in the animal's profile text.
Exam trap · matching restrictions belong in counselling, not marketing The online animal profile is a marketing tool. Behavioural concerns or potential matching restrictions placed in the initial profile reduce adoption interest and increase LOS. These must be reserved for individual counselling conversations with serious prospective adopters.

Language style

Adoption advertisements that use concrete and analytic language, rather than narrative or story-based language, result in higher interest from potential adopters. Avoid emotional vignettes; focus on clear, action-oriented descriptors.

The mere exposure effect

Repeatedly posting the same animal's photo on social media, even without interaction, makes viewers perceive that face as more attractive over time (mere exposure effect). Frequent and consistent social media posting is evidence-based, not just good practice.

Training to improve adoption metrics notes

Dogs

  • Focus training on meet-and-greet skills, not in-kennel performance.
  • Teach the dog to lie down next to a bench or chair (where an adopter will sit).
  • Teach calm relaxation in the presence of a person, rather than high-energy play.
  • Avoid over-stimulating the dog with excessive play and attention prior to the meet-and-greet.

Cats

  • Clicker training with food treats to approach the front of the kennel.
  • Increases time spent at the front of the cage.
  • Increases exploratory behaviour; decreases inactivity.
  • Cats who approached an unfamiliar experimenter had a shorter time to adoption.
  • Adopters consistently reported preferring cats who were friendly toward them.

Matching programs: benefits and trade-offs notes

Matching programs conduct counsellor-led conversations before potential adopters browse any animals, showing only dogs assessed as a good match for that individual.

  • Benefit: Better positive placement outcomes and higher adopter-animal compatibility.
  • Trade-off: Results in a longer average length of stay (aLOS) for the animals.
Exam trap · matching programs and aLOS Matching programs produce better outcomes but increase, not decrease, average LOS. An exam option stating matching programs "compress" LOS is incorrect.

Post-Adoption: Returns, Counselling, and Support

Behavioural problems are the leading cause of non-retention. The timing of returns is predictable, and targeted counselling at the time of adoption measurably reduces them. This section is statistic-dense and high-yield.

Return rates and timing notes shelter research

Overall return statistics

  • Return rate: 7% to 24% across published studies.
  • 20% of all adopters no longer have their pet within 1 year of adoption.
  • Of those lost from homes, only 49% are returned to the shelter. The remainder use alternative rehoming methods.
  • Key reason for not returning to the shelter: fear of being judged as a failure by the organisation. This underscores the importance of shelters positioning themselves as a non-judgmental resource.

Behavioural reasons for return

  • Problematic behaviour is the leading cause of non-retention of adopted animals.
  • 64% of animals were no longer in the same home one year post-adoption due to behaviour problems.
  • 54% to 74% of returned dogs were returned for behavioural reasons.
  • 38% of returned cats were returned due to behavioural reasons.
Key board statistic · first-week returns 41% of returned dogs came back within the first week of adoption. Separate studies report 54% and 39% of returned animals within the first 2 weeks. Approximately 50% to 70% of adopters globally report a behavioural concern within the first week. These statistics establish that the highest-risk period for placement failure is immediately post-adoption, justifying intensive early support.

Aggression and return risk notes shelter research

Aggression toward people is the adopter's primary concern. The provision of management advice at the time of adoption has a dramatic, quantified effect on return risk:

  • Caregivers who received no behavioural advice regarding a dog's human-directed aggression were 11 times more likely to return the dog.
  • Caregivers who received basic management advice regarding the same behaviour were 5.5 times more likely to return (approximately half the risk of no advice).
High-yield statistic: 11x to 5.5x The management advice exactly halves the return risk: from 11 times more likely (no advice) to 5.5 times more likely (with advice). An exam option stating advice "eliminates" return risk is incorrect. The correct figure for "with management advice" is 5.5 times, not 1 times.

Allow adopters to play an active role in learning training skills by interacting with the animal during counselling, rather than receiving passive verbal instruction only.

At-adoption counselling: the 5-minute housetraining study notes

A study showed that adopters who received a targeted 5-minute counselling session on housetraining at the time of adoption reported significantly greater success one month post-adoption than adopters who received no such information. Short, focused counselling is effective.

For separation-related problems (SRP): 30% of adopted dogs showed signs of SRP. Providing an SRP prevention handout reduced prevalence. The two strategies with highest adopter compliance from the handout were:

  • Do not interact with the dog immediately before leaving the house.
  • Provide a food-filled enrichment toy when the dog is left alone.
Key principle · less is more in counselling Compliance drops when counselling covers too many topics. The core principle is to focus on only a few high-priority issues and keep information short and simple. This maximises retention and adopter follow-through.

Post-adoption support programs notes

The primary goal of post-adoption support is to be available to provide assistance to adopters who may be experiencing problems with their new companion. Shelters must do more than simply saying "call us if you need help." Proactively reaching out, even by email, meaningfully reduces silent relinquishment to non-shelter pathways.

Topics to cover in proactive follow-up

  • Animal adjustment to the new home
  • Animal health concerns
  • Animal behavioural concerns
  • Need for assistance with post-adoption problems
  • Adopter satisfaction with the animal match

Post-Adoption Behavior and Adopter Satisfaction of Shelter Kittens Identified as Undersocialized Prior to Adoption
Post-Adoption Behavior and Adopter Satisfaction of Shelter · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2026

Abstract. Introduction: Undersocialized kittens pose an ethical challenge for shelters. Although undersocialized kittens (especially those under 12 weeks) are thought to adapt better than undersocialized adults, data on their long-term welfare and adopter satisfaction are limited. This study examines behavioral outcomes and adopter experiences for Undersocial and Control kittens, above and below 12 weeks of age. Methods: At least 1 year after placement, a survey was administered to adopters of kittens who were >1 and <7 months old at intake. Multinomial logistic regressions and Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to identify significant differences between groups (Undersocial<12 weeks, Undersocial>12 weeks, Control<12 weeks, and Control>12 weeks) in reported behavioral traits (8 traits, 1–5 Likert scale), responses to situational social interactions (approach and petting, from owner and stranger), and adopter experience (satisfaction 1–5 Likert scale, categorical classification of feelings toward the cats, and categorical classification of what type of environment would make the cat happiest). Results: Of the 724 adopters surveyed, overall differences between groups were minimal. Fearfulness was the only trait significantly associated with group: Control<12 weeks kittens were 71% less likely and Control>12 weeks kittens were 65% less likely to be rated as fearful compared to Undersocial<12 weeks kittens. Responses to stranger approach and petting also differed: Undersocial<12 weeks kittens were more likely to react negatively than Control<12 weeks kittens. Owner-directed behaviors (approach and petting) showed no meaningful differences between groups. Adopter satisfaction was high across all groups (95–98%), most adopters described loving their kittens (96–99%) and thought their kitten would be happiest in a traditional home environment (89–93%). Conclusion: Both age groups of Undersocial kittens were more fearful and wary of strangers, but all groups formed strong bonds with adopters. There was no notable difference between Undersocial kitten age groups, suggesting adoption is a viable, welfare-positive option when intake sources and resources allow. with More Than 22,000 Dog and Cat Adopters · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2026

Abstract. Introduction: Understanding the early post-adoption experience is critical to supporting animal welfare, strengthening the human–animal bond, and improving shelter practices. While most adopters report high satisfaction, behavioural and care-related challenges are common. This study used the Petszel platform to evaluate early post-adoption outcomes across more than 22,000 dog and cat adopters from 112 shelters and rescue organisations in 40 United States (U.S.) states. Methods: Surveys were distributed automatically on Day 1, Week 1, and Month 1 post-adoption. Adopters were asked about general satisfaction, specific behaviours, veterinary care, and microchip registration. Responses were included only if the pet was in the home at time of survey. Data were summarised by species and time point using descriptive statistics. Results: Over 94% rated their experience positively (score of 4 or 5 out of 5) at all time points. However, more than 78% of dog adopters and 50% of cat adopters also reported at least one challenge. Nearly half of dog adopters reported no plans for formal training by Month 1. Conclusion: Despite high satisfaction ratings, many adopters faced behavioural or care challenges. These findings suggest that general satisfaction measures may obscure important challenges as animals settle into their new homes. John Cornelison, Sue Neal and Kevin Horecka · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. Introduction: Owner-to-owner rehoming platforms such as Home-Home.org, Getyourpet. com, and Rehome.adoptapet.com (hereafter, Rehome) were launched in 2016 and 2017 as alternatives to traditional shelter intake. Previous research identified characteristics that predict an animal’s likelihood of successful diversion from shelter intake via person-to-person rehoming platforms. These findings suggested that certain populations – specifically senior animals and large dogs – have lower odds of diversion. This study builds on this by evaluating whether harder-to-adopt animals can benefit from being listed on Rehome. Methods: Using data from Rehome and 23 shelters participating in the Human Animal Support Services (HASS) initiative, this study used Cox regression, chi-square tests, and two outcome metrics: median length of stay (LOS) and adoption rate. Analyses focused on harder-to-adopt animals – defined as senior cats, senior dogs, and large dogs – and compared adoption rates between animals listed on Rehome and those surrendered to shelters. Results: Animals on Rehome had longer LOS than animals in the shelter. Cox regression results further indicated that animals on Rehome had lower probabilities of adoption at any given time compared to those in shelters. Overall, adoption rates were higher in shelters, although considerable variability existed among shelters; whereas Rehome’s performance remained relatively stable. In some regions, Rehome had higher adoption rates than the corresponding local shelter. Conclusion: The results imply that harder-to-adopt animals have a better chance of finding a new home in shelters than through an online rehoming website. However, harder-to-adopt animals still find success on Rehome. Considering that Rehome results in efficiency gains for shelter systems and a reduced-stress environment for animals, if properly expanded and utilized by shelter communities, Rehome could serve as a useful complementary tool for shelters. It may also help reduce shelter intakes, especially of animals traditionally classified as harder-to-adopt. Optimising Shelter Outcomes Using Technology, Foster Care and External Networks A Community Case Study of Community- Centred Adoptions in an Australian Cat Shelter
Optimising Shelter Outcomes Using Technology, Foster Care · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. This study presents an initial in-depth analysis of foster-led adoptions via a digital platform in an Australian shelter context, where adoptions have traditionally occurred on-site. COVID-19 prompted the development of new adoption initiatives that complied with restrictive government guidelines. Between 2018 and 2024, shelter-based adoptions (n = 30,292) reduced from 80 to 60%, with adoptions through foster care, pet shops and community engagement becoming increasingly important. Growth in foster-based and pet shop adoptions supported shelter operations, accelerating the introduction of new services. Incorporating Adopets®, a digital platform compatible with Shelter BuddyPro® and relatively new to Australia, streamlined remote adoption processes, eliminated the need for in-person visits and reduced adoption barriers, notably decision fatigue. Following its implementation, off-site adoptions doubled compared to pre-pandemic levels, peaking at 40% (n = 13,713), with foster care consistently outperforming pet shop placements. In 2023, the median on-site stay for cats and kittens was 6 days and 28 days for those in foster care. In 2024, despite a 467-animal increase in intake, the median shelter stay dropped to 5 days and 24 days for those in foster care. These results highlight how incorporating technology-enabled strategies offer a replicable framework for community-centred, scalable shelter operations in Australian shelters.

The Effect of Kennel Viewing on the Adoption of Slow-Track Dogs
Social Compassion & SCIL, Laguna Beach, CA, USA · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2025

Abstract. The Orange County (California) animal shelter allowed adopters to peruse dog kennels until early 2020 but suspended visitor access to dog kennels during the COVID-19 pandemic. Except for a late 2023 pilot program, the restrictions remained in place through 2023. Then, in January 2024, partial daily viewing sessions were instituted. We study the effect of this change on adoptions of dogs with length of stay (LOS) ≥ 9, who account for 42% of adoptions but 72% of inventory excluding dogs returned to owner. Adoption counts in 2024 are 40% higher than expected in viewing sessions using 2023 as a reference, or 57% higher using 2022 as a reference, and these results are statistically significant (P = 0.009, P = 0.0009, respectively). Adoption rates are computed as a percent of inventory (equivalently, adoption probabilities per animal per session). In the period 2019–2024 but excluding 2020, adoption rates were 1.04% – 1.18% per session (with an average of 1.10%) in viewing sessions versus. 0.68% – 0.80% (with an average of 0.74%) in non-viewing sessions. The evidence suggests that kennel viewing enhances opportunities for increasing adoptions of dogs with LOS ≥ 9.

The role of breed and personality descriptions in influencing perceptions of shelter dog adoptability
Archer C, Hall NJ, Andrukonis A · Animal Welfare · 2025 · 10.1017/awf.2025.10043

Abstract. The study examined how breed labels and personality descriptions affect potential adopters' likelihood of choosing shelter dogs. Researchers presented dog photographs to participants under four conditions: photo alone, photo with breed identification, photo with personality traits, and photo with both elements combined. Key findings indicate that descriptions significantly increased perceived adoptability, while breed labels decreased it. Specific breed designations like Chihuahua mix, Chow mix, and Jack Russell Terrier mix negatively influenced adoption interest, whereas 'Lab mix' improved ratings. Personality descriptors such as affectionate, calm, and friendly boosted perceived adoptability; notably, 'energetic' reduced adoption interest. The research suggests removing breed labels and incorporating positive personality descriptions could enhance adoption outcomes and potentially reduce how long dogs remain in shelters, thereby supporting animal welfare objectives.

A Mixed Methods Exploration Of Opportunities For Barriers And Bias During Off Site Animal Adoption Events
Lexis H. Ly, Kelsea Brown, Emily Yau, Becky Kenworthy, Sheila Segurson · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2024

Abstract. Introduction: Adopters and animal shelter leaders are calling for higher equity in services provided by animal shelters to their communities. Specifically, restrictive pet adoption processes have been proposed as a threat to equitable service. The study had three specific goals: (1) to describe the basic characteristics of off-site adoption events, (2) to describe the adoption processes in use by shelters and to determine if there is any potential for unconscious bias in these processes, and (3) to determine if adoption practices differ depending on the characteristics of the animal shelter. Methods: A total of 484 participants reported on how they conduct adoption events and their adopter selection procedures. Characteristics of organisations and their adoption event procedures were described and compared across organisation size using Chi-squared tests. Qualitative responses to open-ended questions asking about screening procedures were analysed through inductive thematic analysis. Results: Commonly, adoption events were held weekly (n = 158, 33.1%) in locally owned stores (n = 259, 54.4%), with volunteers engaging directly with potential adopters (n = 423, 88.5%). Just under half responded that adopters could take pets straight home from the event (n = 229, 47.8%) and that the adoption process does not require a home visit (n = 276, 57.7%). Qualitative analysis revealed three major groups of themes: ‘methods of selection’, ‘information gathered by the animal shelter’, ‘information provided by the animal shelter’. The sub-theme of ‘Vibes’ (coded for 39.4% participants, n = 175) encompassed decision-making based on intuition or feeling when meeting or observing potential adopters face-to-face. Generally, larger organisations held adoption events more frequently, were more likely to let adopters take animals straight home, and did not require a home visit. However, both types of organisations showed a potential for implicit bias when selecting adopters. Conclusion: Our results suggest that procedures of adoption events in animal shelters vary across organisation, with some practices differing based on organisation size. Given that our results suggest possibility for implicit bias when screening adopters during adoption events, we conclude that there is a need for increased awareness, research, and training to address the issue of implicit bias in animal sheltering organisations. Abstract. The researchers investigated whether housing shelter dogs in compatible pairs versus alone affects their wellbeing. The Increasing Adoption Potential of Undersocialized Cats and Kittens- Blessing or Curse?
The Increasing Adoption Potential of Undersocialized Cats and Kittens:... https://jsmcah.org/index.php/jasv/article/view/94 · Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2024 · 10.56771/jsmcah.v3.94

Abstract. The most appropriate management strategy for a cat entering a shelter environment varies based on many factors. Perhaps the most important of these is the cat's level of socialization to people. For unsocialized/poorly socialized cats, pathways such as TNR or alternative placement are typically considered more appropriate for their quality of life. This is particularly true for older cats, outside of the socialization phase. However, as supply of cats have declined and rates of pet ownership have increased, there is increasing pressure for shelters to find homes for cats with socialization histories that would not have been deemed adoptable previously, and adopters are more willing to adopt poorly socialized cats. There has been no investigation of the well-being of poorly socialized cats in their adoptive homes or the experience of these adopters. Adopters of 57 adult cats identified as undersocialized and 155 control cats (not suspected of being undersocialized) were surveyed about their cat’s behaviour and their own satisfaction. Control cats were rated as less fearful, more affectionate, more likely to engage when approached (by either a stranger or their adopter), and more likely to enjoy petting (by either a stranger or their adopter) than undesocialized cats. Owners of control cats also reported greater satisfaction and were more likely to state that their cat would be happier in a home environment than regularly fed outdoors. Adopters of 134 kittens identified as undersocialized and 591 control kittens (not suspected of being undersocialized) were surveyed about their kitten’s behaviour and their own satisfaction. In this survey, control kittens were rated as less fearful, and more likely to engage when approached by a stranger or enjoy a stranger petting them, but there was no difference in ratings of affection, relationship with their owner (either by engaging when approached or enjoyment of petting), adopter satisfaction, or what environment the adopter states the kitten would be happier. Results suggest that although it is becoming more possible to place undersocialized cats in homes, these environments may not be in the best interests of the cats’ or adopters’ quality-of-life. Results for undersocialized kittens suggest that while they may still be prone to fear, particularly towards strangers, both the kitten and the owners have good quality-of-life and satisfaction post-adoption. Too much too soon? Risk factors for fear behaviour in foster kittens prior to adoption
Graham C, Pearl DL, Niel L · Applied Animal Behaviour Science · 2024 · 10.1016/j.applanim.2023.106141

Abstract. Many companion kittens spend their sensitive period for socialization (~2–9 weeks of age) in foster care, and the quality of these early life experiences can impact behavioural development. This study aimed to improve early kitten care by using an online survey to investigate risk factors for fear behaviour in foster kittens prior to adoption (7–9 weeks of age) based on early management practices, foster parent personality traits, and evaluations of kitten behaviour. The main behavioural outcomes of this study were whether kittens showed caregiver-reported fear behaviour during interactions with unfamiliar people and unfamiliar objects just prior to adoption. Fear behaviour included displays of avoidance, freezing, crouching, ears back, piloerection, and aggression. We analyzed the impacts of kitten (n=235) and foster parent (n=72) characteristics, exposure to social stimuli (i.e., general handling, mimicking handling during a vet visit, exposure to people), and exposure to non-social stimuli (e.g., interactive toys, scratching material, different surfaces, etc.) provided. Using mixed logistic regression models, with foster parent as a random intercept to account for clustering at the foster home level, we found that kittens who were reported fearful at intake into foster care had significantly greater odds of displaying fear behaviours towards unfamiliar people and unfamiliar objects than kittens who were not reported fearful at intake. Additionally, kittens who received exposure to cognitive enrichment-based non-social stimuli had greater odds of displaying fear towards people compared to kittens who did not receive cognitive exposure. Kittens whose foster parent had a high score for emotional stability had greater odds of displaying fear towards people compared to those who had a low score for emotional stability. Further, kittens who received high exposure to social stimuli had reduced odds of displaying fear towards people than kittens who received low social exposure, as predicted. Some of these findings challenge recommendations for high amounts of exposure to social and non-social stimuli during early socialization. High social exposure was ineffective at reducing fear levels towards unfamiliar objects, and high cognitive enrichment-based non-social exposure appears to have exceeded kitten limits for optimal behavioural development. While appropriate socialization is beneficial for development, the current study highlights the need for further research to understand the impact of different socialization practices on kitten behavioural development, and the importance of accurately identifying when kittens are fearful and adapting socialization practices appropriately.

Abstract. Background: The natural history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) in cats has been mainly studied in cats referred for suspected heart disease.
