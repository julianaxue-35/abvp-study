Demographic patterns in pet ownership notes AVCC 2018

Household type

  • 46.5% of households owned dogs only; 24% owned cats only; 29.6% had a blended family (dogs and cats).
  • Condo and apartment dwellers: more likely to have fewer pets than other residence types.
  • Higher-income households: more likely to own dogs only.
  • Males: more likely to have only dogs. Younger respondents: more likely to have blended families.

Age trends

  • Older respondents: more likely to own only one pet; more likely to own cats; more likely to have acquired from friend/family or taken in a stray cat.
  • Younger respondents: more likely to have a blended family; more likely to have received their pet as a gift; less likely to have spayed/neutered their pets (if done, more likely at a free or reduced cost clinic).

Acquisition and spay/neuter

  • Approximately 1/4 of dogs and cats were reportedly adopted from shelters.
  • More dogs came from breeders; cats were more often strays or shelter-adopted.
  • Dogs were more likely than cats to remain intact (not spayed or neutered).

Human-animal bond in context notes AVCC 2018

Two theoretical frameworks underpin the human-animal bond (HAB) and its relevance to access to care:

  • Attachment theory: originally developed studying mother-infant relationships; now extended to human-animal bonds. Recognises four attachment styles. The strength and style of attachment shapes how an owner responds to barriers in providing care.
  • Social support theory: social support acts to buffer the health impacts of stress, either through the perception that support is available or through tangible/intangible supportive actions. Companion animals function as social support for their owners.

  • 80.2% = strongly agreed "my pets are members of the family" in the AVCC 2018 national study.
  • 95% = considered their animals family members in a separate 2015 survey also referenced in the notes.
An exam question citing the AVCC/CARE study expects 80.2%. An exam question citing the 2015 survey expects 95%. Read the source carefully.

Additionally: 59% of dog owners and 56% of cat owners viewed their animal as a child or family member (2015 data). Social support has been shown to be a protective factor for overall physical and mental health.

Homelessness definition

A person who lacks a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence. Includes individuals living on streets, in cars or abandoned buildings, or in temporary shelter (staying with family/friends, in a homeless shelter, or in a hotel/motel).

Factors associated with homelessness

  • Living below the poverty level; insufficient resources for low-income families
  • Substance abuse; mental illness; family violence
  • In the US: 17.6% of families with children under 18 years fall below the poverty level; 19.5% of those with children under 6 years
  • Families with children: approximately 33% of the homeless population
  • Unaccompanied youth (under 25 years, without parents/guardian): 7% of the total homeless population

Homeless individuals and pets

  • Difficulty affording veterinary care.
  • Refuse services (shelters, transitional housing) when accommodation policies do not allow animals.
  • Some homeless individuals report putting the animal's needs above their own.
  • Studies show attachments to animals in homeless individuals are stronger than in the general population.

Preventative care barriers in detail notes AVCC 2018

  • 1/4 of all respondents had been unable to obtain preventative care for at least one pet in the recent past.

Sick care: the middle-income convergence notes AVCC 2018

A critical and exam-tested finding: pet owners in the middle-income group (138% to 250% of the poverty level) were as likely to cite financial inability as a barrier to sick care as those in the lowest income category. This economic convergence is not seen for preventative care, where barriers decrease clearly with income.

  • Almost half of those who faced a preventative care barrier also experienced a sick care barrier.

National shelter intake and euthanasia trends Shelter Animals Count / ASPCA

Intake volume

  • Approximately 6.5 million companion animals enter US shelters each year: roughly 3.3 million dogs and 3.2 million cats (ASPCA current estimate; Shelter Animals Count).
  • Not all intake is owner surrender — impoundment, stray intake, and transfer arrivals are combined in national totals.

Euthanasia decline

  • Euthanasia has declined sharply: from approximately 2.7 million animals per year (early 2010s) to approximately 920,000 per year (2022) — a roughly 66% reduction.
  • This decline reflects increased adoption rates, transfer/transport programs, targeted spay-neuter access, and shelter diversion initiatives — all directly tested program interventions.

Species gap and regional variation

  • Cats face higher proportional euthanasia rates than dogs: cats have much lower return-to-owner (RTO) rates (~5–10% vs. ~30% for dogs), lower adoption visibility in shelter settings, and higher unowned/free-roaming population pressure.
  • Regional variation is significant: southern and rural states have substantially higher intake and euthanasia rates than northeastern or urban markets. Transport programs move animals from high-intake southern regions to adoption-demand-surplus northern/western regions.
  • An estimated 30 to 80 million free-roaming cats live in the US — a range reflecting the difficulty of enumeration and the proportion of unowned community cats relative to owned outdoor cats.
Exam trap · euthanasia decline ≠ problem solved The dramatic euthanasia decline is real, but intake volume (6.5M/year) has not declined proportionally. The remaining euthanasias are increasingly concentrated in cats, in southern/rural shelters, and in animals with medical or behavioural complexity. Framing the problem as "solved" is incorrect on the exam.

Spectrum of Care: definition, framework, and exam context VCE 2024 AVCC 2018

  • Definition: the Spectrum of Care is a framework acknowledging that veterinary care exists on a continuum — a range of evidence-based diagnostic and treatment options rather than a single gold-standard protocol. It explicitly rejects the false binary of "gold-standard care or nothing."
  • Key principle: Spectrum of Care is not substandard care. Offering contextualised, client-accessible options is ethically sound when guided by the animal's welfare needs, evidence, and the client's circumstances — it is not a lowering of professional standards.
  • Origin: formalised in the Principles of Veterinary Community Engagement (VCE Principles 2024), which position it as the professional framework for community and safety-net practice; the AVCC 2018 CARE data provided the empirical basis.
  • Professional acceptance: 84.8% of veterinary service providers in the CARE survey were comfortable offering alternatives to the standard of care when severe financial constraints existed — evidence that the spectrum approach is already widely practised.
  • Application: in community veterinary programs, the Spectrum of Care guides decisions such as offering core vaccination only (rather than a full annual wellness panel), choosing non-surgical pain management when surgery is not accessible, or providing step-wise diagnostics rather than full workup at first visit.
Exam context · how Spectrum of Care appears on the board ABVP exam questions in this area test whether candidates understand that offering a lower-cost option is not an ethical breach — the breach would be offering no option or abandoning the patient. Questions may ask about the difference between 79.7% (feel obligated to offer same standard) and 84.8% (comfortable offering alternatives). The correct interpretation: more vets are willing to offer alternatives than feel bound to the single standard.

The "Champion" concept notes

Successful programs require a "Champion": a person or group who:

  • Recognises the need for a program or service.
  • Has the passion and connections to convince others of its importance.
  • Has the stamina to stay committed for the long term.

Programs start with a dialogue in the community, often beginning with a phone call: "What can you do? What are your boundaries?" It requires like-minded practitioners who are seeing the same problem and are willing to initiate collaborative action.

Building community trust notes VCE 2024

  • Trust is built through relationships and sensitivity to the culture of the community being served.
  • Successful programs must meet owners where they are and foster relationships that eventually lead to trust.
  • Involve community members directly: they can go back to neighbours and families, reaching people who actually trust them.
  • Most veterinarians and veterinary students have not experienced poverty. Sensitivity training to build cultural competencies is recommended.
VCE Principles 2024 alignment The trust-building approach described in the CARE focus groups directly mirrors VCE Principles 1 and 2: know your community (its culture, history, social networks, and power structures) and build trust with formal and informal leadership. The concept of "meeting owners where they are" reflects Principle 2's asset-based approach, working with community strengths rather than imposing external solutions.

Language access notes

Successful programs must offer services in the pet owner's native language:

  • Having a veterinarian or veterinary technician who is fluent in Spanish is optimal for US-based programmes.
  • At a minimum: all written information must be offered in the second most common language spoken in that service area.
Exam trap · minimum language standard The minimum requirement for language access is written material in the second most common language of the service area. English-only documentation fails this standard. An exam option stating "English is sufficient" is incorrect.

Economic sustainability principles notes

The focus group explicitly warned against programmes that create "fiefdoms": siloed operations that refuse to collaborate with other organisations. The recommended approach is cooperative, shared-resource networking that avoids duplication and maximises community impact.

Care Frameworks: Spectrum, Context, and Consent

The conceptual scaffolding behind accessible veterinary care. These terms are frequently tested and frequently conflated. The distinctions below, and the way each concept shifts between a private clinic and an animal shelter, are high yield.

Spectrum of care spectrum-of-care literature

Spectrum of care is the range of acceptable, evidence-based diagnostic and treatment options that extends from basic, lower-cost interventions to advanced, higher-cost interventions. The central premise is that there is no single correct level of care: more than one option along the range can be medically and ethically sound. It explicitly rejects the binary of gold standard or nothing.

  • Each point on the spectrum should be evidence-based, not a compromise that abandons standards.
  • The veterinarian's role is to present options across the range, not to default to the most advanced one.
  • In the AVCC provider survey, 84.8% of veterinarians reported being comfortable offering alternatives to the standard of care when a client cannot afford it (see Barriers tab).

Contextualised care spectrum-of-care literature

Contextualised care is the practice of selecting a point along the spectrum of care using the full context of the individual patient and client: the animal's clinical condition together with the client's financial reality, logistics, values, and goals. Spectrum of care describes the range of options; contextualised care is the decision-making process that chooses among them.

Clarification · spectrum of care is not the same as contextualised care Your summary treats these as synonyms. They are related but distinct, and the exam may test the distinction. Spectrum of care = the menu of evidence-based options (basic to advanced). Contextualised care = choosing from that menu for this patient and this client. A third related term, incremental care, describes one delivery model that sits toward the lower-resource end of the spectrum.

Incremental care notes Forsgren / AVCC

Incremental care (Forsgren) is patient-centred, evidence-based medicine delivered in the context of limited resources. It avoids non-critical procedures and emphasises prevention, early diagnosis, client-delivered in-home care, and frequent re-evaluation.

  • Guiding principle: no care is not an option, and above all, do no harm.
  • Key components: a practical-minded general practitioner, available equipment, trained support staff, strong client communication, and use of telehealth.
  • Requires client compliance and frequent re-evaluation rather than a single definitive intervention.
  • Supporting evidence: an outpatient canine parvovirus protocol (Colorado State) reported approximately 80% survival, versus approximately 90% with full hospitalisation, supporting incremental care as a viable option when hospitalisation is not affordable.
Exam trap · recognition by boards and insurers A recurring exam point: state licensing boards and professional liability insurers should formally recognise incremental care as appropriate medicine. Where they do not, fear of liability drives defensive, gold-standard-or-nothing practice, which is itself a documented cultural driver of the access crisis.

Least-harm approach and gold standard VCE 2024 ref

  • Least-harm approach: when resources are limited, choose the intervention that most reduces suffering, even if the gold standard is not achievable. Something is better than nothing.
  • Gold standard: the most advanced, resource-intensive level of care. It is one valid end of the spectrum, not the only acceptable option. A gold-standard-or-nothing culture is exactly what the spectrum-of-care framework was developed to correct.

Informed consent AVMA ethics VCE 2024

Informed consent is the client's voluntary agreement to a proposed plan after the veterinarian has disclosed the relevant options, risks, benefits, likely outcomes, and costs. It is required regardless of the client's socioeconomic status.

Four elements

  • Disclosure: the diagnosis or differentials, the reasonable options along the spectrum, risks, benefits, prognosis, and cost.
  • Comprehension / capacity: the client understands what is proposed; information is given in plain, accessible language.
  • Voluntariness: the decision is free from coercion or manipulation.
  • Documentation: the consent and the discussion are recorded in the medical record.
VCE Principles 2024 additions for community settings Consent in outreach and community programs must be accessible and culturally appropriate, offered in the client's language, and documented even where it is given verbally. Consent processes that assume literacy, English fluency, or familiarity with veterinary norms can quietly exclude the populations these programs exist to serve.

Needs assessment VCE 2024

A needs assessment is a systematic process for identifying the gap between a community's current state and its desired state before a program is designed. It guards against the common failure of importing a solution that does not fit the community's actual assets and needs (see Community Engagement tab).

What is VCE VCE 2024

Veterinary community engagement (VCE) is the process of working collaboratively with communities to provide veterinary services that improve the health and wellbeing of animals and the people who care for them. It is rooted in the CDC Principles of Community Engagement.

Clarification · cultural humility, not cultural competence The Programs tab uses "cultural competence" wording, which is faithful to the AVCC focus groups. What makes outreach succeed VCE 2024

Proximate leaders

Members of the community being served who bridge the veterinary team and the community. They are essential for trust, outreach, and cultural translation, and cannot be substituted with outsiders.

Asset-based community development (ABCD)

Start by mapping what the community already has (its people, organisations, and institutions) and build from those strengths, rather than leading with what it lacks. This pairs with social capital, the value generated by a community's relationships, networks, and trust; communities with high social capital can self-organise more effectively.

Five resource categories

  • Fiscal: grants, donations, fee-for-service income, in-kind support.
  • Human: veterinarians, technicians, volunteers, and proximate leaders.
  • Physical: space, equipment, supplies, vehicles.
  • Information: data, research, community knowledge.
  • Organisational: partnerships, institutional backing, governance.

Sustainability

Programs that rely entirely on donated labour burn out. Long-term sustainability requires paid staff, stable funding, and integration into existing community infrastructure.

One Health and One Welfare VCE 2024

One Health recognises the interconnection of human, animal, and ecosystem health. One Welfare extends this to treat the wellbeing of humans and animals as inseparable. Improving animal welfare in vulnerable populations also improves human welfare, which is the conceptual basis for integrating veterinary social workers into care teams (see Housing Insecurity tab).

Abstract. Accessible veterinary care (AVC) programmes aim to reduce barriers to veterinary services for under-resourced pet owners and communities. We sought to understand this community’s access to veterinary care and identify any perceived barriers. The study also aimed to elucidate ways SM/CAIT can tailor outreach programs to underserved populations with pets and improve animal welfare throughout East Tennessee. The survey was translated into Spanish and offered by the SM/CAIT team to CH members attending events and classes at their organization’s community center. The municipal shelter attended, and local resources and AVMA brochures in Spanish were provided on pet ownership, internal parasites, external parasites, vaccines, heartworm disease, and spay-neuter options. An Opportunity to Increase Access to Pyometra Treatment
· Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health · 2023 · 10.56771/jsmcah.v2.53

Abstract. Pyometra in dogs and cats is a common and severe problem encountered by private practice (PP) clinicians that requires urgent treatment. This interruption can disrupt the clinic day's flow and result in pet owners facing high expenses for treatment. In many cases, there is a risk of economic euthanasia. Referral to high quality, high volume, spay/neuter (HQHVSN) clinics may be helpful for cases where treatment would be disruptive to clinic operations and referral to an emergency room is not feasible. A previous survey of pyometra practices of PP veterinarians demonstrated that, despite 66% of respondents finding pyometra to be majorly or moderately disruptive to their clinical day, 44% would be unwilling to refer to an HQHVSN clinic. This survey, conducted from June to July 2021 (142 respondents), and a similar survey of HQHVSN clinics conducted from June to July 2020 (118 respondents) were analyzed for opportunities for increased partnership. Proportions were compared using two-sided tests of proportions, while non-normal data were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum. The three primary reasons indicated by PP with a reluctance to refer to HQHVSN (beyond a lack of local HQHVSN clinics) were a perceived lack of prompt appointment availability (20%), lack of aftercare (18%), and lack of adequate equipment (14%). In contrast, most HQHVSN clinics estimated that they generally accept referrals the same day (50%) or the next day (42%). Ninety-five percent of HQHVSN clinics reported having the resources needed to treat most presenting pyometras. Median estimated survival rates for patients presenting BAR/QAR were 100% for both PP and HQHVSN, with IQR of 95-100% and 100-100%, respectively. For patients presenting dull or nonambulatory, the median estimated survival was 80% (IQR 70-95%) for PP and 95% (IQR 95-100%) for HQHVSN. The median estimated survival for patients presenting obtunded or lateral was 15% (IQR 0-50%) for PP and 20% (IQR 2-80%) for HQHVSN. Estimated survival was only different for patients presenting dull or lateral (P = 0.018, z = 2.4). The estimated cost to treat a pyometra in dogs in PP, a median of $1,000 (IQR $700-1500), was significantly (P < 0.0001, z = 8.4) higher than HQHVSN clinics with a median of $200 (IQR $125-300). The estimated cost to treat a pyometra in cats, a median of $725 (IQR $450-1,000) in PP and $125 (IQR $58-200) in HQHVSN clinics, was also significantly different (P < 0.0001, z=8.6). PP ranked economic euthanasia as the most likely alternative outcome to surgery at their PP clinic 32% of the time, while HQHVSN indicated it only 11% of the time. Given that PP clinicians estimate that economic euthanasia is the most likely outcome for approximately one-third of patients not treated by their clinic, there is an opportunity for greater collaboration. The concerns with HQHVSN clinics expressed by PP should be addressed knowing that HQHVSN clinics typically accept referrals within 48 hours, believe that they have adequate equipment, and are generally estimated to have good survival outcomes. For those patients requiring significant aftercare, there is an opportunity to refer the patient for surgery and then accept them back for hospitalization at the PP.
