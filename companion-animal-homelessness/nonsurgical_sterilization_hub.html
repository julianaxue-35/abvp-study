Overview, Ideal Criteria, and HPG Axis

Non-surgical sterilization exploits the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis at three distinct levels. Understanding the axis architecture and the population modeling thresholds is foundational before learning individual product profiles.

Population modeling: the 40 to 50% rule notes

Non-surgical contraception was shown to be effective at controlling feral cat populations only when two conditions are met simultaneously:

  • Immigration and emigration must be extremely limited (essentially a closed population).
  • Treatment rates must exceed 40 to 50% of cats per 6-month interval.
Exam trap · both conditions must be met The 40 to 50% threshold alone is insufficient if immigration and emigration are unrestricted. In an open population, treated animals are continuously replaced by incoming intact animals, negating any population-level effect. Both conditions are required and testable.

The HPG axis: three-level architecture notes

Three levels of HPG axis interference notes

  • Level 1: Hypothalamus / anterior pituitary. GnRH agonists (deslorelin, gonazon) and progestins/androgens interfere with GnRH signalling and gonadotropin release.
  • Level 2: Gonads. Chemical contraceptives (zinc gluconate, calcium chloride) destroy testicular tissue directly. GnRH vaccine targets GnRH after it leaves the hypothalamus but before it crosses the blood-brain barrier, shutting down both ovulation and spermatogenesis.
  • Level 3: Downstream / oocyte level. Porcine zona pellucida (PZP) vaccine targets the glycoprotein matrix surrounding the ovulated egg, preventing sperm recognition. Works downstream, sparing the HPG axis, with fewer hormonal and behavioural side effects.

The HPG axis function is highly conserved across mammalian species, which gives GnRH-targeting approaches particularly broad cross-species efficacy. Gonadal targets include the zona pellucida, proteins expressed on the ovulated egg to facilitate sperm recognition, and the thecal support cells of the ovaries and testes.

Identification marking for non-surgical sterilants notes

Because chemical and hormonal sterilants leave the testicles in situ and animals retain their normal phenotypic anatomy, a form of identification is required to prevent redundant surgeries and to allow field identification of treated animals.

Acceptable identification methods

  • Microchip
  • Tattoo over the caudolateral ventral abdomen
  • Tattoo on the inner pinna of the ear
  • Placement of a small metal ear band

Identification requirements for field / feral animals

The ideal marker for wild or feral contracepted animals should be:

  • Non- or minimally invasive
  • Easy to apply in a field setting
  • Visible from a distance (to inform capture decisions)
  • Lasting approximately as long as the contraceptive is expected to last, OR containing encoded information about the contraception type and when it was applied
Exam trap · ear-tipping is CONTRAINDICATED for non-surgical contraception Ear-tipping is reserved for surgically sterilised animals. It is a permanent, irreversible mark. If applied to an animal with a temporary non-surgical contraceptive (such as a 6-month or 12-month Suprelorin implant), the implant will eventually wane and the animal will return to full fertility. Field teams seeing the ear-tip will incorrectly assume the animal is permanently sterile, completely undermining population management data and control.

Contraceptive Modality Mechanisms

Five distinct modalities exist for non-surgical contraception. Each targets a different level of the reproductive system with different mechanisms, species applicabilities, and failure modes.

1. Chemical contraceptives notes

Chemical contraceptives destroy target tissue (typically the testes) through direct injection, causing permanent sclerosis, rendering the organ non-functional.

Zinc gluconate (Neutersol / Esterilsol)

  • Intratesticular injection causes testicular sclerosis.
  • Results in permanent azoospermia or severe oligospermia and a partial reduction in circulating testosterone.
  • Testicles remain in situ; identification marking is required.
  • Subcutaneous misdirection of the drug causes severe necrotising injection-site reactions and scrotal ulcers, necessitating complete scrotal ablation.
  • Chemical restraint is strongly advisable to prevent misdirection and adverse events.
  • Indicated when surgeons, surgery suites, or skilled staff are unavailable, or where physical castration is culturally unacceptable.

Feline use

Veterinarians are significantly more reluctant to use zinc gluconate in cats for two reasons: (1) feline surgical castration is much faster than performing a chemical castration, and (2) cats experience a significantly higher rate of severe local inflammatory reactions, scrotal necrosis, and self-mutilation following intratesticular zinc gluconate injection compared to dogs.

Calcium chloride

  • Can also be used to sterilise male dogs via intratesticular injection.
  • Must be formulated in a lipid vehicle (such as alcohol or lidocaine) to achieve deep testicular necrosis without causing extreme pain and acute scrotal sloughing.
  • Limited peer-reviewed evidence compared to zinc gluconate.

Saponin (experimental)

  • Harmless in the bloodstream; toxic once taken into a cell.
  • Once taken up by gonadal support cells, triggers apoptosis.
  • Reduces sperm number and motility in male rats; reduces estrous cycling in female rats.
  • Not in routine clinical use.

2. Immunocontraceptives notes

Immunocontraceptives use the immune system to generate antibodies against specific reproductive proteins at one or more levels of the HPG axis.

Porcine zona pellucida (PZP) vaccine

AttributeDetail
TargetZona pellucida glycoprotein matrix surrounding the ovulated egg; prevents sperm recognition.
SexOnly effective in females (by definition).
AdvantageWorks downstream, avoiding upstream HPG axis disruption. Fewer concomitant changes in hormone levels, behaviour, and health consequences.

CostExpensive.

GnRH vaccine

  • Targets GnRH after it leaves the hypothalamus but before it crosses the blood-brain barrier.
  • Shuts down both ovulation and spermatogenesis.
  • Effective in large free-roaming animals: horses, white-tailed deer, elephants. Requires an annual booster injection. Local chronic sterile abscess may develop at injection site.
  • Canine failure: deemed neither safe nor effective in dogs. Caused severe injection-site reactions without reducing sperm population density or correcting sperm morphology.

Other approaches under investigation

  • Several additional targets remain experimental with non-conclusive results: kisspeptin analogs, the NSAID firocoxib, and cytotoxin delivery to the pituitary have all been tested but have not produced conclusive contraceptive efficacy; intraovarian injection of necrosing compounds and intravaginal/intrauterine devices have also been tried. As of this review, no non-surgical method is authorized by the FDA or EMA for contraception or sterilization of female cats or dogs (Pena-Corona et al., Animals (Basel), 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

3. Hormonal contraceptives notes

GnRH superagonists: deslorelin (Suprelorin) and gonazon

  • Provide continuous, non-pulsatile GnRH receptor stimulation, which initially activates then down-regulates and desensitises pituitary GnRH receptors.
  • Result: suppressed release of LH and FSH, reduced gonadal hormone production.
  • Cross-species efficacy is broad due to evolutionary conservation of the HPG axis.
  • As the implant's effectiveness wanes, reproductive hormone levels rise again; females experience an estrous cycle shortly after implantation and at the end of effectiveness.
  • Reversible GnRH-agonist castration is increasingly used as a behavioral "trial run" before committing to surgery: in an owner survey of 404 dog owners, the most common reason for using reversible chemical castration was to test how surgical castration would affect the dog's behavior beforehand; owner-reported behavioral and health effects of chemical versus subsequent surgical castration were completely or largely the same in 81.7% of cases (Roulaux et al., Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Prepubertal deslorelin implantation delays puberty without impairing later fertility: the bitch's age at implantation and the dosage used are the main determinants of response, and long-term delay of puberty was not associated with negative carry-over effects on subsequent ovarian function, sex hormone concentrations, uterine health, or fertility — though effects on joint health, tumor development, the immune system, and long-term social behavior still need further investigation (Schafer-Somi, Animals, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Historical progestins and androgens (1970s)

  • Megestrol acetate (progestin) and mibolerone (androgen): introduced in the 1970s.
  • Required frequent oral administration (high dosing burden).
  • Severe adverse effects: weight gain, pyometra, induction of mammary hyperplasia and carcinomas, diabetes mellitus, adrenal suppression, and behaviour changes.
  • Largely abandoned due to this adverse effect profile.

Feralstat

  • A low dose of megestrol acetate in powdered form, designed to be mixed into canned cat food weekly and fed to feral cats for pregnancy prevention.
  • Ethical concern: no mechanism to ensure the medication is administered only to target animals. Non-target species and wildlife may consume medicated food.
  • Not generally recommended by shelter veterinary authorities.

4. Physical devices (IUD) notes

The dogspiral is the primary example. In humans, IUDs change the uterine environment to prevent implantation of fertilised eggs and are widely used. In dogs:

  • No peer-reviewed safety or efficacy studies have been conducted in dogs.
  • Significant concerns about insertion given the anatomy of the canine cervix.
  • Device safety once inserted is also a concern.

5. Gene delivery (experimental) notes

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors delivering anti-GnRH antibodies

  • Antibody levels increased after administration but returned to normal levels within just one month. Insufficient for practical field use.

Mullerian-inhibiting substance (MIS)

  • A ligand produced by the ovaries that inhibits primordial follicle activation.
  • Lifelong super-physiological expression of MIS would theoretically lead to permanent contraception in females.
  • Tested in cats: only lasted a few months. Not currently a viable field tool.
  • Newer AAV-delivered anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) gene therapy has since achieved durable sterilization in prepubertal female cats: a single intramuscular AAV injection carrying an AMH transgene was monitored for up to 21 months, was well tolerated, did not impair physical growth, and did not affect male spermatogenesis; in treated females it induced sterility by preventing breeding-induced ovulation and the progesterone rise of the luteal phase, giving safe and potentially lifetime sterilization (Godin et al., Nature Communications, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
  • A separate AAV-delivered feline AMH-analog approach works by a different mechanism — pregnancy loss rather than blocked conception: the analogs raised serum AMH more than 1000-fold, sustained for 9 months, and were associated with abnormal estrus cyclicity, non-follicular ovarian cyst formation, and declining antral follicle numbers; surviving follicles still ovulated, so supraphysiologic AMH did not block conception but was incompatible with maintaining pregnancy (Stocker et al., Nature Communications, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Board-Critical Gaps and Exam Traps

Four high-yield items identified in the gap analysis documents that are thin or missing from the primary notes and are likely to appear on the ABVP exam.

Gap 1: the deslorelin "flare window" timing gap analysis board gap

Gap · deslorelin flare timing Your notes correctly state that deslorelin initially stimulates before it suppresses. The high-yield board fact is the specific timeline:
  • The initial stimulation phase (the "flare effect") causes a transient increase in LH, FSH, and gonadal steroids lasting 1 to 3 weeks before receptor downregulation occurs.
  • During this 1 to 3 week flare window: female dogs can experience a fertile estrus; male dogs may show increased libido or aggression.
  • This must be communicated to owners/handlers at the time of implantation.
  • The flare-related behavioral resurgence can be pharmacologically blunted: in a feline case report, gabapentin (10 mg/kg PO q12h for 21 days) given alongside a 4.7 mg deslorelin implant was followed by only a brief resurgence of aggression/marking at day 10 post-implant, which resolved spontaneously within 4 days — much shorter than the full 1 to 3 week flare window (Uccheddo et al., Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · deslorelin does NOT immediately suppress An exam option stating that deslorelin produces "immediate, profound downregulation of pituitary receptors within 2 hours" is incorrect. Receptor downregulation and suppression take 1 to 3 weeks. During this window, fertility is transiently enhanced, not suppressed. Female dogs should not be exposed to intact males during the flare window.

Gap 2: feline reluctance for zinc gluconate is not only about speed gap analysis board gap

Gap · feline zinc gluconate: two reasons for reluctance Your notes state that veterinarians are reluctant to use chemical castration in cats because surgical castration is faster. This is correct but incomplete. The board-complete answer requires BOTH reasons:
  1. Feline surgical castration is indeed very fast and straightforward.
  2. Cats experience a significantly higher rate of severe local inflammatory reactions, scrotal necrosis, and self-mutilation following intratesticular zinc gluconate injection compared to dogs.
An exam option that cites only speed, or only local reactions, is partially correct. The correct answer includes both.

Gap 3: calcium chloride vehicle requirement gap analysis board gap

Gap · calcium chloride must be suspended in a lipid vehicle Your notes mention that studies on calcium chloride as an intratesticular sterilant are limited, without specifying formulation requirements. The board-complete fact is:
  • Calcium chloride must be suspended in a lipid vehicle such as alcohol or lidocaine.
  • This is required to achieve deep testicular necrosis without causing extreme acute pain and scrotal sloughing at the injection site.
  • A pure aqueous solution of calcium chloride would cause severe pain and surface sloughing, not controlled deep necrosis.

Gap 4: ear-tipping contraindication: the operational reason gap analysis board gap

Gap · WHY ear-tipping fails for non-surgical contraception Your notes state that ear-tipping is not appropriate for non-surgical contraception, which is correct. The operational rationale that must be understood for the board exam is:
  • An ear-tip is a permanent, irreversible mark that communicates to field teams that an animal is permanently sterile.
  • Non-surgical products (such as a 6-month or 12-month Suprelorin implant) are temporary.
  • When the implant wanes, the animal returns to full fertility. But the ear-tip persists.
  • Field monitors seeing the ear-tip will assume the animal is still sterile, and will not re-treat or re-capture it.
  • This completely undermines population management models: the animal reproduces freely while flagged as sterile.
The correct marker must match the duration of contraception, or encode information about contraception type and expiry date.

Addressing flare-up effect in an aggressive tomcat following a 4.7 mg deslorelin implant: A case report
Uccheddo S, et al · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2025 · 10.1016/j.jveb.2025.08.005

Abstract. This case report details the management of a 3-year-old intact male British Shorthair cat presenting with severe owner-directed aggression and urine marking, both of which worsened after the introduction of a second tomcat. A behavioral consultation identified fear-based defensive aggression with territorial traits as the primary diagnosis. The case was managed through a multimodal approach, involving the expertise of a veterinary behaviorist and a reproductive medicine specialist, along with pharmacological sterilization using a 4.7 mg deslorelin implant. To reduce the expected flare-up caused by the GnRH agonist, gabapentin (10 mg/kg PO q. 12 h for 21 days) was administered, and behavioral management strategies were implemented. A temporary resurgence of aggression and marking occurred ten days after implantation, resolving spontaneously within four days. Improvements included cessation of urine marking, increased affiliative behaviors, and stable cohabitation with a younger tomcat also implanted after puberty. This case highlights the importance of a comprehensive, interdisciplinary treatment plan. The successful control of the flare-up phase with gabapentin and behavior modification is especially noteworthy, as it may serve as a model for similar hormone-mediated or fear-based behavioral issues. Owner education on feline emotional expression was also vital to the success of the treatment.

Advancing and Historical Reversible Contraceptive Solutions for Feline Reproductive Control
Lluis Ferre Dolcet · Reproduction in Domestic Animals · 2025

Abstract. Medical approaches to reproduction control have traditionally relied on progestins, but these drugs are associated with significant adverse effects in both males and females, including an increased risk of uterine infections, mammary tumours, and metabolic complications. As a result, veterinarians often advocate for strategies such as postponing estrus in females to balance reproductive health and manage population control. In recent decades, advancements in pharmacological interventions have opened new doors. GnRH agonists, which can reversibly suppress reproductive function, have emerged as a safer and more flexible alternative to surgical sterilisation. Similarly, the use of melatonin in female cats has shown promising results in regulating estrus cycles. Other innovative solutions, such as non-surgical sterilisation techniques using immunocontraceptive vaccines, are under active development, offering hope for scalable, humane population control measures. These emerging technologies provide veterinarians with an expanding toolkit to address both clinical and ethical challenges in reproductive management.

Chemical castration of male dogs: Owner-reported effects on behavior and health, reasons for chemical castration, and similarities with subsequent surgical castration
Roulaux PEM, et al · Journal of Veterinary Behavior · 2025 · 10.1016/j.jveb.2025.11.006

Abstract. Male dogs are often castrated to correct unwanted behavior. As behavioral effects vary and there are few health benefits, reversible chemical castration (CC) may be used prior to surgical castration (SC), to ensure desired effects thereof. We aimed to gain insight on when and why CC is used and how dog owners report on its effects. We used Chi-square tests to analyze online survey data of a convenience sample of 404 dog owners, most of which were (applied) university educated women. The most common reason for CC was testing the influence of SC on behavior. Similar numbers of owners subsequently left their dog intact or opted for SC. The most common reason for leaving intact was negative behavioral effects of CC. Owner-reported effects of CC and SC were completely or largely the same in 81.7% of the cases. Our findings might aid clinical practitioners, and prompt dog owners to opt for CC when they consider castration, to assure themselves that future SC will have the desired effects.

Comprehensive Evaluation and Future Perspectives of Non-Surgical Contraceptive Methods in Female Cats and Dogs
Sheila I. Pena-Corona, Melissa Aurea Vaquera-Guerrero, Jose Cerbon-Gutierrez, Juan I. Chavez-Corona, Adrian E. Iglesias-Reyes, Alonso Sierra-Resendiz, Juan Jose Perez-Rivero, Socorro Retana-Marquez, Pablo Adrian Vizcaino-Dorado, David Quintanar-Guerrero, Gerardo Leyva-Gomez, Dinorah Vargas-Estrada · Animals (Basel) · 2025

Abstract. The issue of stray cats and dogs is a global concern with considerable implications for animal welfare and public health. This review aims to provide an updated and comprehensive analysis of non-surgical contraceptive methods tested in studies controlled in vivo in feline and canine females. Immunocontraception via vaccination against gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), the luteinizing hormone receptor, zona pellucida proteins, and sperm, or use of viral-vectored delivery, is yet developing. Hormonal treatment (progestins, androgens, or GnRH) analogs act directly to block the reproductive axis. However, it produced essential side effects. Analogs of kisspeptin, non-steroid anti-inflammatory drugs such as firocoxib, and delivery of cytotoxins to the pituitary have shown non-conclusive results. Additional methods have also been tested, such as intraovarian injection of necrosing compounds or intravaginal and intrauterine devices. At present, neither of these methods offers permanent sterility that can replace surgical sterilization techniques. To our knowledge, none are currently authorized by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for contraceptive methods or sterilization of cats or dogs. Therefore, it is necessary to continue the development of a compound that warrants the sterility of cats and dogs.

Gene therapy delivery of anti-Mullerian hormone in prepubertal female domestic cats induces long-term sterilization
Philippe Godin, Nicholas Nagykery, Natalie Sicher, Julie L. Barnes, Amy G. Miller, Christina Bunner, Amy K. Thompson, Motohiro Kano, Guangping Gao, Dan Wang, Patricia K. Donahoe, Linda Rhodes, David A. Brake, Thomas J. Conlon, William F. Swanson, Lindsey M. Vansandt, David Pepin · Nature Communications · 2025

Abstract. The uncontrolled reproduction of free-roaming domestic cats exacerbates their welfare challenges and the ecological pressure they exert on wildlife populations. Because of logistic and economic constraints, surgical sterilization alone cannot scale to control the reproduction of the hundreds of millions of intact cats worldwide. Herein, we report that the single administration of an adeno-associated viral vector delivering an anti-Mullerian hormone transgene to prepubertal cats can fully prevent pregnancy once females reach adulthood. Treated kittens were closely monitored for up to 21 months to assess long-term health, transgene expression, reproductive hormones, and reproductive function. The intramuscular injection was well tolerated and did not impact physical growth. The sustained expression of anti-Mullerian hormone did not impact spermatogenesis in males. However, it induced sterility in mated females by preventing breeding-induced ovulation and increases in progesterone associated with luteal phases, resulting in safe and potentially lifetime sterilization in the female domestic cat.

Gene therapy with feline anti-Mullerian hormone analogs disrupts folliculogenesis and induces pregnancy loss in female domestic cats
William A. Stocker, Lauren Olenick, Shreya Maskey, Denise Skrombolas, Haitong Luan, Sophie G. Harrison, Matt Wilson, Anne Traas, Mark Heffernan, Samantha Busfield, Kelly L. Walton, Craig A. Harrison · Nature Communications · 2025

Abstract. For female domestic cats, ovariohysterectomy is the only method of inducing permanent infertility. However, hundreds-of-millions of free-roaming cats globally highlight the necessity for alternative contraceptive approaches. One strategy involves a single injection of vector delivering a fertility-inhibiting protein for lifetime contraception. Recent studies in mice and cats have identified anti-Mullerian hormone as an excellent candidate for this type of contraception. Here, we leverage our recent characterization of the molecular mechanisms underlying human anti-Mullerian hormone synthesis and activity, to generate potent feline anti-Mullerian hormone analogs. Single intramuscular delivery of these analogs to female cats using an adeno-associated viral vector leads to a greater than 1000-fold increase in feline anti-Mullerian hormone levels, which are sustained for 9 months. High serum anti-Mullerian hormone is associated with abnormal estrus cyclicity, non-follicular ovarian cyst formation, and a progressive decline in antral follicle numbers, however, the few surviving large follicles continue to ovulate. Unlike previous studies, supraphysiologic levels of anti-Mullerian hormone do not block conception, although they are incompatible with the maintenance of pregnancy. Our findings highlight the complexity of the effects of anti-Mullerian hormone on ovarian physiology but confirm that this growth factor is a candidate for fertility control in free-roaming cats.

2024 AAFP indoor/outdoor lifestyle position statement
American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) · JFMS · 2024

Abstract. Meeting the environmental needs of cats is an essential part of providing them with appropriate care. Individual cats have variable needs, and those needs are, in part, influenced by lifestyle. There are both risks and benefits to cats living exclusively indoors, exclusively outdoors, or spending some time in each environment. Understanding the risks and benefits of these different feline lifestyles provides veterinarians with the tools necessary to counsel clients on how to minimize health risks to their cat while meeting the individual cat's lifestyle needs. This statement addresses the cat's welfare - the welfare of wildlife is addressed elsewhere. Cats need to be microchipped and, when appropriate, wear visible identification such as a tag. Indoor-only lifestyle Although an indoor-only lifestyle avoids the risks inherent in the outdoor environment, significant effort must be made to meet all of the environmental needs of the indoor-only cat. In order to ensure good welfare, the indoor-only cat should have access to all essential resources, including food, water, comfortable and safe resting places, simulated hunting and foraging opportunities, play, safe territory with vertical space, and appropriate and clean toileting areas. See the 'AAFP and ISFM Environmental Needs Guidelines'1 for more information. If the environmental needs of the indoor-only cat are not adequately met, the cat may develop anxiety and stress-related illnesses or behavioral concerns may emerge. Indoor/outdoor lifestyle Safety is the primary concern when letting cats outdoors. Providing controlled access to the outdoors, such as an outdoor enclosure, use of a cat-safe fence, or by walking the cat on a harness and leash allows a more stimulating environment with the potential for more normal feline behaviors while minimizing the risks of being outdoors. To improve safety from their predators, cats should be allowed outdoor access only during the day, and kept indoors or in an outdoor enclosure with indoor access at night. Young cats and male cats are more likely to engage in activities that increase the risk of injury and should be monitored more closely. Outdoor-only lifestyle There are some cats whose welfare needs can never be met indoors. Often these are cats that have been previously outdoor-only, are experiencing tension with other companion animals in the household, and/or whose physical and/or emotional needs are not met in the indoor environment. Forcing these cats to live in confinement creates considerable concerns for the individual's welfare. Barn placement or colony management may increase safety for these cats while allowing for an outdoor-only lifestyle. Trap-neuter-vaccinate-return programs are becoming more widely available and have been shown to improve health, reduce fighting and spread of disease among the population of cats, and humanely reduce the number of free-roaming cats leading an outdoor-only lifestyle.

Impact of a Local Government Funded Free Cat Sterilization Program for Owned and Semi-Owned Cats
Rand · Animals · 2024

Abstract. In most states of Australia, local governments (councils) are responsible for the enforcement of legislation relating to domestic cats. Traditional methods used for cat management based on trap–adopt or euthanize programs have been ineffective, with cat-related calls and cat impoundments continuing to increase, resulting in many healthy cats being euthanized. This has detrimental effects on the mental health of animal management officers, staff in shelters and council facilities, and cat caregivers. The city of Banyule, Victoria, implemented a free cat sterilization, microchipping, and registration (licensing) program in 2013/14. Initially, it was targeted at three low-socioeconomic suburbs with the highest cat-related calls and intake, and was microtargeted at call locations. An average of 4.1 cats/1000 residents per year were sterilized over eight years. The program included stray cats being fed by caregivers, provided they took ownership. The program was later expanded city-wide. Over eight years, city-wide cat intake decreased by 66%, euthanasia by 82%, and cats reclaimed by owners increased from 6% of intake (2012/13) to 16% (2020/21). Cat-related calls decreased in the target area by 51%, and city-wide by 36%. The council realized cost savings of AU $440,660 associated with reduced costs for cat-related calls to council (AU $137,170) and charges from the contracted welfare agency (AU $303,490), for an outlay of AU $77,490 for sterilization costs. Instead of the traditional management of urban cats, proactive management based on targeted sterilization should be utilized by government and animal welfare agencies in Australia and internationally. These types of programs are effective at reducing cat intake and euthanasia and are cost-effective.

Outcomes of a Community Cat Program Based on Sterilization of Owned, Semi-Owned and Unowned Cats in a Small Rural Town
Rand · Animals · 2024

Abstract. Free-roaming cats in urban and peri-urban areas and in rural towns pose significant challenges due to their nuisance behaviors, threats to wildlife, and potential disease transmission. Traditional management methods often involve trapping and euthanizing many of these cats, which is not only costly but also distressing for the shelter staff involved, and have failed to reduce numbers of impounded cats or cat-related complaints. Our study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a Community Cat Program that provided free sterilization, microchipping, and preventative veterinary care for all owned, semi-owned, and unowned cats in a small rural town with high cat impoundments in Ipswich, Queensland. The program was instigated by the Australian Pet Welfare Foundation in collaboration with the Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, Queensland and the Animal Welfare League, Queensland, and it was funded by various organizations including Fondation Brigitte Bardot. Data from 2017 to 2023 were analyzed to assess the impact of the program on cat intake and outcomes in local shelters. A total of 308 cats were sterilized, representing 94 cats per 1000 residents over 3.4 years, and in the third year, this was associated with a 60% decrease in cat intake, an 85% reduction in numbers euthanized and 39% fewer cat-related calls to the local council. These findings suggest that high-intensity, targeted sterilization programs significantly decrease shelter intake and euthanasia, and thus alleviate the psychological burden on staff. These programs are a humane and effective alternative to traditional cat management, and benefit animal welfare as well as community wellbeing. This is aligned with a One Welfare approach, which enhances the well-being of animals, humans, and their environments. Legislative changes are required to support these programs to effectively decrease the numbers of free-roaming cats in cities and towns and the issues they cause, while protecting human wellbeing.

2023 AAFP free-roaming cat position statement
American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) · JFMS · 2023

Abstract. The AAFP supports reducing the number of free-roaming cats through humane capture, sterilization and appropriate homing based on the spectrum of socialization to people and lifestyle that characterizes Felis catus with adoption into homes, where appropriate, and return to the original or appropriate location (eg, barn, community) once sterilized.1 The AAFP supports non-lethal programs for controlling free-roaming populations. Outdoor access for companion cats should focus on methods that address the goals listed above, including leash-walking and fenced yards or other enclosures when possible.2 The AAFP recognizes that the management of free-roaming cats is complex. The AAFP encourages collaboration between humane groups, conservationists, animal control authorities, caregivers and other stakeholders. The AAFP encourages more interdisciplinary research to increase evidence-based management practices.

Prepubertal Use of Long-Term GnRH Agonists in Dogs: Current Knowledge and Recommendations
Schafer-Somi · Animals · 2022

Abstract. The search for an alternative approach of estrus control (induction or suppression) in dogs is an important issue and the use of slow GnRH agonist-releasing implants has been the subject of frequent research in recent years. Studies to date demonstrate that the short- and long-term effects of deslorelin implants applicated at different time points of the prepubertal period are similar to those of adult dogs; however, there are important differences. The age of the prepubertal bitch and the dosage appear to be the main determinants of the response to deslorelin, as well as the individual metabolism of the bitch. Recent studies reported that the deslorelin-mediated long-term delay of puberty does not have negative carry-over effects on subsequent ovarian functionality, serum steroid hormone concentrations, uterine health, and fertility; however, more molecular studies are needed to determine the effects of application time of GnRH agonists on hormone concentrations and peripheral receptor expression. Furthermore, the long-term effects of delay of puberty with deslorelin on joint health, tumor development, the immune system, and social behavior deserve further investigations.
