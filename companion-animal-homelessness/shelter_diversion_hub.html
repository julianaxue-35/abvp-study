Overview: Shelter Diversion Programs

Strategies that help companion animals avoid entering the physical shelter system, preserving the human-animal bond, reducing shelter intake, and protecting Capacity for Care (C4C) for animals already in care.

Definition and primary goal notes

Shelter diversion programs are strategies that help animals avoid entering an animal shelter. They include financial assistance, rehoming support, behavioral training, temporary housing, and miscellaneous community aid.

Safety net programs are the operational vehicle for diversion. Their primary goal is to facilitate pet retention. Impact is defined as the direct, measurable outcome of the program, most commonly a sustained increase in pet retention or a reduction in owner-surrender intake.

Historical roots: the field's modern relinquishment-prevention research traces to a series of 1992–93 workshops (led by Dr. Patricia Olson, Dr. M.D. Salman, and Ms. Carol Moulton) that grew out of 1980s studies first identifying behavior as a major driver of surrender. These workshops led to the founding of the National Council on Pet Population Study and Policy, the organization credited with establishing the evidence base that safety net and diversion programming is now built on.

Relinquishing owners are not simply less caring or less bonded to their pets: a comparative study of individuals who had adopted versus those who had relinquished a dog or cat found no significant difference in human-animal empathy between the two groups, and empathy levels were not linked to relinquishment risk. This matters for how safety net staff frame outreach and counseling — a client considering surrender is not presumptively an uncaring owner (Cardoso, Journal of Veterinary Behavior, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Children in the household is one of the most consistently documented relinquishment risk factors: a multi-country survey conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic found households with children were 4.6 times more likely to consider or have already relinquished a cat or dog compared to households without children (male pet ownership and younger animal age were additional risk factors, and online acquisition source was not associated with risk); 76.2% of owners who relinquished found the decision emotionally difficult even though all agreed it was logically correct (Carroll, Animal Welfare, 2023 — see journal abstracts below). A separate Portuguese case-control study similarly found that presence of children and apartment-style dwelling were the only significant predictors of relinquishment, with guardianship duration before relinquishment significantly shorter when children were in the household (Cardoso, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Human allergy to cats is an under-recognized but quantifiable driver of cat homelessness: up to 10–20% of adults globally are estimated to be sensitised to cats, and the published data on the impact of this allergy fall into three outcomes — avoiding cat ownership altogether, relinquishing an owned cat, and returning a cat after adoption from a rehoming centre (Sparkes, Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Who benefits and how notes AVCC 2018

Owners benefit through

  • Direct pet retention, avoiding a difficult relinquishment decision.
  • Reduction of stress related to inability to meet the pet's needs.
  • Continuation of the documented physical and mental health benefits of pet ownership (cardiovascular, psychological, social support benefits; AVCC 2018).
  • Strengthened human-animal bond.

Shelters benefit through

  • Reduced live intake, enabling the organization to operate within its Capacity for Care (C4C).
  • Higher welfare baseline for animals and personnel remaining on-site.
  • Freed resources for high-complexity cases.
  • Ability to scale operations or expand community programming.
  • Deeper community knowledge, enabling more targeted future programs.
  • Triage and appointment-based (rather than unscheduled walk-in) admission is an increasingly adopted parallel strategy: shelters that moved away from unmanaged, unscheduled intake report improved staff morale, reduced disease rates, and substantially reduced euthanasia (Hurley, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Deferred intake as part of Capacity for Care (C4C) — using waitlists and scheduled intake appointments rather than same-day surrender — can let owners with a resolvable veterinary concern be retained: one shelter study found cats whose owners' primary concern at initial contact was a veterinary issue were significantly more likely to be kept by their owner than to be relinquished, rehomed, or lost to follow-up, while cats whose owners had their own medical issues were more often surrendered outright (Hobson, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).
Exam trap · the crucial guardrail Safety net programs are tools for preservation of the human-animal bond. They do never replace legal or mandatory interventions in cases of active animal cruelty or neglect when the caregiver is unwilling or unable to accept assistance. If an exam scenario describes genuine neglect or abuse, diversion programs are not the correct answer.

One Health model VCE 2024 AVCC 2018

Human health and social services present an opportunity to identify at-risk pets and connect owners with beneficial safety net programs. This uses a One Health model that recognizes the intrinsic interconnection of human, animal, and environmental health and well-being. VCE Principles (2024) reinforces that animals can be a source of stress and disease when caretakers lack access to basic services, preventive medications, or veterinary care, making community diversion programs a public health priority, not just an animal welfare one.

The scale of the problem underscores why this is a public-health issue, not only an animal-welfare one: the ASPCA estimates roughly 6.3 million pets enter US shelters annually and nearly 1 million are euthanised, and involuntary family–pet separation creates stress, grief, and safety risk for families while overwhelming the animal-welfare system. A 2025 One Health call to action identifies three major challenges driving this: a severe shortage of pet-inclusive rental housing, family vulnerability during natural disasters, and increasing lack of access to veterinary care — arguing that animal-welfare and veterinary professionals must be integrated into public-health research and policy at every level (Dolan, American Journal of Public Health, 2025 — see journal abstracts below).

Shelter surrender-and-adoption flow itself can be inequitable: comparing the communities animals are surrendered from against the communities they are adopted into found that surrendering communities had significantly higher social vulnerability (ethnocultural composition, situational vulnerability, and economic dependency, though not residential instability) than adopting communities — for puppies and kittens specifically, animals moved from more vulnerable to less vulnerable communities. This finding is presented as further support that animal sheltering is best understood from a One Welfare perspective (Ly, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Basic Needs Programs

The first of the three focus areas. Basic needs programs address fundamental barriers to pet ownership: food insecurity, housing volatility, rehoming knowledge gaps, lost pets, and crisis-driven temporary separation.

Pet food banks notes

The most widely utilized safety net program in shelters. Provide pet food to companion animals at no cost to owners, usually via donated food. Can be run independently or in partnership with human social services.

Operational requirements

  • Dedicated physical storage space for inventory prior to distribution.
  • Inventory tracking system including monitoring of food expiration dates for safety compliance.
  • Staff or volunteer time for actual food distribution.
  • Ongoing donation management and community partner relationships.
Exam trap · the nutritional self-sacrifice finding Food-insecure pet owners may choose to feed their companion animals at the expense of their own nutrition. Pet food banks therefore protect not only animal welfare but also the human nutritional well-being of the client. This One Health rationale is high-yield on the exam (AVCC 2018, AVCC survey data).

Community partnerships formed around food banks create a framework for other outreach programs, amplifying broader shelter community engagement.

Pet-friendly housing support notes

Housing volatility is a primary relinquishment driver, particularly acute for renters. Shelter interventions include:

  • Tenant rights education: providing accessible information on local rental laws so owners understand their rights in both private and public housing.
  • A regularly updated database of pet-friendly rental units and landlords, including explicit documentation of species, size, and breed restrictions. Building this database requires multiple research methods to verify landlord policies.
  • Collaboration with attorneys, housing assistance programs, and social service agencies.
AVCC 2018 · housing link to homelessness The AVCC 2018 report documents that some homeless individuals choose to remain unsheltered rather than enter a shelter that does not allow pets, and that companion animals may be relinquished due to housing moves triggered by job loss, financial difficulties, or natural disasters. Housing support programs directly address this pipeline from housing instability to shelter surrender.

Housing is not a niche relinquishment driver — it is a major and growing one: an analysis of over 1 million shelter intake records from 21 US shelters (2019–2023) found housing issues accounted for 14% of overall intakes, split among unspecified housing problems (54%), pet-related restrictions (27%), landlord issues (8%), housing loss (5%), and unhoused owners (5%). Intakes due to loss of home increased significantly over the study period while intakes due to pet restrictions and landlord issues decreased. Pit bull-type dogs and animals from unhoused owners were at significantly higher risk of euthanasia or other shelter death than other housing-relinquished animals (Applebaum, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Rehoming advice and resources notes

When keeping a pet is unavoidable, owner-to-owner (O-to-O) rehoming services bypass shelter intake entirely. Research supports this approach:

  • 37% of owners who rehomed a pet in the past 5 years gave their pet directly to a friend or family member.
  • 36% rehomed by taking their pet to a shelter.
  • 11% rehomed directly to a person not previously known to the owner.

These data suggest owners may be willing, and even prefer, direct rehoming when supported. Shelter roles include: hosting courtesy adoption profiles on the organization website, sharing rehoming guides, and referring to specialist peer-to-peer platforms.

Self-rehoming platforms measurably divert animals away from shelter intake: analysis of dog (n=100,342) and cat (n=48,484) listings on the AdoptaPet.com "Rehome" self-rehoming platform found 87.1% of dogs and 85.7% of cats were successfully diverted from shelters, of which 37.8% of dogs and 35.3% of cats were kept by their original owner after all. Owners who set a longer rehoming deadline (more than 8 weeks) were over twice as likely to keep or successfully rehome their animal, and owners surrendering for owner-related reasons had higher odds of successful diversion than those citing animal behaviour issues (Ly, Animal Welfare, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Income shapes which rehoming pathway an owner uses: a US survey of 6,318 dog owners found reliance on informal social networks (friends and family) for both acquiring and rehoming dogs decreased as income increased, while shelter surrender increased with income — an owner earning over USD 100,000/year was more than four times as likely to surrender to a shelter as one earning under USD 15,000/year. Lower-income communities rely more on informal networks to place dogs that would otherwise enter the shelter system (Kremer, Animals, 2024 — see journal abstracts below).

Lost and found programs notes Shelter Animals Count

Return-to-owner rates and the species gap

  • National RTO (return-to-owner) rates reflect a marked species gap: dogs are returned at roughly 30% of shelter intakes, while cats are returned at only 5–10%.
  • The cat RTO gap reflects lower identification (fewer microchips, no collar/ID in free-roaming cats), owner reluctance to search at physical shelters, and shorter stray hold periods before disposition.

Microchip impact on reunification

  • AKC Reunite data: microchipped dogs are returned to owners at 52.2% vs. 21.9% for non-microchipped dogs — more than double the RTO rate.
  • For microchipped cats: RTO rate 38.5% vs. only 1.8% for non-microchipped cats — a 20-fold difference.
  • The cat data is the most striking: microchipping is a low-cost, high-impact reunification intervention specifically for cats.

Stray hold periods

  • Legal stray hold periods (the minimum time an impounded stray animal must be held before disposition) typically range from 3 to 10 days across US jurisdictions, with significant state and local variation.
  • Shorter holds limit the owner's window to reclaim; extending holds increases LOS and shelter costs. Policies vary and are a source of advocacy in shelter medicine.

Reclaim barriers

  • Fees: impoundment fees, daily boarding charges, and required licensing/vaccination compliance costs create compounding financial barriers disproportionately affecting low-income owners.
  • Hours and access: limited facility hours (e.g., weekday-only 9–5 operations) exclude working owners. Extended hours and online viewing reduce access barriers.
  • Language: lost-and-found databases, public notifications, and reclaim processes that are English-only exclude non-English-speaking communities.
  • Fear and distrust: undocumented residents and those with prior negative institutional experiences may avoid contacting shelters out of fear of consequences unrelated to animal reclaim.

Digital and social media tools

  • Effective modern lost-and-found programs integrate: Nextdoor (hyperlocal community notifications), Facebook lost and found groups, PetFBI (national lost pet database, free), and Finding Rover (facial recognition for dog photos).
  • Photo-sharing via social media is documented to significantly increase reunification rates in urban areas where community networks are active.

Owner reclaim initiatives

  • Prevention focus: proactive microchipping clinics, subsidised ID tags and collars, and fencing/containment assistance programs to reduce escapes; owner attitudes already support this focus — a survey of Australian dog and cat owners found 98% agreement that dogs should be confined to the owner's property whenever unsupervised (though only 54% supported confining dogs indoors at night), and 89% agreement that cats should be confined indoors at night (Rand, Animals, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).
  • Recovery focus: up-to-date public-facing lost-and-found database posted on the shelter website and social media; proactive outreach (posting flyers, social media alerts) in the neighborhood of intake.
  • Eliminate heavy reunification fees, which act as institutional barriers to reclamation and disproportionately impact low-income owners.
  • These programs are recommended even for organizations not legally mandated to admit stray animals.
Exam trap · fees as barriers Heavy impoundment and boarding fees are explicitly described as unnecessary barriers to reunification that shelters should eliminate. An exam option suggesting compounding daily board fees to "incentivize faster retrieval" is incorrect.

Temporary sheltering programs notes

Triggers for temporary sheltering

  • Natural disasters
  • Personal health emergencies
  • Domestic violence (DV) situations

Core principle

The primary objective is to avoid permanent relinquishment by facilitating reunification once the owner's circumstances stabilize. Ideally, even this temporary separation is avoided via co-sheltering (housing people and pets together).

Operational requirements

  • Legally approved written agreements with clear guidelines establishing expectations for duration and scope of care. Prevents ambiguity and potential legal disputes.
  • Foster care networks are the preferred housing model: avoids medical and behavioral deterioration associated with physical shelter kennels, and reduces shelter crowding.
  • Fair investment of shelter resources to oversee the program without detriment to the existing shelter population.
  • Collaboration with governmental or non-profit human social services allows the animal welfare organization to focus primarily on animal care.
Exam trap · foster care vs. kennel housing For temporary crisis animals, the ideal housing model is dedicated foster care, not communal kennel runs or isolation wards. Foster care avoids the crowd stress, infectious disease exposure, and behavioral decline inherent to physical shelter environments.

Real-world crisis fostering data support this model: the Toronto Humane Society's no-cost Urgent Care fostering program for people in crisis (housing instability, fleeing interpersonal violence, or undergoing healthcare treatment) had 358 admissions of 328 animals from 244 families over the study period, and 74% of admitted animals were reunited with their owner. The highest reunion rates were linked to dogs, requests in support of people fleeing interpersonal violence, consistent/frequent communication with the client, and animals not requiring veterinary or behavioural care beyond standard levels (Ellis, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2024 — see journal abstracts below). A social-return-on-investment analysis of a similar emergency boarding and homelessness program found an estimated AUD 8.21 of social value returned for every AUD 1 invested, with clients and their animals capturing the bulk of that benefit (Ma, Animals, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Foster care programs: types, LOS impact, and foster-to-adopt notes shelter research

Types of foster care

  • Standard foster: short-term placement for healthy or mildly ill animals awaiting adoption; the most common program type. Reduces shelter crowding and allows behavioural assessment in a home environment.
  • Medical/behaviour foster: animals with active medical treatment needs (post-surgical recovery, bottle-baby neonates, URI treatment) or intensive behaviour modification plans that cannot be managed in the shelter environment.
  • Ringside/hospice foster: end-of-life care for animals with terminal diagnoses who would otherwise remain in the shelter until death. Improves welfare and frees medical resources.
  • Foster-to-adopt: the foster family has the right of first refusal to adopt the animal at the end of the foster period. Reduces the decision threshold for potential adopters and increases placement success.

LOS and medical impacts

  • Foster placement consistently reduces length of stay (LOS) compared to shelter kennel housing — animals in foster are removed from the adoption floor but continue accumulating adoption interest via photos, social media, and adopter matching.
  • Foster programs for kittens and cats demonstrably reduce URI and CIRDC incidence: removing susceptible animals from congregate shelter environments decreases infectious disease exposure, particularly critical for neonates and immunologically naïve juveniles.
  • Behaviour: dogs in foster homes show reduced fear, stress-related behaviours, and reactivity compared to kennel-housed counterparts; more accurate temperament assessment is possible.

Foster-to-adopt outcomes

  • Foster-to-adopt programs reduce the 30-day return rate — families who have lived with an animal during the foster period are better matched and more committed at the time of formal adoption.
  • The model is particularly effective for animals with behavioural complexity: a 2-4 week trial period allows both the family and the animal to adjust before the permanent commitment.
High-yield · foster care is the preferred model for temporary sheltering crisis animals For animals entering temporary sheltering due to DV, disaster, or owner health emergency, foster care networks are explicitly preferred over shelter kennel housing. This avoids the infectious disease exposure, stress, and behavioural deterioration of congregate shelter environments. Written legal agreements establishing care scope and duration are required.

Humane education programs notes

Aim to foster a community that values and understands responsible pet ownership. Targets youth settings: schools, camps, childcare settings, and adult education programs.

Evidence base

  • Studies document the enhancement of prosocial behavior and empathy in children participating in humane education programs.
  • Single classroom visits require minimal shelter resources.
  • In-house programs (after-school camps) are resource-intensive but highly impactful for building community relationships.
  • These programs are rewarding for shelter staff and volunteers, building the shelter-community connection.

Accessible Veterinary and Behavioral Care

The second and third focus areas. Inability to access veterinary care and unresolved behavioral problems are major drivers of companion animal relinquishment, each requiring distinct but complementary intervention frameworks.

Accessible veterinary care: the evidence base notes AVCC 2018

Inability to access veterinary care is a documented common reason for companion animal relinquishment. In one study, up to 28% of surrenders cited cost in conjunction with medical issues as the primary driver. The AVCC 2018 national survey confirms that financial constraints are the number one barrier across all care types (preventative, sick, and emergency care), reported by approximately 80% of owners who experienced a barrier to preventative care.

Cross-reference · spectrum of care framework Board exams test the shift away from a binary gold-standard-versus-no-care mindset toward the Spectrum of Care framework: offering evidence-based, incremental medical and surgical options tailored to the financial and socioeconomic realities of the individual client. The full definitions of spectrum of care, contextualised care, incremental care, and informed consent, plus a shelter-versus-private-clinic comparison, now live in the Access to Veterinary Care hub (Care Frameworks tab). Targeted spay/neuter (S/N) programs notes

Community-level benefits

  • Increases the prevalence of altered animals in the community.
  • Reduction in shelter relinquishment is documented for cats; evidence is less consistent for dogs. This species distinction is high-yield.
  • Client race and ethnicity are not barriers to program utilization.
  • Reduces nuisance behaviors: vocalization, urine marking, roaming.
  • Reduces serious dog bites: intact dogs are statistically overrepresented in serious bite incidents.
  • Opportunity for collaboration with private practitioners (referrals for clients unable to afford full-cost sterilization).
  • Very resource-intensive to establish and maintain; requires careful community landscape analysis to target the desired population effectively.
Exam trap · dogs vs. cats in S/N research Accessible spay/neuter programs show a documented reduction in feline shelter relinquishment, but studies do not show a consistent equivalent reduction for canine relinquishments. Selecting "eliminates dog surrenders" or "no impact on cats" is incorrect.

Return-to-field (RTF) programs notes

A specific diversion model for free-roaming community cats. Cats are captured, sterilized, vaccinated, and returned directly to their original location, avoiding shelter intake and placement in a home. RTF is classified under accessible veterinary care because sterilization and vaccination are the core interventions. RTF programs are distinct from TNR (trap-neuter-return) programs but share the same foundational rationale.

The cat-lifestyle framework underlying RTF/TNR eligibility: guidance from International Cat Care describes free-roaming cats along a lifestyle spectrum and categorises them as feral (avoidant of people, low-density, uninhabited areas), street (free-roaming in inhabited areas, reliant on people for food), pet (living with and coping well with people), or "inbetweener" (previously lived as a pet but unsuccessfully). For street cats, the recommended solution is TNR — trapping, neutering, and returning to the original location, with the tip of the left ear removed as a permanent identifier to prevent re-trapping — continued until as many cats as possible in the target area are neutered; friendly free-roaming and relinquished pet cats are instead best directed to homing or foster care, neutered before roughly 4 months of age (Halls, Journal of Shelter Medicine and Community Animal Health, 2023 — see journal abstracts below).

Accessible behavioral care: the evidence base notes

Behavioral reasons are a primary driver of relinquishment: reported in up to 40% of relinquished dogs and up to 28% of relinquished cats. Behavioral interventions remain an important tool to prevent surrender.

Gap · behavioral risk assessment (Word doc) Board exams pair the behavioral relinquishment statistics with public safety risk assessment. If an animal has a severe aggression history, diversion and behavioral helpline tools must not override public safety or animal welfare mandates. Safety net behavioral interventions are appropriate when risk is manageable; they do not replace decisions about dangerous dogs.

Strategic Program Design and Measurement

Choosing the right mix of safety net programs requires systematic community assessment, resource analysis, measurable goal-setting, and planned lifecycle management from launch through phase-out.

Step 1: Community landscape assessment notes

Before launching any program, assess pet ownership needs in the community. All owners need access to foundational services: basic pet needs, veterinary care, and behavioral resources.

Data sources to use

  • National and local surveys about pet ownership trends and demographics.
  • Municipal pet licensing data.
  • Veterinary clinic patient logs; pet food and supply store sales data.
  • Animal shelter and rescue intake metrics.
  • Primary research: surveys, polls, interviews, focus groups.
  • The Pets for Life (PFL) community assessment toolkit: the validated standard tool for identifying targeted community pet-ownership needs and resource deserts.
VCE Principles 2024 · asset-based community assessment VCE Principle 1 emphasizes becoming knowledgeable about the community's culture, economic conditions, social networks, power structures, and prior experience with outside engagement. Principle 7 highlights building community capacity from existing assets, not just cataloging deficits. A good community landscape assessment is strength-based, not only deficit-focused.

Region-specific intake-reason data sharpen targeting: an analysis of 32,081 dog relinquishments to British Columbia SPCA shelters (2008–2019) found the leading reported reasons were having too many dogs (19%), housing issues (17%), personal issues (15%), financial issues (10%), dog behaviour (10%), and guardian health (8%), with the proportion relinquished for "too many" and "behaviour" increasing over time and financial reasons decreasing; promising region-specific interventions included preventing "too many animals" in small population centres and improving pet-inclusive housing in large population centres (Eagan, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2022 — see journal abstracts below).

Social-vulnerability indices can supplement standard needs-assessment data sources: using the Canadian Index of Multiple Deprivation (four factors: ethnocultural composition, economic dependency, residential instability, and situational vulnerability) to predict surrender risk across nearly 30,000 BC shelter records found these deprivation factors predicted increased risk of surrender, including higher odds of pit-bull-labelled dog surrender in areas with greater situational vulnerability — supporting geospatial social-vulnerability mapping as a tool for a strength- and equity-based community landscape assessment (Ly, Frontiers in Veterinary Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Step 2: Program development notes

  • Define clear, concrete, and actionable goals that align with the organization's mission.
  • Goals should reflect identified needs, weighted by desired impact.
  • A program may target issues that put pets at high immediate risk for surrender, or less urgent but more common challenges, or some combination of both.

Step 3: Resource analysis notes

Program goals and their desired impacts must align with available resources. A realistic resource analysis covers:

  • Resources required to develop and launch the program.
  • Resources required to run the program on an ongoing basis.
  • Resources required to measure the impact of the program.
  • Components: staff and volunteer time, funding for supplies and equipment, dedicated facility space.

Lack of staff and resources is the most commonly cited reason programs are discontinued: a cross-sectional survey of 111 US animal sheltering organizations found most already offered at least one retention-support program (behaviour helplines, pet food banks, veterinary care), and organizations believed lack of affordable veterinary/behavioural services and affordable pet-friendly housing were the most common drivers of relinquishment — but reasons for discontinuing or being unable to offer a desired program were dominated by lack of staff and other resources, reinforcing that resource analysis must be realistic before committing to a program (Russo, Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science, 2021 — see journal abstracts below).

Step 4: Strategic decision matrix notes

A structured process for analyzing and weighing identified community needs and desired impacts against available internal resources. Used to decide which programs to launch, scale up, scale down, continue, or phase out, in order to maximize community impact per dollar invested.

Exam trap · decision matrix purpose The strategic decision matrix balances community needs and desired programmatic impacts against available operational resources. It is not about aesthetics, politics, or adoption room design. A scenario asking what a shelter should do before adding a new program almost always requires a community landscape reassessment, not immediate expansion.

Step 5: Impact measurement notes

Metric typeDefinitionExamples
Usage metrics Measure the volume of program activity: how much the program is being used. Pounds of food distributed; number of unique clients served; number of calls to a behavior helpline.
Impact metrics Measure the direct, measurable outcome: whether program goals are being achieved. A statistically significant, documented reduction in owner-surrender intake numbers; a sustained increase in pet retention rates.
Exam trap · usage vs. impact This is one of the highest-yield distinctions in this subdomain. Usage metrics count activity (e.g., bags of food distributed). Impact metrics count outcomes (e.g., a reduction in surrenders). A helpline with high call volume has strong usage metrics but uncertain impact metrics. The correct answer for "which is an impact metric?" is always the one describing an actual change in the target outcome.

Step 6: Scalability and phase-out notes

Scaling decisions

  • Scaling parameters should be planned at a program's inception, not as an afterthought.
  • A program can be successful by consistently meeting benchmarks without expanding its footprint. Success does not automatically mandate expansion.
  • Any scaling decision must trigger a fresh review of the community landscape assessment.

Phase-out protocol (high-yield exam topic)

  • Clear timeline communicated proactively to past and current clients.
  • Formal transition pathways directing clients to alternative community services.
  • Targeted emotional and operational support for affected staff and volunteers.
  • Relates directly back to community landscape assessment and impact metrics.
Exam trap · phase-out ethics An ethically managed program closure requires transparent communication, structured client transition plans, and staff support. Immediately halting intake, silently deleting records, or automatically transferring animals into the permanent adoption pool are all incorrect approaches.

True barriers to accessing care AVCC 2018 Board gap

AVCC 2018 · top three barriers (high-yield) Your notes note correctly that race and ethnicity are not barriers to program utilization. However, the AVCC 2018 report identifies the top three documented barriers across all care types, which you must know for board exams:
  • 1. Financial constraints: the number one barrier across all care types. Approximately 80% of owners who experienced a preventative care barrier cited inability to afford it. For emergency care, 55.7%.
  • 2. Lack of transportation / geographic barriers: "veterinary deserts" where no accessible care exists within reasonable distance. Reported by 15% as a barrier to emergency care access.
  • 3. Socioeconomic and language barriers: lack of multilingual educational materials, distrust of institutional authorities, and limited awareness of available programs. Younger owners also cited "not knowing where to go" at higher rates.

Don't Forget Fido: A Call to Include Pets in Public Health Research and Policy to Support Families and Communities
Emily D Dolan, Brett Wyker, Elizabeth A Berliner, Matt Goldweber, Ana Hernandez · American Journal of Public Health · 2025

Abstract. No structured abstract is published; this is an editorial/commentary in the American Journal of Public Health (2025) authored by members of the ASPCA's Strategy and Research, Shelter Medicine Services, and Community Engagement teams — a One Health call to action. The ASPCA estimates ~6.3 million pets enter US shelters yearly and almost 1 million are euthanised.
