Access to Veterinary Care

Access to Veterinary Care · Companion Animal Homelessness (12%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield cheatsheet — AVCC/CARE 2018 data, income-stratified barriers, the housing-insecure cohort, program models & care frameworks; added / clarified points marked +.

Epidemiology of Pet Ownership and At-Risk Populations

  • Scale — ~20–29M (million) pets live in US families that struggle to afford veterinary care +
  • Source — AVCC/CARE national study = Univ. Tennessee CARE centre × Access to Veterinary Care Coalition, 2018 (avg 2.2 pets/HH)
  • Multiple-pet paradox — lower-income HH own more pets; only ~1/3 of lowest bracket own a single pet vs 43.6% mid / 44.4% high
  • FPL strata — <138% (SNAP-eligible) · 138–250% · >250%; 2017 FPL family of 4 = $24,600
  • "Pet = family" — 80.2% strongly agreed (AVCC 2018); 95% in a separate 2015 survey
  • Vulnerable groups — elderly · homeless · abuse victims · mentally ill · racial/ethnic minorities; bond via attachment + social-support theory
Trap · the multiple-pet paradox is counter-intuitive and tested — do not conflate with affordability of care, which runs the opposite way. And 80.2% (AVCC) ≠ 95% (2015 survey).

Barriers to Veterinary Care: Three Care Types

  • Three care types — preventative · sick · emergency care +
  • Barriers — cost, transport, trust (plus awareness / language) +
  • Financial % by type — preventative 80% > sick 73.8% > emergency 55.7%; cost is dominant across ALL income levels & all 3 types
  • Preventative — 1/4 of owners couldn't obtain it (highest barrier); falls as income rises; hits younger owners most; 65% want vaccination, 50.7% HW/flea-tick
  • Sick-care convergence — mid-income (138–250%) cite cost as often as the lowest bracket; income does not protect here (unlike preventative)
  • Vet stance — 79.7% feel obliged to offer same standard; 84.8% comfortable offering alternatives (more flex than hold-the-line)
Trap · emergency has the lowest barrier rate not because it's easier to access but because it's needed less often (~1/3 in 2 yrs). Don't invert 79.7% vs 84.8%.

Pet Owners Experiencing Housing Insecurity

  • Cohort — mean age 44 · 77% female · 65% on public assistance · mean 2 pets; 32% had trouble finding housing because of a pet
  • Cost barrier — 96.1% cite cost (vs 80% general pop); pet insurance only 5.3%; 71.1% want vaccination, 53.9% antiparasitic
  • Homelessness — no fixed/regular/adequate nighttime residence; bond stronger than general pop; may refuse a shelter that bars pets; put pet's needs above own
  • Fix — interdisciplinary team; veterinary social workers map housing/financial/social systems (One Health / One Welfare)
Trap · age reversal — in the general population younger owners face the most preventative-care barriers; in the housing-insecure cohort it is older owners. Highest-yield flip.

Community Programs and Sustainable Solutions

  • Spectrum of Care — offer a range of acceptable options, not gold-standard-or-nothing; pairs with incremental care +
  • Named programs — Pets for Life (HSUS) · Rural Area Vet Services (rural / Native American, mobile) · Street Dog Coalition (homeless) · academic student clinics · Companion Animal Foundation (RI VMA, $100 GP voucher)
  • "Champion" — a launch needs a person/group with recognition of the need + passion & connections + long-term stamina
  • Sustainability — geographic means testing (serve low-income zones, no paperwork) · maximise vet-tech leverage · pharma outreach · avoid "fiefdoms" (siloed non-collaborators)
  • Language — Spanish-fluent vet/tech optimal; minimum = written info in the area's 2nd-commonest language
Trap · English-only documentation fails the minimum language standard. Programs that won't collaborate ("fiefdoms") are the warned-against failure mode.

Care Frameworks: Spectrum, Context, and Consent

  • Incremental care — evidence-based diagnostics & treatment delivered in stages, adapted to client resources +
  • Spectrum of care — range of evidence-based options basic→gold; each point valid; rejects "gold-standard-or-nothing"; NOT substandard care
  • Contextualised care — the decision process that picks a point on the spectrum for this patient + client (≠ the spectrum itself)
  • Incremental (Forsgren) — low-resource delivery: prevent, early dx, in-home care, frequent re-eval; "no care is not an option, do no harm"; CSU outpatient parvo ~80% survival vs ~90% hospitalised
  • Informed consent — 4 elements: disclosure · comprehension · voluntariness · documentation; required regardless of SES
Trap · spectrum ≠ contextualised (menu vs choosing from it). In a shelter the shelter consents once the animal is in its legal custody — not the owner; stray / legal holds are the exception.

Veterinary Community Engagement

  • VCE (Veterinary Community Engagement) — meet people where they are; grounded in One Health / One Welfare and community trust +
  • Engagement continuum — Outreach → Consult → Involve → Collaborate → Shared leadership; community power rises L→R (CDC Principles of Community Engagement)
  • 9 principles — know community · build trust · partner authentically · self-determination · respect diversity · transparent goals · mobilise assets · long-term commitment · reflect & account
  • Proximate leaders — community insiders who bridge team & community; essential, can't be swapped for outsiders; pair with ABCD + social capital
  • 5 resources — fiscal · human · physical · information · organisational
Trap · VCE prefers cultural humility (ongoing self-reflection) over cultural competence (a checklist to "master"). If the item cites VCE, choose humility.

  • Student Veterinarian Perceptions of Community-Based Primary Care Veterinary Clinics in Indigenous Communities in Southern Ontario, Canada: The objective of this study was to explore the perceptions of student veterinarians who had volunteered at community-based primary care veterinary clinics in First Nations communities in southern Ontario, Canada. Most students were motivated to volunteer to make a positive difference in the lives of people and animals and improve their clinical skills. In general, respondents felt adequately prepared for and supported during their experiences but did recommend additional pre-departure instructions on roles and responsibilities. Participants were ambivalent as to whether they had learned about Indigenous culture, with several strongly recommending more cultural sensitivity training. Most students found their experiences rewarding because of the gratitude expressed by clients and the feeling that they had made a difference.
  • Telemedicine, Access to Veterinary Healthcare, and Animal Welfare: Expanding the availability and use of telemedicine is one way to help relieve this burden expeditiously and conversations around this tool have become more prominent in both veterinary professional organizations and state legislatures around the country. Embracing the role of telemedicine in the modern practice of veterinary medicine is good for veterinarians, clients, and, most importantly, for patients.
  • The Impact of the Social Determinants of Human Health on Companion Animal Welfare: Despite this, there is a lack of research specifically exploring the relationship between SDH and animal welfare outcomes. Given that the SDH impact on humans, which in turn directly impacts on their companion animal, it is important to adapt an SDH framework for companion animal welfare by characterising the impact of the SDH on companion animal guardians in their attempts to care for their animals and, by extension, the associated welfare outcomes. By integrating the SDH with other key frameworks, including the five domains model of animal welfare, through multidisciplinary collaboration, this framework can be used to inform future programs aiming to improve animal welfare.
  • A Pilot Study Examining the Experience of Veterinary Telehealth in an Underserved Population Through a University Program Integrating Veterinary Student: Cost and transportation are 2 commonly cited barriers to accessing health care in both human and vet med. Nearly 25% of clients reported that they would not have been able to secure transportation and 58% reported they would not have been able to afford an appointment at an in-person veterinary clinic. The population of clients who responded that cost was a significant barrier to accessing care did not entirely overlap with those who responded that transportation was a significant barrier to accessing care, indicating support for the use of telehealth in providing an alternative modality to address transportation challenges as a barrier to accessing veterinary care. Additional data suggests that both client and student experience was overwhelmingly positive, providing support for further service learning initiatives in veterinary student education.
  • Barriers to Accessing Video-Based Telehealth Appointments at a Community Veterinary Clinic During the COVID-19 Pandemic: A total of 205 clients submitted survey responses. Factors affecting access to veterinary care were impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Access to reliable internet in the home was reported by 87% (n = 177) of participants. Digital access was correlated with education when controlling for race, income, age, and gender. Education was predictive of comfort with using video-chat and whether or not clients reported need for assistance for using video-chat. Patient education level was significantly predictive of wanting to use telehealth for at least one type of appointment.
  • Barriers to and facilitators of pet grooming among clients served by a subsidized grooming service program: Ninety-one percent endorsed that at least one supply/support (e.g., brush/comb, behavioral support) would be beneficial in maintaining their pet's grooming needs at home and more than half reported that three or more supplies/services would be beneficial. Differences in the prevalence of specific barriers to grooming were found across income groups, service locations, and service settings. Clients who received nail-trimming demonstrations, on average, reported statistically significant increases in confidence trimming nails following their appointment.
  • Grooming-Related Concerns Among Companion Animals: Preliminary Data on an Overlooked Topic and Considerations for Animals' Access to Health-Related Services: Thirteen percent of the ASPCA-NYPD Partnership's cruelty cases involved general hair matting concerns and/or strangulating hair mat wounds (93% were long-haired dog breed types). Five percent of CE cases received grooming-related supplies to support pet caregivers' in-home grooming capabilities. Our findings underscore the need to understand the scope of grooming-related concerns among animals served by veterinarians and other community programs to improve animals' access to health-related services.
  • Impacts of COVID-19 on Owner's Veterinary Healthcare Seeking Behavior for Dogs With Chronic Conditions: An Exploratory Mixed-Methods Study With a Convenience Sample: The study examined the impact of COVID-19 on owners' veterinary healthcare-seeking behaviour, with focus on dogs with chronic conditions. A dog's chronic illness diagnosis reduced the odds of seeking care during the pandemic, reportedly due to difficulties in accessing care for non-urgent issues. Dog-client separation during consultations were considered problematic, sometimes delaying veterinary advice-seeking, including for euthanasia. Separating owners from their dogs during veterinary consultations should be avoided wherever possible due to impacts on dogs, owners and healthcare seeking.
  • Opportunities for Expanding Access to Veterinary Care: Lessons From COVID-19: Collectively, these responses highlighted how the COVID-19 pandemic created new, and amplified existing, issues with accessing and providing veterinary care. Three critical themes arose; (1) opportunities for further learning for the veterinary profession; including curricula around telemedicine, financially resilient business models and understanding health disparities and vulnerable populations; (2) a need for a network of collaboration and communication across veterinary clinics and access to care organizations and (3) future preparedness for health, economic or other crises response.
  • Predicting Support for Social Actions and Policies that Enable Pet Ownership Among People Living in Poverty: In study 1 (n=212), when the hypothetical person asking for financial assistance was accompanied by a pet, participants expressed less suspicion of them, and the presence of a pet caused those low in social justice orientation to express more concern and give more money. Social justice orientation and attitudes toward animals were associated with greater support for policies that enable pet care and diminished support for policies that restrict pet access. Study 2 (n=278) largely replicated study 1. In addition, two conditions made differentially salient the benefits of the human–pet relationship for either pet or human wellness (vs. a control condition). The salience of benefits to the pet increased support for enabling policies among those with lower social justice orientations; making salient the human or pet benefits similarly affected support for such policies among those who were less empathetic toward animals.
  • The Impact of Pet Ownership on Healthcare-Seeking Behavior in Individuals Experiencing Homelessness: More people are seeking health care for their pets than for themselves. Persons experiencing homelessness with animals place a high value on the health and welfare of their pets, that the animals can pose a barrier to traditional health services and access to overall services, and that the owner’s need for animal companionship and support is high. These findings suggest that utilizing the human–animal bond and creating integrated (human and animal), interprofessional health services using a One Health approach.
  • Veterinary and Pharmacy Students’ Expectations Before and Experiences After Participating in an Interdisciplinary Access to Care Veterinary Clinic, WisCARES: Wiscares is a low cost vet medical teaching clinic. Students reported improved confidence in managing and leading cases with specific positive outcomes in communicating with clients, particularly regarding leading financial conversations. Developing greater insight into diversity was a common theme expressed in students’ expectations but was less frequently noted as an end-of-day outcome. Veterinary students less frequently described the value of the inter-professional environment and collaboration, but this was a major theme noted among pharmacy students.
  • Veterinary Care Deserts What Is the Capacity and Where Is It: This research aims to advance the effort of identifying veterinary care deserts by specifying normative values for two fundamental parameters: the ratio of care providers to measures of the population and the catchment of clinics. Veterinary employee shortages were observed ranging from 20,000 to 60,000 employees depending on how the data are viewed. Parameters for the weighted mean number of employees were reported in a detailed chart. Results of the distance analysis indicate that most people travel between 2 and 6 miles to reach clinics.
  • Veterinary needs for animal cruelty recognition and response in the United States center on training and workplace policies: Race or ethnicity, education and financial fragility significantly predicted perceived ease of AVC. Financial fragility was prevalent among most pet owners of almost all income brackets, highlighting the need for more research into the cost burden of vet care.
  • Assessing the Impact of a Virtual Shelter Medicine Rotation on Veterinary Students' Knowledge, Skills, and Attitudes Regarding Access to Veterinary Care: All students developed more positive perspectives on nonprofit veterinary clinics and believed veterinarians should offer affordable treatment options. The experimental group specifically showed increased awareness of the potential for implicit bias toward pet owners and demonstrated reduced judgmental tendencies toward clients. These students also reported greater confidence in providing incremental care options.
  • Community Veterinary Medicine Programs: Pet Owners' Perceptions and Experiences.: In conclusion, low-cost or free community veterinary programs—with effective communication, empathy, and cultural competence—can help open the door to future positive veterinary experiences for disadvantaged pet owners.
  • Measuring Changes in Perceptions of Access to Pet Support Care in Underserved Communities: This study provides evidence that community-based animal welfare programming has the potential to increase perceptions of access to pet support services.
