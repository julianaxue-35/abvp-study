Spay-Neuter

Spay-Neuter · Companion Animal Homelessness (12%) · Shelter Medicine

Spay-neuter is the core homelessness intervention: HQHVSN (high-quality high-volume spay-neuter) delivers safe, efficient, accessible and targeted surgery to animals that would not otherwise be neutered, cutting shelter intake and euthanasia. Own the four pillars, the total-IM anaesthesia recipes, the early-age criteria, and the community-cat sterilisation thresholds.

Overview & HQHVSN Program

  • HQHVSN (high-quality high-volume spay-neuter) — efficient surgery that meets/exceeds standards of care to sterilise large numbers of cats & dogs and reduce overpopulation and euthanasia.
  • Four pillars — High Quality (individual-animal care never sacrificed for volume) · High Volume (two-table flow: next patient prepped on a 2nd table as the current surgery is closed → no surgeon downtime) · Accessible (barriers = cost, geography, transport, language, culture, safety) · Targeted (low-income owned pets + community animals).
  • Clinic models — Stationary (greatest capacity, can hospitalise; benchmark ~35 surgeries/vet/day, needs a catchment ~250,000 people within a 60-mile [~97 km] radius, ~$35,000 reserve) · Mobile (self-contained vehicle, ≥25/day) · MASH (mobile army surgical hospital — kit carried to a temporary venue): fastest/cheapest start (~$20,000–35,000), ~5 h surgery = a full day (15–20 dogs up to 50–60 cats) · In-clinic / voucher (private practice + welfare partner).
  • Fees must sit below local general-practice prices; shortfall covered by efficiency, volunteers, or other funding. Staffing ~1 vet : 2–3 trained support staff; 4×10 h or 3×12 h shifts beat 5×8 h.
  • One standardised protocol — design it, then implement it consistently; deviations breed error. Articulate program goals first, then build services that measurably serve them. A for-profit S/N program can partner with a non-profit to accept grants/donations+
  • Data tracking & QI — capture peri-anaesthetic + post-op morbidity/mortality and track when/where/why errors occur; measure program effect via shelter intake & euthanasia ↓, nuisance complaints, colony size, kitten production, disease incidence+
  • Staff wellbeing — repetitive high-volume work drives fatigue & musculoskeletal injury; use micropauses, manage noise/distraction/safety, provide support resources & scheduling flexibility. A dedicated facility must meet local zoning, the veterinary practice act, ADA, ventilation & waste/drainage rules+
⚠ Trap: targeting by income = below-median-income areas + pricing, NOT income screening. Intact status is a leading relinquishment risk, so targeting prevents litters and surrender. Non-surgical sterilisation is explicitly out of scope of the 2016 ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) guideline.

Ages & Patient Selection

  • Pediatric (early-age) spay-neuter — ASV window 6–16 wk; pre-adoption neuter possible from 6 wk (ensures compliance); practical minimum ~2 lb (~1 kg) and clinically stable.
  • Owned pets best scheduled at ≥4 months — timed for immunity via vaccination, NOT skeletal maturity.
  • Fasting — 6–16 wk: small meal 2–4 h pre-op, do NOT withhold food >4 h (hypoglycaemia). >16 wk: withhold ≥4 h (overnight OK, >6 h unnecessary). Never withhold water.
  • Final acceptance is a veterinarian's call (history + physical exam + schedule); surgeon sets min/max age & weight; verify sex/repro status, check tattoo, scan microchip.
  • Proceed anyway for estrus, pregnancy, lactation, pyometra, mild upper-respiratory infection/parasites, subclinical heartworm — welfare cost of a prolonged stay outweighs the surgical challenge; exempt only if surgery poses significant risk.
  • Cryptorchid — remove both testes, locate the retained one first; if it can't be found, do NOT remove the descended testis (refer).
  • Age & neuter-status cues — tom-cat penile spines appear ~12 wk, are androgen-dependent & regress ~6 wk after castration; tom jowls are androgen-driven (mature >2 y). Male pups: testes should descend by ~5–10 days (a further ~25% by ~14 wk); penis/prepuce stay infantile if neutered <12 wk+
⚠ Trap: pregnant en bloc — no fetal euthanasia needed if the gravid uterus is removed intact (fetuses stay unconscious); if the sac is opened a near-term fetus may rouse → humane euthanasia then warranted.

Anaesthesia, Analgesia & Fluids

  • Total intramuscular (IM) "all-in-one" is the HQHVSN backbone: one injection of an alpha-2 agonist + dissociative + opioid (± benzodiazepine) gives analgesia, immobility & reversible unconsciousness without handling an awake (feral) animal.
  • Named recipes — DKB "kitty magic" (dexmedetomidine + ketamine + butorphanol) · TTDex (telazol + butorphanol + dexmedetomidine; small volume, same dose dog & cat) · QUAD "kitten quad" (medetomidine + ketamine + buprenorphine + midazolam) · TKX (telazol/tiletamine + ketamine + xylazine; contains no opioid)+. DKB, QUAD & TTDex suit all ages.
  • Reversibility is the design goal — atipamezole reverses alpha-2 (give ~½ the alpha-2 volume), flumazenil the benzodiazepine.
  • Multimodal analgesia — opioid + NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug) + local block; intratesticular block before castration, incisional / intraperitoneal splash for spay. Avoid NSAIDs if dehydrated, hypotensive, renal/hepatic-compromised or very young.
  • Key doses — local blocks (intratesticular / line / intraperitoneal): lidocaine 2–4 mg/kg (cat) ± bupivacaine 1–2 mg/kg; reversals: naloxone 0.01–0.04 mg/kg (opioid), flumazenil 0.01 mg/kg (benzodiazepine); anticholinergics atropine 0.02–0.04 / glycopyrrolate 0.005–0.011 mg/kg+
  • Fluids — high-risk (pregnant, pyometra, blood loss, long surgery) get warmed IV; healthy electives often need none. Anaesthetic rate ~5 mL/kg/h dog, ~3 mL/kg/h cat. Pediatric hypoglycaemia → transmucosal sugar / 2.5% dextrose.
  • Monitoring — pulse oximetry cuts feline anaesthetic death (CEPSAF confidential enquiry into peri-operative small-animal fatalities); SpO2 98–100% on O2; capnograph ETCO2 35–45 mmHg; mean arterial pressure ≥60 mmHg. Most anaesthetic deaths occur in recovery.
⚠ Trap: do NOT treat alpha-2 bradycardia with an anticholinergic — alpha-2 vasoconstriction spikes BP → reflex bradycardia; atropine raises myocardial work against high afterload. Reverse with atipamezole instead. Cardiac patients: avoid alpha-2s, ketamine & telazol; acepromazine = zero analgesia + vasodilation (avoid if hypovolaemic).

Surgical Technique & Recovery

  • Asepsis — sterile gloves for all abdominal surgery + adult dog castration; exam gloves OK for routine cat / puppy castration. Clip just before induction (clipping >4 h pre-op ~triples surgical-site-infection risk).
  • Suture (synthetic monofilament absorbable = default) — poliglecaprone 25 / Monocryl (rapid, ~90–120 d; pediatric, subcutis) · polydioxanone / PDS (prolonged, ~180 d; linea alba, adult fascia) · polyglactin 910 / Vicryl braided (~50–70 d) — avoid in skin/fascia (bacterial wicking). Separate sterile instruments per patient; suture never shared.
  • Incision anatomy — a ventral-midline spay incises the external rectus fascia (linea alba); a flank approach cuts the external + internal oblique and the transversus abdominis+
  • Technique — autoligation (pedicle tie) OK in cats, avoid in large/deep-chested dogs. OHE (ovariohysterectomy) & OVE (ovariectomy) both ASV-recognised; orchidectomy = remove both testes.
  • ID of altered animals — green linear tattoo on/lateral to the ventral-midline incision for owned pets; unilateral ear tip (~⅓ distal pinna, perpendicular cut) is the international standard for community cats (notching discouraged).
  • Recovery — continuous direct observation, warm/dry/quiet, head-neck extended; complications (haemorrhage, cardiovascular/respiratory depression, pain, hypo/hyperthermia, vomiting/aspiration) mostly occur in the first 3 h. Discharge only when sternal, BAR, well-mentated, comfortable, normal RR/HR; send written instructions on normal-vs-abnormal recovery, pain signs, incision care, food/water timing, activity restriction & emergency contacts+
  • Haemorrhage is usually technical — torn ovarian pedicle (more often the right, more cranial/harder to expose) or ligature failure. Dehiscence median ~4 days → always discharge with an E-collar.
  • Early haemoabdomen (<12 h) — refractory hypothermia, tachycardia without pain, pale mm, hypotension, slow recovery; abdominocentesis caudal & right of midline (a negative tap does NOT rule it out); a medium-large dog with >100 mL free blood → consider autotransfusion. 2° haemorrhage presents day 4–16 (pedicle erosion/infection)+
  • Scrotal swelling after castration — >20% at 24–48 h, higher with the open technique; abdominal haemorrhage if the cord retracts. Stump granuloma = too much / wrong suture or excess stump → needs surgical revision+
  • En bloc caesarean = shortest dam anaesthesia time & highest neonatal survival: 45–60 s max from clamping to delivery, one resuscitator per neonate. Avoid xylazine / alpha-2s; preoxygenate, IV induction + intubate; use glycopyrrolate (does NOT cross the placenta) over atropine (does cross); epinephrine 0.04–0.1 mg/kg is the preferred pressor+
  • Neonatal resuscitation — dry & warm, rub, never swing/shake (intracranial haemorrhage); clear airway, mask O2, start IPPV if not breathing by 30 s (intubate with a 14–18 G catheter). Neonatal bradycardia is hypoxic → fix oxygenation; atropine/glycopyrrolate & doxapram not recommended; epinephrine 0.01–0.03 mg/kg ET/IO/umbilical vein; naloxone 0.1 mg/kg if the dam had an opioid; dextrose for hypoglycaemia (~2.2–3.3 mmol/L)+
  • Ovarian remnant syndrome ~0.3% cats, ~0.04% dogs — estrus returns; diagnose while in heat — cornified cells on vaginal cytology, or progesterone >5 ng/mL 2–3 wk after GnRH/hCG-induced luteinisation; submit excised tissue for histopathology+

Cleaning & Biosecurity

  • Non-enveloped viruses (parvovirus, panleukopenia) are the hard target — quats (quaternary ammonium) are NOT reliable per independent research; use accelerated hydrogen peroxide, potassium peroxymonosulfate, or bleach (sodium hypochlorite).
  • Bleach & calcium hypochlorite are inactivated by organic matter → need extensive precleaning + a rinse; accelerated hydrogen peroxide & potassium peroxymonosulfate have minimal/slight organic inactivation and need no rinse.
  • Working dilutions — accelerated H2O2 ~1:32 for non-enveloped virus vs 1:16 (stronger) for ringworm; potassium peroxymonosulfate 1% non-enveloped / 2% ringworm; bleach ~1:32 for ~10 min. Instruments: wipe organic matter & flush lumens → warm-detergent soak → non-abrasive brush → distilled-water rinse → dry open on a lint-free towel+
  • Pack storage — muslin ≥140 thread-count, double-wrapped with indicators inside + out; store dry/cool (<25 °C, <70% humidity), unstacked; shelf life ~4 wk double-wrapped woven vs up to 1 yr in sealed peel pouches+
  • Clean & disinfect all direct-contact equipment between patients: tables, endotracheal tubes, masks, laryngoscope blades, pulse-ox clips, thermometers. Mucous-membrane items need a product effective against non-enveloped viruses.
  • Order of work — operate/clean the most vulnerable first (pediatric, then unknown-vaccination adults); suspected-contagious-but-eligible animals go LAST. Cohort by source/age/health/vaccination status; house pediatrics separately (immature immunity, maternal antibody, likely unvaccinated).
  • Steam sterilisation — wrapped goods 250°F (121°C) ~15 min at 15 psi; unwrapped/flash 270°F (132°C) 3–4 min. Biological indicator = Geobacillus stearothermophilus (run weekly). Heat-sensitive items → hydrogen peroxide gas plasma.
  • Hand prep — alcohol-based rubs most effective > chlorhexidine > iodophors; brushless preferred; initial scrub ≥5 min, subsequent ≥2 min.

Non-Surgical Sterilisation

  • Products are ALL labelled for dogs only (off-label in cats). Neutersol / Esterilsol / Zeuterine = intratesticular zinc gluconate → testicular sclerosis, permanent azoospermia; testicles stay in situ so ID marking is required (Neutersol became unavailable 2005). Suprelorin = deslorelin (GnRH superagonist) implant, reversible (Australia 2004). Gonazon = EU-approved 2006 but never commercially released.
  • HPG axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal), 3 interference levels: hypothalamus/pituitary (GnRH gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists, progestins) · gonads (chemical castrants) · downstream oocyte (PZP porcine zona pellucida vaccine).
  • Chemical — zinc gluconate SC misdirection → necrotising reaction / scrotal ulcers, so chemical restraint is essential; cats have far higher rates of severe reactions & self-mutilation. Calcium chloride must be in a lipid vehicle (alcohol/lidocaine) for deep necrosis without sloughing.
  • Immunocontraceptives — PZP vaccine is females-only and completely ineffective in queens; GnRH vaccine works in horses/deer/elephants (annual booster) but is neither safe nor effective in dogs.
  • Population 40–50% rule — non-surgical contraception controls feral cats only if immigration/emigration are essentially closed AND >40–50% are treated per 6-month interval — both conditions required.
⚠ Trap: deslorelin has a "flare" — continuous non-pulsatile GnRH stimulation first STIMULATES for 1–3 wk (fertile estrus in females, ↑libido/aggression in males) before downregulating, so it is not immediate suppression. And ear-tipping is contraindicated for non-surgical contraception: it's a permanent mark but the implant wanes, so field teams wrongly assume permanent sterility (use microchip/tattoo/ear band).

Population Impact & TNR

  • A homelessness intervention — intact status is a leading relinquishment risk, so accessible/targeted surgery prevents both litters and surrender.
  • TNR / TNVR (trap-neuter-return / trap-neuter-vaccinate-return; a.k.a. return-to-field) minimum package = affordable HQ spay-neuter + FVRCP (feline core vaccine) + rabies where endemic + ID (ear tip + tattoo) + humane euthanasia for irredeemable suffering.
  • Sterilisation thresholds to stabilise a colony — rule of thumb ~70% sterilised; models ~75% of intact cats every 6 months (≈50% of intact adults per 6-mo interval), or ~80% annually / ~60% annual removal.
  • Free-roaming cat dynamics are more sensitive to survival than fecundity (adult survival most) → removal often outperforms equal-intensity sterilisation, and one untreated litter can undo intensive effort. Sterilising males only = essentially no change.
  • Targeted TNR works — a 2-yr campaign altering ~54% of cats cut shelter intake from the targeted zip code ~66% (vs ~12% elsewhere). Desexing reliably cuts cat intake/euthanasia, less so dogs.
  • Community-cat epidemiology — pregnancy is highly seasonal (peaks March/April), ~1–2 pregnancies/yr × 4–5 kittens; FeLV/FIV ~4% (FIV ~4× higher in males); retroviral false-positives reach ~50% in low-prevalence healthy cats → do NOT test-and-cull. Rabid cats outnumber rabid dogs in the US.

Stress & Low-Stress Handling

  • Fear activates the sympathetic nervous system (↑HR/RR/BP); fear-induced tachycardia raises peri-induction arrhythmia risk — stress is a welfare, safety AND anaesthetic-risk issue.
  • Signs span the 4 Fs (fight, flight, fret/fidget, freeze): tense body/face, ears back, dilated pupils, hissing or silence, refusing treats. Fear can imprint and is contagious between animals.
  • Tactics — acclimatisation, calm reception & short waits, consistent housing (same spot before/after), keep littermates together, warmth, secure footing, treats, pheromones, towel wraps; never drag with a slip lead. Community cats: keep in the covered trap, hands-off, move slowly.
  • Pre-visit anxiolytics — trazodone; gabapentin (cat 50–100 mg/cat).
⚠ Trap (cat safety): many gabapentin oral solutions contain xylitol (toxic to cats) — use xylitol-free compounded liquid or capsules (mix in tuna juice).

Repro Pathology & DSD

  • In all DSD (disorders of sexual development), gonadectomy is recommended. Sexual development = 3 steps: chromosomal sex → gonadal differentiation → phenotypic development (Sertoli cells → anti-Mullerian hormone regresses the female ducts; Leydig cells → testosterone).
  • Sex-chromosome DSD — XXY (Klinefelter, most common in dogs/cats), XO: underdeveloped genitalia, abnormal cycling/infertility.
  • Pseudohermaphrodite = gonads disagree with phenotype (female pseudohermaphrodite = ovaries + XX with masculinised genitalia). True hermaphrodite = at least one ovary AND one testis (or an ovotestis).
  • Persistent Mullerian duct syndrome — hereditary (Miniature Schnauzer in the US, Basset Hound in Europe); male external genitalia + male and female internal ducts, often cryptorchid.
  • Male calico/tortoiseshell cats — castrate if testes external; abdominal surgery only if cryptorchid. Uterus unicornis → hunt for the ipsilateral ovary to prevent ovarian remnant syndrome.
  • Feline mammary fibroadenomatous hyperplasia — benign progesterone-driven proliferation in young cycling queens or progestin-treated cats (firm glands, sometimes brown "milk") → flank spay recommended.
  • Hypospadias = abnormal ventral urethral opening from incomplete phenotypic masculinisation+

Pain Physiology, Thermoregulation & Pediatric Anaesthetic Risk +

  • Nociception = 4 steps — transduction → transmission → modulation → perception. 1° hyperalgesia is local; 2° spreads to uninjured tissue via central sensitisation (glutamate → NMDA receptor). Multimodal drug targets: opioids (μ/κ/δ; brain, cord, periphery) · NSAIDs (COX; periphery + cord) · local anaesthetics (Na+ channel; axons) · ketamine (NMDA antagonist; dorsal horn).
  • Thermoregulation — Phase 1: rapid core→periphery redistribution (vasodilation); Phase 2: slower linear loss (radiation/evaporation); Phase 3: plateau >3 h when production matches loss. Hypothermia → ↓drug clearance, platelet dysfunction, ↑blood loss, ↑infection; shivering raises O2 demand 200–300%.
  • Pediatric anaesthetic risk — cardiac output is rate-dependent, so bradycardia → immediate hypotension; O2 demand ~2–3× adult; tiny airways (resistance ∝ 1/r4); high hypoglycaemia risk. Warm fluids, minimise fasting, provide glucose.

HQHVSN — HPAI Biosecurity Framework

HPAI (highly pathogenic avian influenza) mitigation for HQHVSN clinics & free-roaming cat colonies — a 6-domain framework:

  • Facility layout & traffic flow — designated intake areas for feral cats / shelter animals / community pets with signage to prevent crossover; isolation & quarantine space away from high-traffic zones; controlled, limited-personnel access to feral holding.
  • Personnel hygiene & PPE (personal protective equipment) — mandatory handwashing + alcohol-based sanitiser; gloves, gowns, N95 masks during outbreaks; clinic-only scrubs & footwear to protect staff's own pets.
  • Cleaning & disinfection — written SOPs (standard operating procedures) for daily & between-patient cleaning; dedicated equipment per animal group to minimise fomite spread.
  • Patient management — grouped scheduling separating high-risk feral surgeries from routine; immediate isolation of cats with respiratory / neurological signs; plan ante- & post-mortem testing.
  • Public & staff education — train volunteers / caretakers on biosecurity; guide colony caregivers on safe feeding & monitoring for illness.
  • Monitoring & wildlife control — surveillance of colony cats + reporting of unexplained illness; rodent control & wildlife deterrents near colonies and clinics.
⚠ HPAI: isolate any feral/colony cat with respiratory or neurological signs and pursue testing — cats can acquire H5N1 from raw poultry / wild birds.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Integrated HPAI-mitigation biosecurity framework for HQHVSN clinics & cat colonies: a 6-domain protocol — facility layout/traffic flow, personnel hygiene & PPE, cleaning & disinfection SOPs, patient management (grouped scheduling + isolation), education, and monitoring/wildlife control — intended to reduce HPAI transmission risk to staff and patients in HQHVSN settings.
  • Gabapentin in shelter cats (OVH): pre-op gabapentin did NOT significantly reduce stress or pain or improve ease of handling perioperatively — limited evidence for its perioperative use in this setting.
  • HQHVSN anaesthetic/analgesic protocols (2017 US survey, 106 facilities): commonest canine premed = acepromazine + opioid (ace/hydromorphone 26%, ace/butorphanol 23%); canine induction = ketamine/midazolam (39%); commonest feline = total-IM DKT (dexmedetomidine/ketamine/butorphanol) or TTDex; most gave an injectable NSAID; only 40% used local anaesthetics; 86% satisfied with their protocol.
  • Local block in HQHVSN feline castration (RCT): an intratesticular lidocaine (± bupivacaine) block lowered the intraop heart-rate response and improved anaesthetic depth vs placebo, without more incisional complications — supports local blocks in HQHVSN multimodal protocols (~$1 per cat).
  • Perioperative hypothermia in HQHVSN & ambient temperature: perioperative inadvertent hypothermia (PIH) is common in spay-neuter populations despite short surgical times, and is linked to prolonged anaesthetic recovery (cats) and more postoperative pain (cats and dogs). Raising the ambient temperature in surgery and recovery areas may reduce it. PIH prolonged time to extubation by ~3.8 min, and each 1 °C (1.8 °F) drop in rectal temperature at extubation raised the pain score by ~0.51 units.
  • Sterilization identification — veterinary training & guideline compliance: only 5% of private practices tattooed all owned animals and 21% tattooed all unowned; 80% of shelters and 72% of spay/neuter clinics tattooed animals. Better training and broader implementation of sterilization-identification best practices (e.g. tattoos) are essential. Teaching coverage was 31% of lecture-based training, 75% of spay/neuter lab-based training and 38% of clinical-practice-based training; across 425 facilities, those seeing large numbers of animals of unknown background, performing high surgical volumes, or with specialised spay/neuter training were significantly more likely to use an identifier.
  • 'Find My Cat' — AirTags to establish home ranges of feral cats (St. Kitts): limited success due to inconsistent location updates — likely too few Apple devices within range of the AirTags to ping off, plus feral cats' ability to disable the quick-release mechanism. Sex and reproductive status impact the home ranges of feral cats.
  • When goals align — targeted HV-TN(V)R service-learning project (Cornell + local SPCA): Cornell University collaborated with the local SPCA to launch a service-learning HV-TNVR. Daily surgical capacity increased by ~3 procedures; students gained confidence and perception of professional readiness. The target community saw a significant reduction in unweaned kittens and a 3-fold reduction in euthanasia; a small increase in intake from the neighbourhood after intervention reflected better alignment between community needs and access to shelter services. Residents reported profound improvements in cat welfare, kitten births and mortality, reduced fighting, injuries and property damage, and renewed trust in the local SPCA.
  • An ASV critique of the 2024 WSAVA guidelines for the control of reproduction in dogs and cats: ASV hoped to develop a worldwide standard-of-care document for small-animal reproduction control, but became concerned about specialty-care bias, a lack of critical reading of the scientific literature, use of unsupportive citations, promotion of untested and high-cost practices, a controversial blanket recommendation not to spay large dogs, a bias against low-income owners, and a lack of emphasis on the health concerns associated with reproduction and overpopulation.
  • Changes in free-roaming dog demographics & health with a CNVR program (Jamshedpur, India, 2014–2018): used bi-annual street surveys plus clinical data from dogs captured and treated. The probabilities of dogs entering the clinic with mange, TVT (transmissible venereal tumour) and pregnancy were significantly reduced. Sterilised dogs increased, with higher proportions at CNVR-treated sites; the probability of observing lactating female dogs decreased in treated sites but increased in untreated sites.
  • Cumulative gonadal-hormone exposure & cranial cruciate ligament disease (CCLD) — generalised additive model of 20,590 dogs (1988–2023): retrospective cohort investigating the association between age at gonadectomy and health outcomes. CCLD prevalence 2.49% in females, 1.89% in males. CCLD risk was nonlinearly associated with cumulative gonadal-hormone exposure in both sexes — greatest in dogs with the least exposure, decreasing sharply to a minimum at 1054 days (females) and 805 days (males). Early gonadectomy may be preliminarily defined as before 2.9 years (females) / 2.2 years (males).
  • Decrease in proportion of sterilised dogs entering US animal welfare organisations post-pandemic: prealtered dogs had a shorter median LOS to adoption than unaltered dogs. Multiple factors influenced the odds of being prealtered — higher odds among senior and male dogs, lower odds among juveniles and stray dogs. The % of dogs prealtered at intake decreased year over year post-pandemic, which may impact time spent in care. Organisations should consider assessing prealter status at intake to predict shelter capacity and identify trends in access to sterilisation services.
  • Four years of TNR in Córdoba, Spain — a scalable model for urban feline management: 4 years of TNR showed an overall population decline of 2.68%; modelling suggests this prevented ~70% of the population growth that would have occurred without intervention. Program cost ~€0.62 annually per resident. Population viability analysis projects a potential 55% reduction by 2028 with sustained implementation.
  • Gonadectomy status & age and overweight/obese (OvOb) outcomes across 15 dog breeds — retrospective cohort using primary-care veterinary-clinic data: Pugs, Golden Retrievers and Labradors had the highest underlying susceptibility to OvOb outcomes. Among all toy/small breeds, gonadectomy at 3 or 6 months had hazard ratios lower than, or not statistically different from, gonadectomy at a year or older. Gonadectomy still offers significant benefits at individual and population levels; however, the associated OvOb risks are not uniform across breeds.
  • Influence of gonadectomy & age at gonadectomy on intervertebral disc herniation (IVDH) in French Bulldogs — retrospective case-control study: dogs with complete clinical & reproductive histories were classified as affected (A group, with IVDH) or control (C group, without IVDH). Early-gonadectomised dogs (<12 months) had a significantly higher IVDH risk than intact dogs: early-gonadectomised males were nearly twice as likely to develop IVDH (OR = 1.92) as intact males; early-gonadectomised females showed an even stronger association (OR = 9.84) vs late-gonadectomised (OR = 2.77) and intact females. Preliminary results suggest a potential influence of gonadectomy and age at gonadectomy on IVDH risk in French Bulldogs.
  • Management of free-roaming cat populations in Slovakia — attitudinal perspectives of residents engaged with unowned cats (community case report): 276 responses analysed — most respondents women (89%), 61% aged 31–50, 52% involved in unowned-cat management for <5 years. People showed interest in further education, particularly TNR program planning and strategies for cats unsuitable for rehoming. Despite financial constraints being the main difficulty, education was largely accessible to most. Should consider training courses for volunteers covering domestic-cat biology, data collection, impact assessment, and financial planning.
  • Managing free-roaming domestic dog populations using surgical sterilisation — randomised controlled trial: one-off, albeit intense, sterilisation campaigns in open populations require substantial investment and are unlikely to reduce population size in isolation, though there may be some reduction in problematic behaviours and improved animal welfare.
  • Optimising dog population-control strategies in Thailand using mathematical & economic modelling: sterilisation programs targeting female indoor, outdoor and stray dogs may prove most effective in reducing the total dog population by >50% over a 5-year period, surpassing the efficacy of the current intervention.
  • Public perceptions of free-roaming dogs and cats in India and the United States — cross-sectional investigation: Indian participants largely viewed these animals as community members, with significantly greater support for allowing them to roam freely. Both groups expressed welfare concerns, but Americans were more likely to cite welfare, public-health and wildlife risks. Americans favoured adoption and euthanasia strategies; Indians preferred spay-neuter, vaccinate and release for population management.
  • Successful control of feral-cat populations through TNR and long-term monitoring on a remote island in Hong Kong — community case study: initially high cat numbers close to public restaurants and walkways (n>100); as the program progressed, a decrease in cat abundance was documented until only a single individual was sighted between 2019–2023. An example of a successful population-control program for island cats in Asia.
  • Territorially stratified modelling for sustainable management of free-roaming cats in Spain — a national approach to urban & rural environmental planning: outlines a replicable, scalable framework for coordinated action across administrative levels, exemplifying how national policy can be operationalised through data-driven, territorially sensitive planning tools. Findings support strategic deployment of TNR-based programs across diverse municipal contexts — a model for other countries seeking to align animal-welfare policy. Incorporates One Health principles and introduces tools for adaptive management, including digital monitoring platforms and standardised welfare protocols.
  • Impact of CNVR on Greater Bangkok residents' attitudes & behaviours towards free-roaming dogs: in 2016–2023, ~400,000 dogs in Bangkok were CNVR'd. The program showed a decline in the free-roaming dog population and puppies born in the community, and in residents being troubled by free-roaming dogs now vs before intervention. Residents in areas with the highest levels of CNVR also had lower odds of reporting a dog bite. Dog owners showed higher tolerance and care for street dogs than non-owners. Results suggest owned dogs contribute substantially to the roaming dog population and must be considered in management efforts.
  • The pandemic's cruel aftermath — progressive decline in spay/neuter capacity: throughout COVID the number of S/N surgeries performed reduced drastically. The continued decline in S/N over the years impedes access to basic preventive pet healthcare and threatens to undermine decades of progress in controlling pet overpopulation.
  • Trap–neuter–return (TNR) and return-to-field (RTF) programs for managing community cats at Florida animal shelters — survey study: TNR/RTF programs were more common in shelters admitting higher numbers of cats and reporting higher live-outcome rates for cats; rural shelters had a higher euthanasia rate. Programs often provide rabies vaccine (100%), FVRCP (80–88%), FeLV testing (44% TNR / 41% RTF), FIV testing (43% / 41%), intestinal parasite control (44% / 84%), external parasite control (76% / 88%), treatment of illness/injury (91% / 97%) and humane euthanasia (85% / 88%). Expanding shelter-based TNR/RTF alongside community-based programs to support pet retention and increase access to veterinary care can reduce unnecessary admission and euthanasia of cats and deaths of kittens born in the wild; regional collaboration between urban areas could be expanded to support neighbouring rural communities where resources are scarce.
  • "Using my tools for the goodness of the world" — HQHVSN veterinarians on remote-area volunteerism & the impacts of spaycations (survey): 70% had been on a spaycation, motivated by a desire to make a difference and give back to communities in need, as well as a desire to travel and experience other cultures. The study highlighted potential pitfalls: the pressures placed on volunteers, the high cost of spaycations, the questionable/unevaluated efficacy of spaycation clinics, and the potential for colonialism and savior attitudes.
  • Smooth monofilament vs barbed suture using the three-layer continuous closure technique in canine OVH at an HQHVSN clinic — randomised trial: barbed suture showed higher efficiency than smooth monofilament for the 3-layer continuous pattern. No short-term telemedicine-assessed complications with either method. Barbed suture can improve efficiency in surgical closures — especially given large volumes of animals — with a similar short-term telemedicine-assessed complication rate vs smooth monofilament.
  • Assisting decision-making on age of neutering for German Short/Wirehaired Pointer, Mastiff, Newfoundland, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Siberian Husky (joint disorders, cancers, urinary incontinence): major differences among breeds in vulnerability to joint disorders and increased cancers with early neutering. Male & female Pointer breeds had elevated joint disorders and increased cancers; male Mastiffs had increased CCL and lymphosarcoma (females non-significantly elevated CCL); female Newfoundlands had heightened joint-disorder risk (males non-significant); female Ridgebacks had heightened mast-cell tumour with very early neutering; Huskies showed no significant effect on joint disorders or cancers, though female Huskies had a non-significant but elevated CCL.
  • Cat-caring behaviours & ownership status of residents enrolling a cat in a free sterilisation program (Australia): ~33% of cats entering shelters and pounds are euthanised annually; surveyed 1,094 cat owners and semi-owners. Most cats were under 12 months, primarily DSH, and acquired through informal channels (friends/family/strays). Veterinary care was limited — only 15% had visited a vet and 28% were vaccinated. Prior to hearing about the free-sterilisation program, 88.5% identified as owners and 11.5% as semi-owners; these semi-owners were willing to become owners. Of the semi-owners, 93% indicated their transition to ownership was driven by feelings of responsibility, emotional attachment and access to free sterilisation services.
  • Comparison of ear-tipping techniques to identify sterilised community cats — randomised controlled trial: no particular technique was superior on all measures — techniques: Mayo scissors (MS), wood-burning tool (WBT), scalpel blade (SB); haemostatic agents: styptic gel (gel), compounded haemostatic paste (CHP), hemostat (H). While WBT+H and MS+H were superior by the primary outcome, SB+H/CHP was unanimously preferred by tippers.
  • Effect of neutering timing in relation to puberty on health in the female dog — scoping review: robust evidence to support veterinarians, those working with dogs and dog owners when discussing timing of neutering relative to puberty does not yet exist. The impact of neutering before or after puberty on atopy, developmental orthopaedic disease (DOD), neoplasia, obesity and urogenital disease in female domesticated dogs remains unclear.
  • Population management based on TNR and trap-neuter-adopt (TNA) in a free-roaming cat colony (Federal District, Brazil): following interventions there was a 47.8% reduction in colony size and 98.8% of the animals were sterilised. Adoption played an important role in reducing population growth, preventing kitten births, fostering stronger bonds between caregivers and animals, and protecting against death, disappearance and abandonment.
  • Intraoperative nociceptive & clinical comparison between ventral-midline and flank ovariectomy in feral and stray cats: significant differences between approaches — the midline (OVEm) group had a larger incision and longer surgery time. No significant difference in intraoperative nociception. Based on nociception there is no strong reason to prefer an approach for feline OVE in sterilisation programs; however, considering surgery time and incision size, the flank (OVEf) approach may contribute to sterilising more animals and reduce the risk of wound dehiscence.
  • Later-age neutering causes lower risk of early-onset urinary incontinence than early neutering — VetCompass target-trial-emulation study: later-age neutering causes reduced odds of early-onset UI diagnosis vs early-age neutering. Decision-making on age of neutering should be carefully considered, preferring to delay until after 7 months unless other major reasons justify earlier surgery. (Target trial emulation = an observational, quasi-experimental design that emulates a randomised clinical trial.)
  • Opinions of Turkish veterinarians on the termination of unwanted pregnancies in dogs: on the debate over unwanted-pregnancy termination in dogs, there wasn't much ethical debate, and opinion generally allowed that in this case it should be permitted.
  • Readability of postoperative discharge instructions is associated with complication rate in companion animals undergoing sterilisation: only 2% of owner-directed discharge instructions were written at readability levels aligning with the recommendations set forth in the human guidelines.
  • Social mechanisms for integrating community cats into community governance in urban China: social norms and trust activate community social capital, creating a social network centred on community-cat care.
  • Tensile testing in feline ventral abdominal coeliotomy closure with different sizes of polydioxanone (PDX) suture — biomechanical study: the risk of suture failure increased with decreasing suture diameter. PDX 2-0 and 3-0 can be used without reservation for ventral-midline coeliotomy closure in cats. Although there was no statistically significant difference between PDX 2-0, 3-0 and 4-0, PDX 4-0 showed a higher probability of suture breakage and should be used only after careful consideration of the patient while clinical evaluation is pending. PDX 5-0 cannot be recommended as a safe suture size for this closure.
  • Veterinarian use of and sentiment regarding standard of care for the feline ovarian pedicle tie — survey: of those who used the pedicle tie, most learned it from a dedicated HQHVSN facility or a colleague/mentor; they had used it for a median of 5 years and 99% were comfortable teaching it. Of those who did not use it, 60% reported it meets standard of care, 14% that it does not, and 26% that it sometimes meets SOC. Absence from the veterinary curriculum was the most frequently cited reason for not using the technique.
  • "When you cannot put that cat back where it came from" — the call for a 'Working Cat Program': 968 cats enrolled over 5 years; 99% (n=959) had a live release — 90% (862) were "hired" (adopted into non-traditional homes), 9.9% transferred to rescue groups, and 2 returned to their original location. 85% of adopters who responded to a post-adoption survey (329/387) were very or completely satisfied with their cat's performance, and only 10% were not or somewhat satisfied. Implementing programs like Operation Catnip's Working Cat Program can increase live-release rate.
  • "Who cares" — demographics & proportion of people providing care for community cats in seven US study communities: the profile of people feeding community cats is diverse, with Black and Hispanic respondents more likely to feed than White respondents. On average 30% of people feed community cats, and most feed three or fewer. Significant variation across the seven communities, perhaps driven by the number of cats in each. Confirming prior studies, people who have pets of their own are significantly more likely to feed community cats. Results indicate communities have a relatively high capacity to care for community cats.
  • "A cat is a cat" — attachment to community cats transcends ownership status: of 329 survey participants, 295 (90.2%) had provided food, water or shelter to one or more community cats currently or in the recent past. These caregivers tend to identify as white, female and middle-class. Levels of attachment to the cats in their care, monthly expenditures and other sacrifices made as part of caregiving provide further evidence of the strong attachment these individuals feel for community cats.
  • Effect of a canine surgical-neutering educational programme on knowledge & confidence of Indian veterinary participants: a 12-day educational program focused on S/N skills, developed to address the needs of a large free-roaming dog population in India. Participants had increased knowledge scores; being female was associated with significantly higher overall scores. Those aged 25–34 had the lower overall score; among those with postgraduate qualifications, overall scores increased with age.
  • Canine laparoscopic-assisted ovary-sparing hysterectomy does not increase risk of stump pyometra: laparoscopic-assisted ovary-sparing spay appears to provide a safe and reliable sterilisation method, with no observable increased risk of pyometra with hysterectomy. Owners must be counselled prior to surgery regarding the consequences of gonadal-hormone retention and multiple heat cycles.
  • Capturing free-roaming dogs for sterilisation — multi-site study in Goa, India: documented dog-capture rates across six sterilisation campaigns using spatial coordinates and population surveys to assess efficiency. As sterilisation coverage increases within a community, capturing remaining intact dogs becomes progressively more difficult, with corresponding escalation in unit costs per dog captured. For efficient, cost-effective population management: target areas of lower coverage first and adapt capture strategies as coverage improves to maximise program impact.
  • Empirical modelling of Felis catus population dynamics in the UK: a multistate matrix population model combining multiple life-history parameters into an integrated model of cat demography and dynamics, characterising cats by age, subpopulation and reproductive status, and accounting for density-dependence, seasonality and uncertainty. Neutering within the owned-cat subpopulation influences the dynamics of all subpopulations. Neutering owned cats younger is sufficient to reduce overall population growth rate, regardless of overall neutering prevalence. Population growth rate is most influenced by owned-cat survival and fecundity; owned > stray > feral > shelter cats had the most influence on overall dynamics.
  • Characteristics of cat semi-owners: surveyed 8,708 New South Wales residents; 588 were 'semi-owners' of unowned cats. These individuals were significantly more likely to be female, urban-dwelling, from lower-socioeconomic areas, and renting. Notably, most semi-owners also owned their own cats and had more cats than non-semi-owners.
  • Comparison of growth in neutered Domestic Shorthair kittens with growth in sexually-intact cats: before neutering, growth patterns were similar, but neutered kittens were heavier by 52 weeks, with greater fat and greater lean mass. Abdominal girth and rib cage were also greater, but there were no differences in other zoometric measurements. Veterinarians should consider the impact neutering has on adipose-tissue gain, especially early neutering in female kittens; bodyweight should be monitored closely during growth and especially after neutering to prevent inappropriate weight gain.
  • Desexing dogs to decrease sexually dimorphic behaviours (urine marking, mounting, roaming): urine marking occurred most frequently in intact male dogs, with a significant difference between intact females and desexed males. Mounting occurred more frequently in intact males vs intact females and a significant difference between intact and desexed males. Roaming occurred similarly in males and females and in intact vs desexed male dogs. In intact male dogs, age-related differences were found for mounting and roaming — both occur less often in older male dogs.
  • Effects of peripheral active warming & passive insulation on core body temperature during feline OVH — multi-arm randomised clinical trial: female cats were block-randomised to passive (cotton toddler socks), active (heated toddler socks) or control (uncovered extremities). Rectal temperature was monitored every 5 minutes from induction through return to trap/carrier. The rate of rectal-temperature decrease was significantly slower for the active group vs the others. The cumulative difference in final temperature was modest (superior materials might enhance performance); cotton toddler socks alone did not slow the rate of decrease.
  • Feline ovarian pedicle ligation — comparison of two techniques taught among AAVMC institutions (survey): sent to 52 AAVMC member schools (67.3% response); 35 schools responded. 97.1% teach double ligation of the feline ovarian pedicle; 48.6% teach autoligation (pedicle tie); 45.7% teach both. Only 1 of the schools teaching pedicle ties reported not also teaching double ligation.
  • Indoor and outdoor management for cats — inferences about welfare and the cat-caretaker relationship: aimed to identify the influence of management type on cat health and relationship with caretakers. 50.88% used indoor management, associated with a greater variety and frequency of care practices (fur combing, claw cutting, toy offering) and a more positive cat-caretaker relationship. Outdoor management (49.12%) was associated with lower frequency and variety of care practices, not allowing such a positive relationship.
  • Performing an OVH at the time of C-section does not increase risk — retrospective study (125 bitches): performing OVH at the time of C-section did not pose a significant increase in risk of mortality, intra-operative complications, post-operative complications, or decreased mothering ability of the bitch. The C-section+OVH group had an increased duration of surgery and time from delivery to nursing, but it was insignificant.
  • Public opinions on stray cats in China — evidence from social-media data: concerns centred on (1) irresponsible feeding behaviours among citizens; (2) mixed/divided public perceptions of the ecological impacts of stray cats; (3) TNR held high support in public discussion; (4) knowledge about stray cats' ecological impacts was positively correlated with support for lethal control methods. Management policies should communicate to potential/existing cat feeders about the negatives of irresponsible feeding, raise ecological-awareness campaigns, highlight environmental impacts of stray cats, understand citizens' perceptions toward different management scenarios, and make decisions accordingly.
  • Seasonal movement patterns of urban domestic cats living on the edge in an African city: 23 cats tracked in Cape Town, South Africa; in summer, all cats living on the urban edge had significantly larger home ranges than in winter and ventured further from their homes in summer than winter.
  • Solutions-based approach to urban cat management — case studies of a One Welfare approach: explored the relationship semi-owners and owners of multiple cats have with the cats they care for, and the social/psychological impact of an alternative assistive-centred approach. Caregivers showed a strong emotional bond with the cats they cared for, experienced a positive impact on their QOL, and indicated improvement in the cats' welfare after sterilisation through the program; they held a negative view of agencies such as the municipal council. Recommends an assistive-centred approach to urban cat management be prioritised by local councils and welfare agencies to improve caregivers' QOL and psychological wellbeing while also improving cat welfare.
  • Spatial & temporal movements of free-roaming cats and wildlife in two local-government areas in Greater Sydney, Australia: used motion-capture cameras to indirectly capture animal movements over 2 months at 2 locations. Most active time for cats was ~9:30am and 8pm at one location and ~7am and 12pm at the other — overlapping with activity times recorded for free-roaming cats and for bandicoots, possums and small mammals.
  • Impact of lethal, enforcement-centred cat management on human wellbeing — lived experiences of cat carers affected by culling at the Port of Newcastle: cats classified as feral in Australia can be managed by lethal methods including shooting, poisoning, trapping and blunt trauma. The impact of killing animals on shelter staff is well documented, but no prior research had investigated psychological impacts of lethal cat management on citizens who care for free-roaming cats. The study (Port of Newcastle, 2020) showed strong relationships between caregivers and cats and negative impacts on caregiver psychological health and QOL associated with lethal management — a care-centred approach should be prioritised.
  • The impact of pediatric neutering in dogs and cats — retrospective study: evaluated the impact of pediatric neutering on obesity, behavioural changes, and urinary and joint diseases via a telephone questionnaire to 105 owners of cats and dogs neutered and adopted from Coimbra Municipal Animal Shelter. The analysis did not show any statistically significant relationship between age of neutering and the presence of any of these conditions.
  • The role of veterinarians in managing community cats — a contextualised approach for biodiversity, public health & animal welfare: provides a nuanced perspective on the impact of cats on biodiversity in natural areas, roles in transmitting main zoonotic diseases identified in European countries (particular focus on Spain), and the effectiveness of TNR + adoption. To promote animal welfare, veterinary professionals must collaborate with public administrations to implement long-term, sustainable solutions to cat overpopulation, with greater social awareness of the importance of spay/neuter and ID to prevent abandonment and reduce the number of free-roaming cats.
  • A review and analysis of the National Dog Population Management Program in Chile: the program aimed to mitigate human-dog conflict; as human density increased there were fewer dogs per person. Veterinary services and sterilisations were the mainstay and offered free to citizens. Education was offered to all ages through public events and municipality/organisation activities; ID of dogs was obligatory for owners. Recommendations: conduct pre-intervention baseline data collection and tailor interventions/indicators; use dog-population estimates at the local level rather than a countrywide estimate; replace free vet services with low-cost sterilisation campaigns; and create sustainable education plans with enforcement teams in communities.
  • Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as a diagnostic tool to identify queens with ovarian remnant syndrome (ORS): evaluated whether AMH can diagnose ORS in cats. AMH serum concentrations were significantly higher than in spayed cats and significantly lower than in sexually intact cats. Among cats with ORS, the highest AMH concentrations were in queens with cystic ovarian alterations and in one cat from which a whole ovary was obtained. The cat with the lowest AMH had a simultaneously high progesterone concentration.
  • Community attitudes & perceptions towards free-roaming dogs (FRD) in Goa, India: 37% of respondents fed FRDs, with dog owners and Hindus most likely to feed. The majority agreed FRDs were a menace (57%), a nuisance (58%) and scary (60%). Most also agreed FRDs were a vulnerable population (59%), belong in communities (66%) and have a right to live on the streets (53%). Barking was the most commonly reported problem, and the preferred solution was to impound FRDs in shelters.
  • Community dog program in five municipalities of Paraná, Brazil: implemented in 5 phases — initial planning, screening of animals, registration of dogs and caretakers, veterinary procedures with animals included, and a final meeting with all involved. The methodology proved applicable across the five municipalities for implementing the Community Dog Program, allowing a list of relevant characteristics for population management and the most common challenges.
  • Ear-tipping practices for identification of cats sterilised in TNR programs in the USA — survey: nearly all respondents recognised the ear tip as indicating sterilisation, though some associated it with rabies vaccination or socialisation status. Most practitioners tipped the left ear (81%) using visual estimation (92%), preferring a straight-across silhouette. The median post-haemostasis bleeding rate was 5%, with median negative feedback at 1%. The most common complaint involved removing excessive ear tissue.
  • Elective OVH or castration can proceed in shelter dogs despite mild-to-moderate presurgical leukocytosis: 34 dogs <3 months, 22 dogs ≥3 to ≤6 months, 82 dogs >6 months. 45.6% had leukocytosis; dogs <3 months had higher median leukocyte and lymphocyte counts than dogs >6 months. Only 1 dog had a stress leukogram. 47 dogs had recognised infection, but there was no association with leukocytosis. Surgery proceeded successfully for all dogs with leukocytosis. Shelter dogs with mild-to-moderate leukocytosis can proceed with surgery safely; a CBC should be reserved for ill-appearing dogs rather than used as a screening test, and age-specific reference intervals should be considered.
  • Estimating the population of unowned free-ranging domestic cats in Denmark using questionnaires + GPS tracking: aimed to estimate the size of the unowned free-ranging domestic cat population in Denmark and its distribution across habitats. Estimated 89,000 ± 11,000 unowned free-ranging cats, with a mean density of 2 ± 0.3 cats/km², living primarily in rural habitats. Approximately ⅓ of the cats were estimated to be socialised and ⅔ unsocialised.
  • Pyometra treated surgically in a non-specialised setting — outcomes & prognostic indicators: 98% of bitches survived to hospital discharge after ovariohysterectomy; 44% stayed ≥2 nights; 3% had a ruptured uterus. Raised blood urea nitrogen concentration or the presence of a heart murmur carried greater odds of death, though the model had low predictive power. Greater odds of ≥2-night hospitalisation with uterine rupture, inappetence, raised blood urea nitrogen or serum creatinine concentration, low packed cell volume, or dehydration.
  • Owner-reported long-term outcomes, QOL & longevity after pyometra surgery (bitches & queens): 306 owners surveyed by telephone and online. Most reported their pet's health and quality of life at the time of survey were better than or the same as before the pyometra. Outcomes were similar for pets over 8 years versus 8 and under.
  • Outpatient community-medicine surgery for canine pyometra: 50 dogs treated at community clinics; 97% survival to hospital discharge overall, with no difference by facility type. Dogs at both facility types were similar in age, bodyweight and clinical signs. Median time from diagnosis to ovariohysterectomy was shorter at the referral hospital (0 days) than at community clinics (1 day), but the delay was not related to survival to discharge.
  • Ovarian pedicle tie — complications in 15,927 cats at a large US HQHVSN clinic (2017–2018): Complications (pedicle drop or tear, pedicle haemorrhage, pedicle-related death) were coded by a veterinarian blinded to surgeon experience level. Only 0.12% had pedicle-related haemorrhage, and in every instance it was corrected intraoperatively with no further complication or death. Reproductive status, bodyweight, age and surgeon experience level were NOT significantly associated with pedicle-related complications.
  • Isopropyl alcohol vs chlorhexidine rinse and body temperature in feline sterilisation: 70% alcohol rinse versus chlorhexidine scrub. Recovery temperature, heat loss per kg, heat loss per minute, need for reversal and need for recovery heat support did not differ significantly. Predictors of recovery temperature were weight under 2.3 kg (5 lb), body condition score under 4, surgery duration and post-induction temperature. Heat-loss rate in the first 30 minutes was slightly lower with alcohol, and recovery was shorter for cats under 2.3 kg (5 lb) in that group. No clinically meaningful difference — both are appropriate rinse agents.
  • Haematology in juvenile vs adult shelter dogs presented for OVH or castration: Outwardly healthy-appearing juvenile shelter dogs often have packed cell volume and calculated haematocrit below those of adult shelter dogs and below adult reference intervals, but this rarely requires postponement of ovariohysterectomy or castration.
  • Free-roaming community cat management — bioeconomic analysis: Cost-effective reduction in cat numbers required sufficient management intensity regardless of the approach taken. Removal produced faster initial population declines, but sterilisation-based methods were potentially more economical at higher intensities, and strategies emphasising sterilisation cost substantially less than removal-only approaches.
  • Rethinking the animal shelter's role in free-roaming cat management: Compares three shelter-based strategies: removal by impoundment, sterilisation with return to origin, and leaving cats in place with community referrals. Traditional removal is ineffective for population control — removal short of eradication may not only fail to reduce numbers but can magnify concerns. Sterilise-and-return shows promise for reducing shelter intake while improving cat welfare. Advocates individualised case management and an evidence-based approach regardless of whether the shelter intervenes.
  • Memphis TN municipal shelter — impact of incorporating multiple best practices: Five strategies from 2017: managed intake, pet-owner safety nets, community-cat return-to-field, transforming field services from enforcement to assistance, and streamlined adoption/transfer. Cats: median live release rate rose from 35% to 92%, and the correlation between intake and euthanasia shifted from strong and significant to non-significant. Dogs: 25% to 87%. Kitten euthanasia fell from 59% to 3%. Dog return-to-owner rose from 10% to 13%.
  • Population dynamics of community cats before and after TTVARM (Onomichi, Japan): Route censuses over 107 days, fixed-point observation and GPS tracking. Only 11 of 30 cats remained in the community-cat-programme areas, while 13 non-neutered cats immigrated into those areas within a year — suggesting limited efficacy. Worth asking of any such programme: are there caretakers, regular feeding and resources, predators, shelter?
  • Health status of community cats in a tourist area (Onomichi, Japan): Medical checks on over 30 community cats. Commonest problems were alopecia, gingivitis, incisor tooth loss, anaemia and glucosuria. Feline immunodeficiency virus positive in 16.7%; 100% carried Capnocytophaga species and 38% Bartonella henselae — a zoonotic risk to tourists and residents.
  • Attitudes toward spay/neuter in the US population — urban/rural, cat/dog, demographic differences: Survey of 991 US adults. Fewer urban-rural and cat-dog differences than expected, though rural respondents were less likely to find sterilisation cruel, less likely to think animals should have a litter first, and less likely to support laws requiring sterilisation. People asked about dogs were more likely to agree the operation is expensive but less likely to believe it is the right thing to do than those asked about cats. Guardianship, age and gender were the most influential characteristics.
  • Responsible guardianship after a public sterilisation programme: Higher income and education were associated with a higher responsible-guardianship score — more attention to castration, rabies vaccination and veterinary visits in the preceding 12 months, and higher scores for veterinary visits and anthelmintic administration in the preceding 6 months. Cat owners scored lower than dog owners.
  • Should cats be allowed outdoors? Welfare risks for free-ranging cats in Brazil: Online survey; 37.1% of respondents allowed outdoor access. Factors associated with allowing it included unneutered cats, manner of acquisition, rural residence, number of cats owned, other pets in the house, younger owner age, the owner not declaring responsibility for the cat, owner perception of the cat's role, and limited knowledge about disease transmission, zoonoses and toxoplasmosis. Outdoor access carried significantly higher odds of reported flea infestation, sporotrichosis, going missing, poisoning, mistreatment and accidents.
  • The Law and the Pussycat — public perceptions of municipal bylaws for free-roaming cats (Canada): Perceptions depended on cat-ownership status: most non-owners and former owners were concerned about free-roaming cats, current owners less so. There was considerable support for mandatory identification. Cat owners were not supportive of bylaws restricting roaming while non-owners were, and owners were unenthusiastic about fines whereas non-owners favoured them. Owner concerns were the negative impact on caring for a pet cat and fear that bylaws would increase abandonment.
  • The social lives of free-ranging cats (review): Intraspecific social dynamics vary by group — some cats show strong social bonds and preferential affiliations, others are loosely associated with little or no social interaction. Influencing factors include body size, social rank, individuality, age, kinship or familiarity, sex, level of human caretaking, presence of food, individual health and the sexual status of conspecifics. Even the human's sex and the weather on the day of interaction have been shown to affect social behaviour.
  • Home range & activity patterns of free-ranging cats (Chinese university campus): 29 adult cats GPS-tracked 2018–2020. Males had larger home ranges than females during the breeding season, and male home ranges were smaller in the non-breeding season, whereas females tended to have larger ranges in the non-breeding season. Mean steps per day 19,864 ± 1,627. Peak activity 6–10am and 5–9pm.
  • Movement patterns of roaming companion cats (Denmark, GPS tracking): 97 cats with outdoor access tracked for 7 days. Median 5 hours per day away from home, median 2.4 km (1.5 miles) moved daily, median 95% home range 5 hectares (12 acres). Cats over 7 years spent less time away, were less active and had smaller ranges. Access to nature areas increased time away, activity and range size. Intact males spent more time away and had larger ranges than neutered males. Rainfall mattered — cats moved 3.6 km (2.2 miles) on dry days versus 2.4 km (1.5 miles) on heavy-rain days.
  • Health-related welfare problems in unowned unsocialised cats — 598 necropsies (Denmark): More than 83% of cats had no major findings and 54% had no finding indicating a welfare issue at all; over 83% had a body condition score in the normal range and only 0.3% were emaciated. The commonest finding was ectoparasites — lice 15.9%, fleas 12.3%, ear mites 6.7%, ticks 4.7%. Feline immunodeficiency virus 9.2% and feline leukaemia virus 1.2%. The commonest lesions related to the teeth. Useful counterweight to the assumption that unowned unsocialised cats are uniformly in poor welfare.
  • Seasonal patterns of oestrus & reproduction in street dogs of Indian cities: Consistent reproductive seasonality across all three cities studied, with peak oestrus and pregnancy in the late and post-monsoon season and pup proportions peaking soon after. Minor inter-city differences are attributed to localised seasonal climatic and human factors — relevant to timing sterilisation campaigns for maximum effect.
  • Technology for street-dog welfare & digital data capture during sterilisation programmes: Large-scale street-dog sterilisation is limited by resources, staffing and less-than-ideal facilities, and recordkeeping is often a challenge — which complicates returning a sterilised dog to its exact point of capture. Street dogs are territorial, so return to an incorrect location brings welfare problems and increased risk of post-operative complications including death. The HSIapp can support accurate return to the exact capture location, digital data capture and patient welfare.
