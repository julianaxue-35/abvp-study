Spay-Neuter

Spay-Neuter · Companion Animal Homelessness (12%) · Shelter Medicine

Spay-neuter is the core homelessness intervention: HQHVSN (high-quality high-volume spay-neuter) delivers safe, efficient, accessible and targeted surgery to animals that would not otherwise be neutered, cutting shelter intake and euthanasia. Own the four pillars, the total-IM anaesthesia recipes, the early-age criteria, and the community-cat sterilisation thresholds.

Overview & HQHVSN Program

  • HQHVSN (high-quality high-volume spay-neuter) — efficient surgery that meets/exceeds standards of care to sterilise large numbers of cats & dogs and reduce overpopulation and euthanasia.
  • Four pillars — High Quality (individual-animal care never sacrificed for volume) · High Volume (two-table flow: next patient prepped on a 2nd table as the current surgery is closed → no surgeon downtime) · Accessible (barriers = cost, geography, transport, language, culture, safety) · Targeted (low-income owned pets + community animals).
  • Clinic models — Stationary (greatest capacity, can hospitalise; benchmark ~35 surgeries/vet/day, needs a catchment ~250,000 people within a 60-mile [~97 km] radius, ~$35,000 reserve) · Mobile (self-contained vehicle, ≥25/day) · MASH (mobile army surgical hospital — kit carried to a temporary venue): fastest/cheapest start (~$20,000–35,000), ~5 h surgery = a full day (15–20 dogs up to 50–60 cats) · In-clinic / voucher (private practice + welfare partner).
  • Fees must sit below local general-practice prices; shortfall covered by efficiency, volunteers, or other funding. Staffing ~1 vet : 2–3 trained support staff; 4×10 h or 3×12 h shifts beat 5×8 h.
  • One standardised protocol — design it, then implement it consistently; deviations breed error. Articulate program goals first, then build services that measurably serve them. A for-profit S/N program can partner with a non-profit to accept grants/donations+
  • Data tracking & QI — capture peri-anaesthetic + post-op morbidity/mortality and track when/where/why errors occur; measure program effect via shelter intake & euthanasia ↓, nuisance complaints, colony size, kitten production, disease incidence+
  • Staff wellbeing — repetitive high-volume work drives fatigue & musculoskeletal injury; use micropauses, manage noise/distraction/safety, provide support resources & scheduling flexibility. A dedicated facility must meet local zoning, the veterinary practice act, ADA, ventilation & waste/drainage rules+
⚠ Trap: targeting by income = below-median-income areas + pricing, NOT income screening. Intact status is a leading relinquishment risk, so targeting prevents litters and surrender. Non-surgical sterilisation is explicitly out of scope of the 2016 ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) guideline.

Ages & Patient Selection

  • Pediatric (early-age) spay-neuter — ASV window 6–16 wk; pre-adoption neuter possible from 6 wk (ensures compliance); practical minimum ~2 lb (~1 kg) and clinically stable.
  • Owned pets best scheduled at ≥4 months — timed for immunity via vaccination, NOT skeletal maturity.
  • Fasting — 6–16 wk: small meal 2–4 h pre-op, do NOT withhold food >4 h (hypoglycaemia). >16 wk: withhold ≥4 h (overnight OK, >6 h unnecessary). Never withhold water.
  • Final acceptance is a veterinarian's call (history + physical exam + schedule); surgeon sets min/max age & weight; verify sex/repro status, check tattoo, scan microchip.
  • Proceed anyway for estrus, pregnancy, lactation, pyometra, mild upper-respiratory infection/parasites, subclinical heartworm — welfare cost of a prolonged stay outweighs the surgical challenge; exempt only if surgery poses significant risk.
  • Cryptorchid — remove both testes, locate the retained one first; if it can't be found, do NOT remove the descended testis (refer).
  • Age & neuter-status cues — tom-cat penile spines appear ~12 wk, are androgen-dependent & regress ~6 wk after castration; tom jowls are androgen-driven (mature >2 y). Male pups: testes should descend by ~5–10 days (a further ~25% by ~14 wk); penis/prepuce stay infantile if neutered <12 wk+
⚠ Trap: pregnant en bloc — no fetal euthanasia needed if the gravid uterus is removed intact (fetuses stay unconscious); if the sac is opened a near-term fetus may rouse → humane euthanasia then warranted.

Anaesthesia, Analgesia & Fluids

  • Total intramuscular (IM) "all-in-one" is the HQHVSN backbone: one injection of an alpha-2 agonist + dissociative + opioid (± benzodiazepine) gives analgesia, immobility & reversible unconsciousness without handling an awake (feral) animal.
  • Named recipes — DKB "kitty magic" (dexmedetomidine + ketamine + butorphanol) · TTDex (telazol + butorphanol + dexmedetomidine; small volume, same dose dog & cat) · QUAD "kitten quad" (medetomidine + ketamine + buprenorphine + midazolam) · TKX (telazol/tiletamine + ketamine + xylazine; contains no opioid)+. DKB, QUAD & TTDex suit all ages.
  • Reversibility is the design goal — atipamezole reverses alpha-2 (give ~½ the alpha-2 volume), flumazenil the benzodiazepine.
  • Multimodal analgesia — opioid + NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug) + local block; intratesticular block before castration, incisional / intraperitoneal splash for spay. Avoid NSAIDs if dehydrated, hypotensive, renal/hepatic-compromised or very young.
  • Key doses — local blocks (intratesticular / line / intraperitoneal): lidocaine 2–4 mg/kg (cat) ± bupivacaine 1–2 mg/kg; reversals: naloxone 0.01–0.04 mg/kg (opioid), flumazenil 0.01 mg/kg (benzodiazepine); anticholinergics atropine 0.02–0.04 / glycopyrrolate 0.005–0.011 mg/kg+
  • Fluids — high-risk (pregnant, pyometra, blood loss, long surgery) get warmed IV; healthy electives often need none. Anaesthetic rate ~5 mL/kg/h dog, ~3 mL/kg/h cat. Pediatric hypoglycaemia → transmucosal sugar / 2.5% dextrose.
  • Monitoring — pulse oximetry cuts feline anaesthetic death (CEPSAF confidential enquiry into peri-operative small-animal fatalities); SpO2 98–100% on O2; capnograph ETCO2 35–45 mmHg; mean arterial pressure ≥60 mmHg. Most anaesthetic deaths occur in recovery.
⚠ Trap: do NOT treat alpha-2 bradycardia with an anticholinergic — alpha-2 vasoconstriction spikes BP → reflex bradycardia; atropine raises myocardial work against high afterload. Reverse with atipamezole instead. Cardiac patients: avoid alpha-2s, ketamine & telazol; acepromazine = zero analgesia + vasodilation (avoid if hypovolaemic).

Surgical Technique & Recovery

  • Asepsis — sterile gloves for all abdominal surgery + adult dog castration; exam gloves OK for routine cat / puppy castration. Clip just before induction (clipping >4 h pre-op ~triples surgical-site-infection risk).
  • Suture (synthetic monofilament absorbable = default) — poliglecaprone 25 / Monocryl (rapid, ~90–120 d; pediatric, subcutis) · polydioxanone / PDS (prolonged, ~180 d; linea alba, adult fascia) · polyglactin 910 / Vicryl braided (~50–70 d) — avoid in skin/fascia (bacterial wicking). Separate sterile instruments per patient; suture never shared.
  • Incision anatomy — a ventral-midline spay incises the external rectus fascia (linea alba); a flank approach cuts the external + internal oblique and the transversus abdominis+
  • Technique — autoligation (pedicle tie) OK in cats, avoid in large/deep-chested dogs. OHE (ovariohysterectomy) & OVE (ovariectomy) both ASV-recognised; orchidectomy = remove both testes.
  • ID of altered animals — green linear tattoo on/lateral to the ventral-midline incision for owned pets; unilateral ear tip (~⅓ distal pinna, perpendicular cut) is the international standard for community cats (notching discouraged).
  • Recovery — continuous direct observation, warm/dry/quiet, head-neck extended; complications (haemorrhage, cardiovascular/respiratory depression, pain, hypo/hyperthermia, vomiting/aspiration) mostly occur in the first 3 h. Discharge only when sternal, BAR, well-mentated, comfortable, normal RR/HR; send written instructions on normal-vs-abnormal recovery, pain signs, incision care, food/water timing, activity restriction & emergency contacts+
  • Haemorrhage is usually technical — torn ovarian pedicle (more often the right, more cranial/harder to expose) or ligature failure. Dehiscence median ~4 days → always discharge with an E-collar.
  • Early haemoabdomen (<12 h) — refractory hypothermia, tachycardia without pain, pale mm, hypotension, slow recovery; abdominocentesis caudal & right of midline (a negative tap does NOT rule it out); a medium-large dog with >100 mL free blood → consider autotransfusion. 2° haemorrhage presents day 4–16 (pedicle erosion/infection)+
  • Scrotal swelling after castration — >20% at 24–48 h, higher with the open technique; abdominal haemorrhage if the cord retracts. Stump granuloma = too much / wrong suture or excess stump → needs surgical revision+
  • En bloc caesarean = shortest dam anaesthesia time & highest neonatal survival: 45–60 s max from clamping to delivery, one resuscitator per neonate. Avoid xylazine / alpha-2s; preoxygenate, IV induction + intubate; use glycopyrrolate (does NOT cross the placenta) over atropine (does cross); epinephrine 0.04–0.1 mg/kg is the preferred pressor+
  • Neonatal resuscitation — dry & warm, rub, never swing/shake (intracranial haemorrhage); clear airway, mask O2, start IPPV if not breathing by 30 s (intubate with a 14–18 G catheter). Neonatal bradycardia is hypoxic → fix oxygenation; atropine/glycopyrrolate & doxapram not recommended; epinephrine 0.01–0.03 mg/kg ET/IO/umbilical vein; naloxone 0.1 mg/kg if the dam had an opioid; dextrose for hypoglycaemia (~2.2–3.3 mmol/L)+
  • Ovarian remnant syndrome ~0.3% cats, ~0.04% dogs — estrus returns; diagnose while in heat — cornified cells on vaginal cytology, or progesterone >5 ng/mL 2–3 wk after GnRH/hCG-induced luteinisation; submit excised tissue for histopathology+

Cleaning & Biosecurity

  • Non-enveloped viruses (parvovirus, panleukopenia) are the hard target — quats (quaternary ammonium) are NOT reliable per independent research; use accelerated hydrogen peroxide, potassium peroxymonosulfate, or bleach (sodium hypochlorite).
  • Bleach & calcium hypochlorite are inactivated by organic matter → need extensive precleaning + a rinse; accelerated hydrogen peroxide & potassium peroxymonosulfate have minimal/slight organic inactivation and need no rinse.
  • Working dilutions — accelerated H2O2 ~1:32 for non-enveloped virus vs 1:16 (stronger) for ringworm; potassium peroxymonosulfate 1% non-enveloped / 2% ringworm; bleach ~1:32 for ~10 min. Instruments: wipe organic matter & flush lumens → warm-detergent soak → non-abrasive brush → distilled-water rinse → dry open on a lint-free towel+
  • Pack storage — muslin ≥140 thread-count, double-wrapped with indicators inside + out; store dry/cool (<25 °C, <70% humidity), unstacked; shelf life ~4 wk double-wrapped woven vs up to 1 yr in sealed peel pouches+
  • Clean & disinfect all direct-contact equipment between patients: tables, endotracheal tubes, masks, laryngoscope blades, pulse-ox clips, thermometers. Mucous-membrane items need a product effective against non-enveloped viruses.
  • Order of work — operate/clean the most vulnerable first (pediatric, then unknown-vaccination adults); suspected-contagious-but-eligible animals go LAST. Cohort by source/age/health/vaccination status; house pediatrics separately (immature immunity, maternal antibody, likely unvaccinated).
  • Steam sterilisation — wrapped goods 250°F (121°C) ~15 min at 15 psi; unwrapped/flash 270°F (132°C) 3–4 min. Biological indicator = Geobacillus stearothermophilus (run weekly). Heat-sensitive items → hydrogen peroxide gas plasma.
  • Hand prep — alcohol-based rubs most effective > chlorhexidine > iodophors; brushless preferred; initial scrub ≥5 min, subsequent ≥2 min.

Non-Surgical Sterilisation

  • Products are ALL labelled for dogs only (off-label in cats). Neutersol / Esterilsol / Zeuterine = intratesticular zinc gluconate → testicular sclerosis, permanent azoospermia; testicles stay in situ so ID marking is required (Neutersol became unavailable 2005). Suprelorin = deslorelin (GnRH superagonist) implant, reversible (Australia 2004). Gonazon = EU-approved 2006 but never commercially released.
  • HPG axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal), 3 interference levels: hypothalamus/pituitary (GnRH gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists, progestins) · gonads (chemical castrants) · downstream oocyte (PZP porcine zona pellucida vaccine).
  • Chemical — zinc gluconate SC misdirection → necrotising reaction / scrotal ulcers, so chemical restraint is essential; cats have far higher rates of severe reactions & self-mutilation. Calcium chloride must be in a lipid vehicle (alcohol/lidocaine) for deep necrosis without sloughing.
  • Immunocontraceptives — PZP vaccine is females-only and completely ineffective in queens; GnRH vaccine works in horses/deer/elephants (annual booster) but is neither safe nor effective in dogs.
  • Population 40–50% rule — non-surgical contraception controls feral cats only if immigration/emigration are essentially closed AND >40–50% are treated per 6-month interval — both conditions required.
⚠ Trap: deslorelin has a "flare" — continuous non-pulsatile GnRH stimulation first STIMULATES for 1–3 wk (fertile estrus in females, ↑libido/aggression in males) before downregulating, so it is not immediate suppression. And ear-tipping is contraindicated for non-surgical contraception: it's a permanent mark but the implant wanes, so field teams wrongly assume permanent sterility (use microchip/tattoo/ear band).

Population Impact & TNR

  • A homelessness intervention — intact status is a leading relinquishment risk, so accessible/targeted surgery prevents both litters and surrender.
  • TNR / TNVR (trap-neuter-return / trap-neuter-vaccinate-return; a.k.a. return-to-field) minimum package = affordable HQ spay-neuter + FVRCP (feline core vaccine) + rabies where endemic + ID (ear tip + tattoo) + humane euthanasia for irredeemable suffering.
  • Sterilisation thresholds to stabilise a colony — rule of thumb ~70% sterilised; models ~75% of intact cats every 6 months (≈50% of intact adults per 6-mo interval), or ~80% annually / ~60% annual removal.
  • Free-roaming cat dynamics are more sensitive to survival than fecundity (adult survival most) → removal often outperforms equal-intensity sterilisation, and one untreated litter can undo intensive effort. Sterilising males only = essentially no change.
  • Targeted TNR works — a 2-yr campaign altering ~54% of cats cut shelter intake from the targeted zip code ~66% (vs ~12% elsewhere). Desexing reliably cuts cat intake/euthanasia, less so dogs.
  • Community-cat epidemiology — pregnancy is highly seasonal (peaks March/April), ~1–2 pregnancies/yr × 4–5 kittens; FeLV/FIV ~4% (FIV ~4× higher in males); retroviral false-positives reach ~50% in low-prevalence healthy cats → do NOT test-and-cull. Rabid cats outnumber rabid dogs in the US.

Stress & Low-Stress Handling

  • Fear activates the sympathetic nervous system (↑HR/RR/BP); fear-induced tachycardia raises peri-induction arrhythmia risk — stress is a welfare, safety AND anaesthetic-risk issue.
  • Signs span the 4 Fs (fight, flight, fret/fidget, freeze): tense body/face, ears back, dilated pupils, hissing or silence, refusing treats. Fear can imprint and is contagious between animals.
  • Tactics — acclimatisation, calm reception & short waits, consistent housing (same spot before/after), keep littermates together, warmth, secure footing, treats, pheromones, towel wraps; never drag with a slip lead. Community cats: keep in the covered trap, hands-off, move slowly.
  • Pre-visit anxiolytics — trazodone; gabapentin (cat 50–100 mg/cat).
⚠ Trap (cat safety): many gabapentin oral solutions contain xylitol (toxic to cats) — use xylitol-free compounded liquid or capsules (mix in tuna juice).

Repro Pathology & DSD

  • In all DSD (disorders of sexual development), gonadectomy is recommended. Sexual development = 3 steps: chromosomal sex → gonadal differentiation → phenotypic development (Sertoli cells → anti-Mullerian hormone regresses the female ducts; Leydig cells → testosterone).
  • Sex-chromosome DSD — XXY (Klinefelter, most common in dogs/cats), XO: underdeveloped genitalia, abnormal cycling/infertility.
  • Pseudohermaphrodite = gonads disagree with phenotype (female pseudohermaphrodite = ovaries + XX with masculinised genitalia). True hermaphrodite = at least one ovary AND one testis (or an ovotestis).
  • Persistent Mullerian duct syndrome — hereditary (Miniature Schnauzer in the US, Basset Hound in Europe); male external genitalia + male and female internal ducts, often cryptorchid.
  • Male calico/tortoiseshell cats — castrate if testes external; abdominal surgery only if cryptorchid. Uterus unicornis → hunt for the ipsilateral ovary to prevent ovarian remnant syndrome.
  • Feline mammary fibroadenomatous hyperplasia — benign progesterone-driven proliferation in young cycling queens or progestin-treated cats (firm glands, sometimes brown "milk") → flank spay recommended.
  • Hypospadias = abnormal ventral urethral opening from incomplete phenotypic masculinisation+

Pain Physiology, Thermoregulation & Pediatric Anaesthetic Risk +

  • Nociception = 4 steps — transduction → transmission → modulation → perception. 1° hyperalgesia is local; 2° spreads to uninjured tissue via central sensitisation (glutamate → NMDA receptor). Multimodal drug targets: opioids (μ/κ/δ; brain, cord, periphery) · NSAIDs (COX; periphery + cord) · local anaesthetics (Na+ channel; axons) · ketamine (NMDA antagonist; dorsal horn).
  • Thermoregulation — Phase 1: rapid core→periphery redistribution (vasodilation); Phase 2: slower linear loss (radiation/evaporation); Phase 3: plateau >3 h when production matches loss. Hypothermia → ↓drug clearance, platelet dysfunction, ↑blood loss, ↑infection; shivering raises O2 demand 200–300%.
  • Pediatric anaesthetic risk — cardiac output is rate-dependent, so bradycardia → immediate hypotension; O2 demand ~2–3× adult; tiny airways (resistance ∝ 1/r4); high hypoglycaemia risk. Warm fluids, minimise fasting, provide glucose.

HQHVSN — HPAI Biosecurity Framework

HPAI (highly pathogenic avian influenza) mitigation for HQHVSN clinics & free-roaming cat colonies — a 6-domain framework:

  • Facility layout & traffic flow — designated intake areas for feral cats / shelter animals / community pets with signage to prevent crossover; isolation & quarantine space away from high-traffic zones; controlled, limited-personnel access to feral holding.
  • Personnel hygiene & PPE (personal protective equipment) — mandatory handwashing + alcohol-based sanitiser; gloves, gowns, N95 masks during outbreaks; clinic-only scrubs & footwear to protect staff's own pets.
  • Cleaning & disinfection — written SOPs (standard operating procedures) for daily & between-patient cleaning; dedicated equipment per animal group to minimise fomite spread.
  • Patient management — grouped scheduling separating high-risk feral surgeries from routine; immediate isolation of cats with respiratory / neurological signs; plan ante- & post-mortem testing.
  • Public & staff education — train volunteers / caretakers on biosecurity; guide colony caregivers on safe feeding & monitoring for illness.
  • Monitoring & wildlife control — surveillance of colony cats + reporting of unexplained illness; rodent control & wildlife deterrents near colonies and clinics.
⚠ HPAI: isolate any feral/colony cat with respiratory or neurological signs and pursue testing — cats can acquire H5N1 from raw poultry / wild birds.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Integrated HPAI-mitigation biosecurity framework for HQHVSN clinics & cat colonies: a 6-domain protocol — facility layout/traffic flow, personnel hygiene & PPE, cleaning & disinfection SOPs, patient management (grouped scheduling + isolation), education, and monitoring/wildlife control — intended to reduce HPAI transmission risk to staff and patients in HQHVSN settings.
  • Gabapentin in shelter cats (OVH): pre-op gabapentin did NOT significantly reduce stress or pain or improve ease of handling perioperatively — limited evidence for its perioperative use in this setting.
  • HQHVSN anaesthetic/analgesic protocols (2017 US survey, 106 facilities): commonest canine premed = acepromazine + opioid (ace/hydromorphone 26%, ace/butorphanol 23%); canine induction = ketamine/midazolam (39%); commonest feline = total-IM DKT (dexmedetomidine/ketamine/butorphanol) or TTDex; most gave an injectable NSAID; only 40% used local anaesthetics; 86% satisfied with their protocol.
  • Local block in HQHVSN feline castration (RCT): an intratesticular lidocaine (± bupivacaine) block lowered the intraop heart-rate response and improved anaesthetic depth vs placebo, without more incisional complications — supports local blocks in HQHVSN multimodal protocols (~$1 per cat).
  • Perioperative hypothermia in HQHVSN & ambient temperature: perioperative inadvertent hypothermia (PIH) is common in spay-neuter populations despite short surgical times, and is linked to prolonged anaesthetic recovery (cats) and more postoperative pain (cats and dogs). Raising the ambient temperature in surgery and recovery areas may reduce it. PIH prolonged time to extubation by ~3.8 min, and each 1 °C (1.8 °F) drop in rectal temperature at extubation raised the pain score by ~0.51 units.
  • Sterilization identification — veterinary training & guideline compliance: only 5% of private practices tattooed all owned animals and 21% tattooed all unowned; 80% of shelters and 72% of spay/neuter clinics tattooed animals. Better training and broader implementation of sterilization-identification best practices (e.g. tattoos) are essential.
  • 'Find My Cat' — AirTags to map feral-cat home ranges (St. Kitts): limited success from inconsistent location updates — too few Apple devices in range for the AirTags to ping off, and feral cats defeated the quick-release mechanism. Sex and reproductive status influenced feral-cat home ranges.
  • When goals align — targeted HV-TN(V)R service-learning (Cornell + local SPCA): daily surgical capacity rose by ~3 procedures; students gained confidence & perceived professional readiness. The target community saw a significant fall in unweaned kittens and a 3-fold reduction in euthanasia; a small rise in intake from the neighbourhood reflected better alignment between community need and shelter access. Residents reported improved cat welfare, fewer kitten births/deaths, less fighting/injury/property damage, and renewed trust in the SPCA.
  • ASV critique of the 2024 WSAVA repro-control guidelines: ASV raised concerns about specialty-care bias, weak critical reading of the literature, unsupportive citations, promotion of untested/high-cost practices, a controversial blanket recommendation not to spay large dogs, bias against low-income owners, and too little emphasis on the health harms of reproduction & overpopulation.
  • CNVR & free-roaming dog health (Jamshedpur, India, 2014–2018): bi-annual street surveys + clinic data showed the probability of dogs presenting with mange, TVT (transmissible venereal tumour) and pregnancy fell significantly. The sterilised proportion rose (higher at CNVR-treated sites), and the probability of observing lactating females fell in treated sites but rose in untreated ones.
  • Gonadal-hormone exposure & cranial cruciate ligament disease (CCLD; GAM of 20,590 dogs, 1988–2023): CCLD prevalence 2.49% female / 1.89% male; risk was nonlinearly tied to cumulative gonadal-hormone exposure — highest in dogs with the LEAST exposure, dropping to a minimum at ~1054 days (females) / ~805 days (males). "Early" gonadectomy provisionally = before ~2.9 y (female) / 2.2 y (male).
  • Falling prealtered-intake proportion, US shelters post-pandemic: prealtered dogs reached adoption with a shorter median LOS than unaltered dogs. Higher odds of being prealtered in senior and male dogs; lower in juveniles and strays. The prealtered share at intake fell year-over-year post-pandemic (may lengthen time in care) — orgs should record prealter status at intake to forecast capacity and track access to sterilisation services.
  • 4 years of TNR (Córdoba, Spain): overall population decline 2.68%; modelling suggests it prevented ~70% of the growth that would otherwise have occurred. Cost ~€0.62/resident/yr; viability analysis projects up to ~55% reduction by 2028 with sustained implementation — a scalable urban model.
  • Gonadectomy status/age & overweight-obesity across 15 dog breeds (primary-care cohort): Pugs, Golden Retrievers & Labradors had the highest underlying susceptibility. In toy/small breeds, gonadectomy at 3–6 mo carried hazard ratios ≤ or no different from gonadectomy at ≥1 yr — early gonadectomy still offers individual & population benefit, but OvOb risk is not uniform across breeds.
  • Gonadectomy & age at gonadectomy & IVDH in French Bulldogs: dogs desexed <12 mo had significantly higher intervertebral-disc-herniation risk than intact dogs, with early-desexed females showing the strongest association — a possible influence of neuter timing on IVDH in this breed.
  • Free-roaming cat management — resident attitudes (Slovakia): 276 respondents (89% women, 61% aged 31–50, 52% involved <5 yr). Strong interest in further education, especially TNR planning & handling cats unsuitable for rehoming; finances were the main barrier. Recommends volunteer training in cat biology, data collection, impact assessment & financial planning.
  • Free-roaming dog control by surgical sterilisation (RCT): one-off, intensive campaigns in open populations need heavy investment and are unlikely to reduce population size on their own, though they may reduce problem behaviours and improve welfare.
  • Dog population-control modelling (Thailand): sterilisation targeting female indoor, outdoor and stray dogs could cut the total dog population by >50% over 5 yr — outperforming the current intervention.
  • Public perceptions of free-roaming dogs/cats (India vs US): Indian participants largely saw them as community members and more supported free roaming; Americans more often cited welfare, public-health & wildlife risks and favoured adoption/euthanasia, whereas Indians preferred sterilise-vaccinate-release.
  • Feral-cat TNR + long-term monitoring (remote Hong Kong island): from >100 cats near restaurants/walkways down to a single cat sighted 2019–2023 — a successful Asian island population-control case study.
  • Territorially stratified modelling (Spain, national): a scalable, data-driven framework for coordinated TNR across administrative levels and urban/rural contexts, incorporating One Health, digital monitoring & standardised welfare protocols.
  • CNVR & resident attitudes (Greater Bangkok, 2016–2023): ~400,000 dogs CNVR'd; declines in roaming-dog numbers, puppies born, and residents being troubled by roaming dogs; the highest-CNVR areas had lower odds of reported dog bites. Owned dogs contributed substantially to the roaming population and must be included in management.
  • Pandemic's cruel aftermath — declining S/N capacity: S/N surgery numbers fell sharply through COVID and kept declining, impeding access to basic preventive care and threatening decades of progress against pet overpopulation.
  • TNR/RTF at Florida shelters (survey): more common in shelters with higher cat intake & higher live-outcome rates; rural shelters had higher euthanasia. Services usually include rabies (100%), FVRCP (80–88%), FeLV/FIV testing (~41–44%), parasite control & treatment of illness/injury (>90%); expanding shelter + community programs and urban–rural collaboration can cut unnecessary admission/euthanasia.
  • Spaycations — HQHVSN vets on remote-area volunteerism: ~70% had done a spaycation, motivated by making a difference and by travel/culture; pitfalls include volunteer pressure, high cost, unevaluated clinic efficacy, and risk of colonialism/savior attitudes.
  • Barbed vs smooth monofilament suture, 3-layer continuous OVH closure (HQHVSN RCT): barbed suture was more efficient than smooth monofilament, with a similar short-term (telemedicine-assessed) complication rate — useful for high-volume closures.
  • Age-of-neuter decision guide (Pointer, Mastiff, Newfoundland, Rhodesian Ridgeback, Siberian Husky): breed-specific — Pointers (both sexes) ↑joint disorders + cancers; male Mastiffs ↑CCL & lymphosarcoma; female Newfoundlands ↑joint disorders; female Ridgebacks ↑MCT with very early neuter; Huskies no significant joint/cancer effect. Vulnerability varies widely by breed.
  • Cat caring behaviours & ownership status in a free-sterilisation program (Australia): ~33% of shelter/pound cats are euthanised yearly; of 1,094 respondents most cats were <12 mo DSH acquired informally, with limited vet care (15% ever seen a vet, 28% vaccinated). Before the program 88.5% were owners, 11.5% semi-owners; 93% of semi-owners transitioned to ownership driven by responsibility, attachment & free sterilisation access.
  • Ear-tipping techniques (RCT): no single method (Mayo scissors, wood-burning tool, scalpel blade × styptic gel/paste/hemostat) was best on all measures; WBT+hemostat and MS+hemostat led on the primary outcome, but scalpel-blade + hemostat/paste was unanimously preferred by tippers.
  • Neuter timing relative to puberty — female dogs (scoping review): robust evidence is lacking; the impact of neutering before vs after puberty on atopy, developmental orthopaedic disease, neoplasia, obesity & urogenital disease in female dogs remains unclear.
  • TNR + trap-neuter-adopt colony management (Federal District, Brazil): 47.8% colony reduction, 98.8% sterilised; adoption was key — curbed growth, prevented kitten births, strengthened caregiver bonds and protected against death/disappearance/abandonment.
  • Midline vs flank ovariectomy nociception (feral/stray cats): midline had a larger incision & longer surgery; no difference in intra-op nociception. No nociceptive reason to prefer either, but the flank approach's shorter time & smaller incision may let more animals be sterilised and reduce dehiscence risk.
  • Later- vs early-age neuter & early-onset urinary incontinence (VetCompass target-trial emulation): later-age neutering causes lower odds of early-onset UI; prefer delaying neuter until after 7 months unless major reasons justify earlier surgery.
  • Turkish vets on terminating unwanted canine pregnancies: little ethical debate — opinion was generally that termination is acceptable in these cases.
  • Readability of post-op discharge instructions & complications: only 2% of owner-directed discharge instructions met human-guideline readability levels; poorer readability was associated with a higher complication rate.
  • Integrating community cats into urban governance (China): social norms & trust activate community social capital, building a care network centred on community-cat care.
  • PDS suture-size tensile testing, feline midline coeliotomy closure: failure risk rose as diameter fell; PDS 2-0 & 3-0 safe without reservation; 4-0 higher breakage risk (use with caution); 5-0 not recommended for this closure.
  • Feline ovarian pedicle tie — vet use & sentiment (SOC survey): users mostly learned it at an HQHVSN facility or from a mentor (median 5 yr use; 99% comfortable teaching it). Among non-users, 60% felt it meets standard of care, 26% "sometimes", 14% "no"; absence from the curriculum was the top reason for not using it.
  • 'Working Cat Program' (5 yr, 968 cats): 99% live-release — 90% "hired" into non-traditional homes, 9.9% transferred, 2 returned to origin; 85% of responding adopters were very/completely satisfied. Working-cat programs raise live-release for unrelocatable cats.
  • Who feeds community cats (7 US communities): ~30% of people feed community cats (mostly ≤3 cats); Black & Hispanic respondents more likely to feed than White; pet owners more likely to feed — communities have high capacity to care for community cats.
  • Attachment to community cats transcends ownership: of 329 respondents, 90.2% had recently provided food/water/shelter to community cats; caregivers (mostly white, female, middle-class) showed strong attachment reflected in monthly spending & personal sacrifice.
  • Canine surgical-neutering education programme (India): a 12-day S/N skills course raised knowledge scores; women scored significantly higher overall; those aged 25–34 scored lowest; among postgraduates, scores rose with age.
  • Laparoscopic-assisted ovary-sparing hysterectomy (dogs): a safe, reliable sterilisation with no increased stump-pyometra risk; counsel owners about retained gonadal hormones & continued heat cycles.
  • Capturing free-roaming dogs for sterilisation (multi-site, Goa): as coverage rises, catching remaining intact dogs gets harder and unit cost escalates — target low-coverage areas first and adapt capture strategy as coverage improves.
  • Empirical cat population-dynamics model (UK): multistate matrix model — neutering owned cats influences all subpopulations; neutering owned cats younger reduces overall growth regardless of overall neuter prevalence; growth is most sensitive to owned-cat survival & fecundity (owned > stray > feral > shelter influence).
  • Characteristics of cat semi-owners (NSW): of 8,708 residents, 588 were semi-owners of unowned cats — more likely female, urban, lower-socioeconomic & renting; most also owned cats and had more cats than non-semi-owners.
  • Growth in neutered vs intact DSH kittens: growth was similar before neutering, but neutered kittens were heavier by 52 wk with more fat & lean mass and larger abdominal girth/rib cage — monitor bodyweight closely after neutering (especially early-neutered females) to prevent excess weight gain.
  • Desexing & sexually dimorphic dog behaviours: urine marking & mounting were most frequent in intact males (significant vs desexed males); roaming was similar across sex & neuter status; in intact males, mounting & roaming declined with age.
  • Peripheral warming during feline OVH (multi-arm RCT): active heated toddler socks slowed the rate of rectal-temperature fall vs passive (cotton socks) or uncovered controls; cotton socks alone did not help; final-temperature gain was modest.
  • Feline ovarian pedicle ligation teaching (AAVMC schools): of 35 responding schools, 97.1% teach double ligation, 48.6% teach autoligation (pedicle tie), 45.7% teach both; only one pedicle-tie school did not also teach double ligation.
  • Indoor vs outdoor cat management & welfare: ~51% indoor management — associated with more varied/frequent care (combing, claw-trimming, toys) and a more positive cat–caretaker relationship; outdoor management (~49%) had less care variety and a less positive relationship.
  • OVH at the time of C-section (125 bitches, retrospective): concurrent OVH did not significantly increase mortality, intra-/post-op complications or reduce mothering ability; C-section+OVH had slightly longer surgery & delivery-to-nursing time (not significant).
  • Public opinion on stray cats (China, social-media analysis): concerns centred on irresponsible feeding; views on ecological impact were mixed; TNR had high public support; greater awareness of ecological impact correlated with more support for lethal control. Educate feeders on the downsides of irresponsible feeding & raise ecological awareness.
  • Seasonal movement of urban cats (Cape Town, n=23): home ranges were significantly larger in summer than winter, with cats venturing further from home in summer — relevant to urban-edge wildlife management.
  • Solutions-based / One-Welfare urban cat management (semi-owners): caregivers had strong emotional bonds and reported improved QOL & better cat welfare after sterilisation via an assistive-centred program, but viewed agencies (e.g. councils) negatively — recommends councils/agencies prioritise an assistive-centred approach.
  • Free-roaming cats & wildlife activity overlap (Greater Sydney, camera traps): peak cat activity overlapped temporally with bandicoots, possums & small mammals — supports containment to reduce predation risk.
  • Lethal, enforcement-centred cat culling & carer wellbeing (Port of Newcastle): strong carer–cat bonds; lethal management caused negative psychological-health & QOL impacts on carers — argues for a care-centred approach.
  • Pediatric-neutering outcomes (retrospective, 105 owners, Coimbra shelter): no statistically significant association between neuter age and obesity, behaviour change, or urinary/joint disease.
  • Veterinarians' role in community-cat management (Europe/Spain): vets should collaborate with public administrations on long-term sustainable solutions to cat overpopulation, with greater awareness of sterilisation & ID to prevent abandonment; spans biodiversity, zoonoses & TNR+adoption effectiveness.
  • National dog population-management program (Chile, review): free vet services + sterilisation were the mainstay, plus education & mandatory owner ID; dogs-per-person fell as human density rose. Recommends baseline data first, local (not countrywide) population estimates, replacing free with low-cost sterilisation, and sustainable education + enforcement.
  • Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) for ovarian remnant syndrome in queens: AMH was significantly higher than spayed and lower than intact cats — a useful ORS diagnostic; highest AMH with cystic ovarian remnants; one cat with low AMH had concurrently high progesterone.
  • Community attitudes to free-roaming dogs (Goa, India): 37% fed FRDs (dog owners & Hindus most likely); most saw them as a menace/nuisance/scary yet also vulnerable, belonging in communities, with a right to street life; barking was the top complaint; preferred solution was impoundment in shelters.
  • Community Dog Program (5 municipalities, Paraná, Brazil): a 5-phase model (planning → animal screening → registration of dogs & caretakers → veterinary procedures → final meeting) proved applicable across all five municipalities for population management.
  • Ear-tipping practices for TNR cats (US survey): nearly all recognised the ear tip as sterilisation (some confused it with rabies vaccination/socialisation); most tipped the LEFT ear (81%) by visual estimation (92%), preferring a straight-across silhouette; median post-hemostasis bleeding 5%; commonest complaint was removing excessive tissue.
  • Elective OVH/castration despite mild-moderate presurgical leukocytosis (shelter dogs): 45.6% had leukocytosis (higher WBC/lymphocytes in <3-mo pups); infection was not associated with leukocytosis and all surgeries proceeded safely. Reserve CBC for ill-appearing dogs (not screening) and use age-specific reference intervals.
  • Unowned free-ranging cat population estimate (Denmark, questionnaire + GPS): ~89,000 ± 11,000 unowned free-ranging cats, mean density ~2/km², mostly rural; ~⅓ socialised, ⅔ unsocialised.
