Stress

Stress · Behavioral Health (16%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield stress cheatsheet — SAM/HPA cascades, cortisol traps, FAS, first-week rule, feline stress, long-stay deterioration. Pre-filled points marked +.

Stress Physiology

  • SAM vs HPA cascades — SAM (sympathetic-adrenal-medullary) fires in seconds → epinephrine + norepinephrine from adrenal medulla; HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) over minutes → CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone) + AVP (arginine vasopressin) → ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) → cortisol from adrenal cortex+
  • Cortisol kinetics — peaks 20–30 min post-stressor, stays elevated ≥90 min.
  • SAM output (seconds) — epi + norepi → pupil dilation, ↑HR, ↑RR; acute stress enhances memory/sensory function.

Chronic HPA Dysregulation

  • Low cortisol ≠ low stress — chronic stress → adrenal hypertrophy + ↑ glucocorticoids but ACTH unchanged; a normal/low resting cortisol in a long-stay resident can signal a dysregulated HPA axis, not a relaxed animal+
  • ACTH / LHRH stim tests (luteinising-hormone-releasing-hormone) — ↑HPA responsiveness + ↓pituitary sensitivity = chronic-stress HPA dysfunction.
  • Cortisol caveats — signals only stress activation, not type (good/tolerable/toxic); confounded by collection time, neuter status, sampling method.
  • Still reactive — dysregulated long-stay dogs remain responsive to acute stressors (restraint, venipuncture) despite blunted resting cortisol.

Systemic Effects of Stress

  • Behaviour & cognition — ↑maladaptive behaviour + aggression, impairs social behaviour/memory/learning; chronic → structural brain change.
  • HPA systemic effects — ↑energy + O₂, ↓flow to non-critical tissue; inhibits digestion, growth, immunity, reproduction, pain perception.
  • Net cost — ↑disease susceptibility, slowed healing, shortened lifespan.

Stress Taxonomy & Dynamics

  • Acute vs chronic — low–moderate acute stress is beneficial (aids coping); extreme acute or chronic stress harms physical + emotional health+
  • Categories — good (copes, no harm) · tolerable (distressing but copes) · toxic (exceeds coping → lasting harm).
  • Types — physical (pain, hunger, temp, disease) vs psychological (novelty, isolation, unpredictability, chronic frustration).
  • Trigger stacking — stressors accumulate with no return to baseline → amplified response/explosion; context learning locks the reaction to that setting.
  • Maternal / early-life — gestational maternal stress alters pup behaviour → learning impairment + easier conditioning of fear (esp. auditory).
Trap Most potent psychological stressors = novelty, withholding of an expected reward, and anticipation of punishment.

The First-Week Rule

  • Stress highest wk 1 — defer non-essential stressful procedures to allow adaptation + relationship-building.
  • Relinquished vs stray — owner-relinquished = intense psychological stress, no adaptation; strays/returns = lower baseline; prior kennelling ↓ activated response.
  • Petting >15 min/day — no change in relinquished dogs, but ↓ stress in strays.
  • Translocation stress — 4 routes: capture · captivity · transport · release.

Measuring & Identifying Stress

  • Cortisol kinetics + leukogram — cortisol peaks 20–30 min, elevated 90+ min; stress leukogram = lymphopenia + neutrophilia+
  • FAS scale (Fear, Anxiety, and Stress) — rapid clinical scoring to identify and grade stress at the cage-side+
  • Sampling matrix — blood/saliva = momentary (confounded by handling/venipuncture); urine/faeces/hair = long-term, non-invasive, for welfare + housing audits.
Trap To audit housing over months use faecal/hair cortisol metabolites; blood/saliva cortisol is a diagnostic error (acute collection stress).

Feline Stress

  • Feigned sleep · FURI · sickness behaviour — feigned sleep is a coping mechanism, not relaxation; high stress score links to high FURI (feline upper respiratory infection) rates; watch for sickness behaviour (inappetence, hiding)+
  • Five pillars of feline welfare — safe place · separated resources · play/predation · consistent human contact · respect for smell
  • ~4% stay high-stress — most cats settle within 2 wks; ~4% remain highly stressed → may be unsuited to shelter housing.
  • Feral floor space — same minimum as friendly cats: 11 sq ft (1 sq m) short-term (may only use hides at night).
Trap Feigned sleep = coping, NOT relaxation ("cat sleeping/relaxed" is an anthropomorphic error). A trapped tame cat is indistinguishable from feral — let it calm before assessing personality.

Adaptation, Deterioration & Management

  • Three states to distinguish — sensitisation (↑ activity), habituation (↑ or ↓ activity), and depression-like affective state (wakeful inactivity, loss of reward responsiveness)+
  • Adaptation timeline — cortisol falls days 1–3 (regaining homeostasis); by day 6 dogs spend more time at back of kennel/less active (may = comfort).
  • Deterioration signs — barrier aggression, ↑arousal, worsening reactivity, ↑fear, abnormal behaviour; escape behaviours (door-scratch, floor-dig, whine) rise with LOS.
  • When stress found — stop & reassess: cause? options? procedure truly necessary now? Weigh need vs want.

Long-Term Stays

  • Keep LOS short (length of stay) — stays >a few days require daily enrichment; chronic stress ↓coping, ↑fear/anxiety/frustration, jeopardises placement.
  • Options — enrichment, added space/choice, foster; spay-neuter long-stays (reproductive stress → spraying, marking, fighting).
  • Group housing — for pro-social dogs (behaviour stabilises in 1–2 days); very fearful dogs rarely acclimate → high deterioration risk.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Age-related changes in gut health and behavioural biomarkers in a Beagle population: Faecalibacterium was relatively more abundant in senior dogs and Blautia in junior dogs. Faecal short-chain fatty acids (especially acetate) rose with age, while propionate was higher in juniors. Blood thyroxine, playing, exploring and total activity were higher in junior dogs — age must be considered when studying gut microbiome and behaviour.
  • Behavioural factors and recurrence in cats with feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC): fear of unfamiliar people was significantly associated with recurrence: 94% of cats with recurrences were reported fearful of strangers vs 59% of single-episode cats; fear of familiar people also trended higher in the recurrence group. No difference for aggression toward humans/animals, being a victim of aggression, urine marking, or owner compliance with environmental modification.
  • Species-relevant auditory stimuli and stress in cats in a novel environment: 20 cats received 4 sound treatments over 28 days: silence, cat purring, cat eating sounds, or purring + eating mixed. Only the mixed sound reduced visual scanning, with no significant effect on other behaviours.
  • A brief positive experience and dogs’ performance and stress resilience during a learning task: experimental dogs had a 15-min positive session (long-leash walk, human interaction, exploration, play, scent foraging); controls were kept on leash in an office. Contrary to prediction, the positive experience did not affect learning and left dogs more stressed in the Disruption phase, with more time near owners (reassurance-seeking).
  • Shelter dog outcomes using behavioural and biological stress markers: at entry, dogs returned to owners panted more, and euthanised or transferred dogs sat less than adopted dogs. After 1 week, human-directed behaviours predicted outcome: adopted/transferred dogs stood with front paws on the cage and wagged tails more than euthanised dogs. Cortisol was higher in adopted than transferred dogs at baseline but not at week 1; immunoglobulin A did not correlate with outcome.
  • Physiological and behavioural evaluation of shelter dogs during routine veterinary health checks: oxytocin correlated negatively with body temperature (possible role in modulating stress-induced hyperthermia). Tryptophan, serotonin, interleukin-6 and other physiological measures showed no association with behaviour scores. Oxytocin may buffer the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and behavioural assessment may be a more sensitive stress measure than hormonal/physiological parameters alone.
  • Behavioural, physiological and pathological approaches to cortisol in dogs (review): compares invasive (blood, saliva, urine) and non-invasive (hair, faecal) cortisol assays for accuracy and practicality; covers environmental influences on cortisol (shelter conditions, human interaction, music) and the clinical/behavioural implications of abnormal cortisol, e.g. Cushing’s disease and stress-related behaviour problems.
  • Cardiovascular effects of dog-appeasing pheromone in the hospital environment: no difference in heart rate variability over time between substances — diffuser-delivered dog-appeasing pheromone did not significantly change stress-related cardiovascular parameters in hospitalised dogs.
  • Nail cortisol for welfare assessment in shelter and owned cats: in Thailand and Vietnam, nail cortisol concentration (NCC) was significantly higher in cats in unfavourable shelters than in favourable shelters or owned cats, with no sex difference. NCC correlated positively with dog exposure and negatively with space allowance, number of litter boxes, free-roaming time, air conditioning, and enrichment (hiding places, elevated areas, outdoor views, scratching structures, toys) — better living conditions improved welfare.
  • Hair cortisol in cats with and without behavioural problems: outdoor cats had lower cortisol than indoor cats, and undesirable behaviour was more frequent in indoor cats. Cortisol was significantly higher in cats that eliminated outside the litter box or were aggressive toward owners; high stress can impair interactions with other cats and humans.
  • Sensor-supported measurement of shelter adaptability in dogs: nocturnal activity and behaviour: shelter dogs shook their bodies less on the first night, and paw lifting decreased over days. They lost body weight by day 12 and had disrupted nocturnal resting patterns and urinary cortisol:creatinine ratios (UCCR) vs pet dogs, partly adapting after ~2 weeks. Age and sex affected few behaviours.
  • A friend in prison: human–animal bond, stress and self-esteem of detained juveniles with Dutch cell dogs: the human–animal bond did not predict improvements in detained juveniles’ stress or self-esteem.
  • Buccal micronucleus assay for stress-associated genomic damage in shelter dogs and cats: tested whether a stressful environment (e.g. a shelter) increases genomic damage; found evidence of a possible correlation between physiological stress and increased genomic damage in sheltered cats and dogs.
  • Serotonin and tryptophan serum concentrations in shelter dogs undergoing a stressful procedure: serum serotonin was not associated with a dog’s behavioural response to a stressful situation, nor with serum concentration of its precursor, tryptophan.
  • Dog behaviours in veterinary consultations, Part 1: owner presence or absence: with the owner present, dogs were more willing to enter the consultation room and appeared more relaxed while exploring; during examination dogs looked toward the owner whether present or behind the door. Allowing owners to stay in the room is better for canine welfare.
  • Dog behaviours in veterinary consultations, Part 2: relationship between dog and owner behaviours: owners’ negative behaviours during an exam intensified their dogs’ anxiety signs; visual and verbal attempts to comfort had no significant effect. The more stress behaviours dogs showed, the more eye contact they made with owners — social referencing or reassurance-seeking.
  • Dog–owner relationship, owner interpretations and dog personality in dogs’ emotional reactivity: both negative and positive situations provoked heightened arousal. Emotional reactivity and the dog–owner relationship modulate each other, depending on the aspect of the relationship and the dog’s individual responsiveness.
  • Effect of music on stress parameters in dogs during a mock veterinary visit: dogs were scored more “afraid” during the physical exam than in the hospital kennel. Salivary cortisol, immunoglobulin A and infrared temperature rose from baseline to post-kennel and post-exam, with no music effect. Core body temperature and odds of ‘relaxed’ lips were lower with music than control.
  • Shelter dog activity before and during COVID-19 using automated analysis: afternoon activity was lower before COVID-19 restrictions, while during restrictions activity stayed at a constant average — lockdown altered in-kennel activity (likely via shelter environment changes), and automated analysis is a hands-off monitoring tool.
  • Extreme life histories, social behaviour and cortisol in shelter dogs: dogs from known early adverse backgrounds had higher salivary cortisol than other shelter dogs, unrelated to time in adverse conditions or in the shelter. With an unfamiliar human they showed more fear, less affiliative behaviour and more time stationary (sitting, standing, freezing) — so much that a food-based social-cognitive task could not be completed.
  • Molecular biomarkers of human and dog stress during canine-assisted interventions (scoping review): canine-assisted interventions consistently improved human stress markers and typically did not harm canine stress markers, but results were inconsistent across measures (e.g. cortisol improved with no change in self-reported stress, or vice versa).
  • Urinary cortisol and oxytocin in domestic cats: factors affecting them: owners’ frequency of tactile and auditory communication correlated positively with urinary oxytocin, and was higher in younger cats or those with shorter cohabitation. No factors associated with urinary cortisol were identified.
  • Scaredy-cat: individual differences in response to an acute everyday stressor across development in cats: vocalisation is a good indicator of individual differences in stress responses to everyday procedures (still needs physiological validation); facial thermography was not sufficiently reliable.
  • Faecal cortisol for enrolling free-roaming dogs in Singapore into a rehabilitation-rehoming programme (allostasis): faecal cortisol metabolite (FCM) levels of recently sheltered dogs were higher than pet dogs and kept rising, i.e. not yet adapted; the difference was moderated by sex. Unexpectedly, dogs with a good prognosis had higher FCM than those with a poor prognosis, with sex and sample state as important covariates.
  • Behavioural correlates of urinary output in shelter cats: urinary suppression (measured by litter clump weights) may be a practical way to track wellbeing in shelter cats, and a support vector machine (SVM) had modest ability to classify cats at risk of suppressed urine output from behavioural responses to a brief test.
  • Characteristics and welfare of long-term shelter dogs: long-term (LT) dogs were more often a “dangerous breed”, male and older at admission; rated higher for aggression and high arousal and lower for amicability, with more aggression signs toward an unfamiliar human but no welfare-assessment difference. They rested head-up more, and before feeding stood, vocalised and yawned more — easily aroused and struggle to relax.
  • Cognitive and composite behavioural welfare assessments of pet cats aged 9–22 months in single- vs multi-cat households: cats from single-cat homes may be more likely to show acute stress than multi-cat cats. Alternatives: lower composite stress scores in multi-cat cats may reflect ‘relief’ from separation for the test, or inactivity linked to negative affect.
  • Cortisol of shelter dogs in animal-assisted interventions in a prison: baseline cortisol fell significantly by the end of the programme, suggesting human activities in a different environment improve kennelled dogs’ welfare. Transportation significantly raised cortisol — a critical phase needing particular care.
  • Hair and nail cortisol and chronic-stress indicators in cats: cats with litter box issues had significantly higher hair (HCC) and nail (NCC) cortisol. Cats with groomed coats had lower HCC; cats with dander and mats had higher NCC.
  • How to evaluate and manage stress in dogs — a guide for veterinary specialists: no universal quantitative or qualitative stress indicators, no clear cortisol reference intervals, and no accepted protocols for preventing/correcting stress. Most reliable approach: behaviour during the appointment + detailed history + an appropriate combination of physiological markers.
  • Restless nights? Nocturnal activity as an indicator of shelter dog adaptability: shelter dogs were more active at night on nights 1–2 than night 12, with large individual variation; pet dogs at home were more active on nights 1 and 12. UCCR was higher shortly after intake than after 12 days of habituation or after adoption, and small dogs scored higher than large dogs for both. Accelerometer-measured nocturnal activity is a valid, cost-effective adaptability indicator.
  • Owner presence and scent on stress resilience in cats: cats showed fewer stress-related behaviours with the owner present (supporting a secure-base effect), but not when alone with the owner’s scent object.
  • Urinary hormonal state of cats and social interaction with humans: urinary cortisol and oxytocin were significantly higher in the non-social than the social condition, and oxytocin correlated with cortisol in the non-social condition. Urinary oxytocin can be quantified in cats, and cats appear to value social interaction with humans.
