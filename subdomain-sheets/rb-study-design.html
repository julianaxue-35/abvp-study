Study Design

Study Design · Research & Biostats (6%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield summary of the Study Design subdomain — designs, statistical inference, bias & validity, and the measure each design yields; every concept in plain English with a worked example or memory hook. Pre-filled points marked +.

Study Designs

  • Direction of reasoning — cohort reasons forward (exposure → outcome); case-control reasons backward (outcome → exposure); cross-sectional captures both at one instant.+
  • Three families — descriptive (who/what/where/when; no comparison group, so only generates hypotheses); analytic observational (adds a comparison group but the investigator only observes — exposure is not assigned); experimental (investigator assigns the intervention — the only family that shows causation directly).
  • Descriptive ladder — case report (single animal, flags the rare, no denominator) → case series (a cluster, still no comparison) → cross-sectional / prevalence survey (fast, gives prevalence, can't show exposure came first) → ecological (group-level correlation from aggregate data).
  • RCT — investigator randomly allocates subjects to intervention vs control; randomisation balances known AND unknown confounders (its source of causal strength). Blinding (single/double) cuts observer & placebo effects; intention-to-treat = analyse each subject in the group they were assigned, whether or not they completed.
Trap — ecological fallacy: a group-level association does NOT prove the same link holds in individuals. A case report or series can never prove a risk factor because it has no comparison group.

Analytic observational studies

  • Design → measure — cohort yields incidence & relative risk (RR); case-control yields the odds ratio (OR) and suits rare or slow diseases. Incidence = new cases over a period; prevalence = existing cases at one snapshot.+
  • Cohort (exposure → outcome, forward) — follows exposed vs unexposed over time; gives incidence + RR; establishes temporality (exposure clearly precedes disease); good for common or multiple outcomes; weak point = loss to follow-up, inefficient for rare disease. Hook: a cohort counts new cases.
  • Case-control (outcome → exposure, backward) — starts with the diseased (cases) vs healthy (controls) and looks back for exposure; gives the OR; efficient for rare or long-latency disease; weak point = recall & selection bias. OR ≈ RR only when the disease is rare.
  • Analytic cross-sectional — exposure and outcome measured together at one instant; gives prevalence / prevalence ratio; cannot order events, so reverse causation is possible.
  • Measures by design — RR (cohort/experimental, null = 1); OR (case-control, null = 1); prevalence ratio (cross-sectional); attributable risk / risk difference = absolute excess risk in the exposed (cohort/experimental).
Trap — a case-control study CANNOT give incidence or prevalence: it starts from the outcome and has no population-at-risk denominator, so it yields an odds ratio only.

Longitudinal designs & repeated measurement

  • Panel vs repeated cross-sectional — a panel follows the same individuals each round → measures within-individual change (weak point: attrition). A repeated cross-sectional survey draws a fresh sample each round → measures net population change only. Flat prevalence may mean nobody changed, or that the affected ones left and were replaced.+
  • Prospective / retrospective / ambidirectional — describes the investigator's vantage point in time, not the direction of reasoning. Retrospective = cohort assembled from existing records; ambidirectional = historical records plus continued forward follow-up.+
  • Repeated measures — same animal measured repeatedly → observations are correlated. Treating them as independent is pseudoreplication (inflates n, shrinks standard errors, manufactures significance). 2 time points → paired t-test / Wilcoxon signed-rank; 3+ → repeated-measures ANOVA / Friedman; general → mixed-effects model with a random effect for animal (tolerates missing time points).+
  • Age / period / cohort — age = changes because animals get older · period = affects everyone in that calendar window · cohort = specific to the group that entered together and travels with them (e.g. pandemic-surge puppies). Entangled: cohort = period − age, so all three can't be estimated at once.+
  • Time series & ITS — features: trend, seasonality (kitten season), autocorrelation. Interrupted time series (ITS) models the pre-intervention trend, projects it forward, and tests for a change in level and in slope — the design for evaluating a programme you couldn't randomise.+
  • Cluster randomisation — randomise groups (wards, foster homes, shelters) when the intervention is group-level or contamination is unavoidable. Inflate sample size by the design effect = 1 + (mean cluster size − 1) × ICC (intracluster correlation coefficient). Stepped wedge = every cluster crosses over eventually, in randomised order.+
Trap — a retrospective cohort is still a cohort: it reasons forward from exposure and yields incidence & RR, so it is not a case-control study. Separately, analysing a cluster-randomised trial as if animals had been individually randomised is the unit of analysis error — it understates standard errors and manufactures significance.

Hierarchy of evidence and EBVM

  • Strength ladder — systematic review / RCT (randomised controlled trial) > cohort > case-control > case series / report > expert opinion.+
  • Why that order — higher tiers control confounding better: an RCT randomises it away, a systematic review / meta-analysis pools many studies. The ladder applies to therapy & causation questions.
  • Off the ladder — laboratory, in vitro and challenge studies in purpose-bred animals aren't a rung at all. They answer mechanism / efficacy under controlled conditions, trading external validity for internal validity. Human pyramids put animal work at the base because it's a step from the human patient; in vet med the species is the target — the limit is the artificial conditions.+
  • Ranks designs, not studies — a large, well-run cohort beats a small unblinded RCT with 40% loss to follow-up. Design is the starting point, conduct decides where a study lands. GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) formalises this: rate down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias; rate up for large effect, dose-response, or confounding that would have worked against the observed effect.+
  • Review types — narrative (informal search, no appraisal) · scoping (systematic search, maps gaps, deliberately doesn't grade quality) · systematic (prespecified reproducible protocol + formal risk-of-bias) · meta-analysis (systematic review plus statistical pooling). Thoroughness alone doesn't make a review systematic — the prespecified protocol does.+
  • SR / MA machinery — PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) frames the question · PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram accounts for every record · I² quantifies heterogeneity · forest plot = per-study estimate (box size = weight) + CI, diamond = pooled · funnel plot asymmetry ⇒ publication bias, which inflates the pooled estimate.+
  • EBVM — five A's — Ask, Acquire, Appraise, Apply, Assess.
  • Exam skill — some items are drawn from journal abstracts (2021–2025 lookback). Practise reading an abstract to name the design, the measure reported, and the main limitation.

Hypothesis testing

  • p-value — the probability of data this extreme if the null hypothesis were true; it is NOT the probability the null is true, nor "the chance the result happened randomly".+
  • H0 vs H1 — null (H0) = no difference/association (e.g. RR = 1); alternative (H1) = a difference exists. You reject H0 when the data are unlikely enough under it.
  • alpha (α) — the significance cut-off set before looking (usually 0.05); it is the Type I / false-positive rate you agree to tolerate.
  • Statistical vs clinical — with a big enough sample even a trivial, meaningless difference can reach p<0.05; always judge the effect size, not just the p-value.
Trap — p<0.05 means statistically significant, NOT clinically important. A significant p attached to a tiny effect can be useless at the cage side.

Error types and power

  • Type I vs II — Type I = false positive (alpha, "cried wolf"); Type II = false negative (beta, "missed the wolf"); power = 1 − beta (the chance of catching a real effect).+
  • Definitions — Type I (α) = reject a true H0 (see an effect that isn't there); Type II (β) = fail to reject a false H0 (miss a real effect).
  • Power — = 1 − β = probability of detecting a real effect; convention ≥ 0.80. It rises with a bigger sample (the main lever), a larger true effect, higher alpha, and lower variability.
  • The 2×2 hook — rows = your test decision, columns = the truth; the two correct outcomes sit on the diagonal, the two errors off it.

Confidence intervals

  • What it is — a 95% CI is the range that would contain the true value 95% of the time on repeated sampling; it shows direction AND magnitude, which a bare p-value hides.
  • Ratio measures — RR & OR: null value = 1; a CI that includes 1 is non-significant.
  • Difference measures — mean or risk difference: null value = 0; a CI that includes 0 is non-significant.
  • Width = precision — larger samples and lower variability narrow it. Hook: a 95% CI that excludes the null agrees with p<0.05.

Bias and validity

  • Bias vs confounding — bias is systematic error built into the design; a bigger sample does NOT fix it (only random error shrinks). Confounding is a separate third variable that distorts the exposure–outcome link.+
  • Bias types — selection (who enters distorts exposure↔outcome); information/measurement (systematic error recording exposure or outcome); recall (cases remember exposures differently — classic in case-control); observer (assessor's knowledge sways the reading); misclassification (non-differential usually biases toward the null, differential either way).
  • Confounder = all 3 — (1) associated with the exposure, (2) an independent risk factor for the outcome, (3) NOT on the causal pathway. Control by design (randomise, restrict, match) or by analysis (stratify / Mantel-Haenszel, multivariable regression).
  • Effect modification ≠ confounding — here the true effect genuinely differs by stratum (helps adults but not puppies); a real finding to report, not a nuisance to adjust away.
  • Validity & sampling — internal validity (measures truly, free of bias/confounding) vs external / generalisability (applies beyond the sample); reliability (precision, repeatable) ≠ validity (accuracy, close to truth) — a measure can be reliable yet wrong; kappa = observer agreement corrected for chance. Probability sampling (random, systematic, stratified, cluster) supports inference; convenience sampling is a classic selection-bias source.
Trap — correlation ≠ causation. Of the Bradford Hill considerations, temporality (cause precedes effect) is the one essential criterion; the rest support but are not a checklist that must all be met.

Diagnostic test evaluation

  • Sn / Sp / predictive values — a Sensitive test with a Negative result rules OUT (SnNout); a Specific test with a Positive result rules IN (SpPin). PPV (positive predictive value) & NPV depend on prevalence.+
  • Formulas — Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN) (of the truly diseased, the share the test flags — a property of the test, prevalence-independent); Specificity = TN/(TN+FP) (of the truly healthy, the share cleared — also prevalence-independent). PPV = TP/(TP+FP), NPV = TN/(TN+FN) — clinically useful but prevalence-dependent.

  • Worked example — Sn 90%, Sp 95%. Screen 1000 cats at 2% prevalence: 18 true positives vs 49 false positives → PPV = 18/67 ≈ 27%, despite a genuinely good test.
Trap — as prevalence falls, PPV falls (a larger share of positives are false) even though Sn & Sp are unchanged. That is why low-prevalence intake screening (e.g. FeLV) needs confirmatory testing.
