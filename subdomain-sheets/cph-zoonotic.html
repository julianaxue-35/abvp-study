Zoonotic Disease

Zoonotic Disease · Community & Public Health (11%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield cheatsheet — key shelter zoonoses (bacterial · parasitic · fungal), immunocompromised-owner precautions, bite-wound rules & the big exam traps. Added / clarified points marked +.

Overview & Classification Framework

  • Definitions & transmission categories — zoonosis · anthroponosis · reservoir / amplifying / dead-end host; routes: direct & bite/scratch, fecal-oral, aerosol, vector-borne, environmental
  • High-risk / immunocompromised owners — HIV/AIDS, transplant, chemo, pregnancy, <5 yr, >65 yr at disproportionate risk; counsel on Toxoplasma, Cryptosporidium, Bartonella, Salmonella, ringworm +
  • Classify by transmission ROUTE (direct/bite · fecal-oral · aerosol · vector · environmental) — route sets the PPE & protocol, not agent type
  • ~60% of human infections are zoonotic; ~75% of emerging human pathogens are animal-origin
  • Reservoir ≠ human-exposure source; dead-end host (humans for most arboviruses) doesn't onward-transmit

Bacterial Zoonoses in the Shelter

  • Salmonellosis · Campylobacteriosis (fecal-oral; raw diets, puppies/kittens)
  • Cat scratch disease (CSD; Bartonella henselae) — cat flea is the essential vector → flea control is the single most important prevention; not spread by saliva/bite alone; in IC hosts → disseminated bacillary angiomatosis (life-threatening) +
  • Pasteurellosis & Capnocytophaga (bite wounds; asplenic risk)
  • Q fever (Coxiella burnetii) — reservoir ruminants + cats, shed in birth fluids/milk/urine/feces; spore-like form extremely stable (survives dust mo–yrs, resistant to heat/drying/most disinfectants); ~60% asymptomatic; symptomatic = fever, headache, myalgia, pneumonia, hepatitis; chronic = endocarditis +
  • Psittacosis (Chlamydophila psittaci) — reservoir psittacines/pigeons/raptors (can look healthy) → inhaled feather dust/dried faeces (±bite); atypical pneumonia in humans; doxycycline treats BOTH bird & human; nationally notifiable +
  • Tularemia (Francisella tularensis, "rabbit fever") — reservoir rabbits/hares/rodents, ticks = vector; highly infectious (tiny inoculum); cat hunts rodent → bite/scratch transmits to handler; bioterrorism agent; same policy as plague — do NOT treat/adopt, REPORT +
  • Salmonella / Campy — raw diets, puppies/kittens, reptiles (reptiles ~74k US cases/yr) → high-risk persons avoid reptiles, chicks, ducklings
  • Capnocytophaga — asplenic/alcoholic → fulminant sepsis/DIC (~30% fatal); even a lick on broken skin
  • Q fever (Coxiella) — AEROSOL from parturient queen's birth fluids (N95, no bite needed)
  • Plague / tularemia — via rodent-hunting cats (cat pneumonic plague = aerosol) → do NOT treat/adopt, REPORT
Trap Bite wounds: NOT first-gen cephalosporins (cephalexin/cefazolin) — poor Pasteurella cover; use amoxicillin-clavulanate.

Leptospirosis

  • Spirochete shed in urine — indirect transmission via urine-contaminated water/soil; wear gloves when handling urine + hand hygiene; zoonotic & reportable in many jurisdictions; doxycycline +
  • Reservoir — rodents (Norway rat) maintenance host; unvaccinated outdoor dogs shed; flood/hoarding intakes high-risk
  • Weil's disease — jaundice + acute renal failure + pulmonary haemorrhage; 5–15% CFR in severe cases
  • Dog 4-serovar leptospiral bacterin recommended in endemic areas
  • Dx — MAT (microscopic agglutination test) is the reference test; single titre can't separate infection from vaccination → need a 4-fold rise on paired acute/convalescent titres. PCR useful early (blood, leptospiraemic phase) before urinary shedding starts. Incubation ~5–14d human / ~7d dog +
  • Environmental survival — weeks in warm, moist, neutral–alkaline conditions (mud, stagnant water); killed by desiccation, UV/sunlight, standard disinfectants (bleach, quats) +
Trap Cats are relatively RESISTANT — not a meaningful occupational lepto source; risk is dogs + rodents.

Canine Brucellosis — Brucella canis

  • Transmission — reproductive fluids / aborted material; intact & breeding dogs, commercial-breeder intake
  • Human disease & shelter policy
  • Import risk (Mexico, C/S America, E Europe, Asia); test late-term abortion, male repro failure, or discospondylitis
  • Confirmed case — euthanasia often recommended: intracellular, not cleared by tx, lifelong intermittent carrier; spay/neuter cuts but doesn't stop shedding
  • NOT airborne — needs contact with repro secretions; notify lab before culture (biohazard)
  • Zoonotic (milder than B. melitensis/abortus); exposed staff → occ-health serology at 4–6 wk
Trap RSAT = high false-positive screen; confirm with AGID/PCR before any euthanasia decision — never cull on RSAT alone.

Parasitic Zoonoses in the Shelter

  • Toxoplasmosis — risk to pregnant women is from PRIMARY infection during pregnancy, not reactivation; oocysts need ~1–5 days to sporulate → daily litter change + gloves; also spread via undercooked meat (commonest route) & organ transplant/transfusion, not just cat faeces; congenital infection → chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, intracranial calcifications, developmental delay; IC reactivation = toxoplasmic encephalitis, #1 cause of brain abscess in HIV/AIDS (CD4<100) +
  • Toxocariasis (visceral / ocular larva migrans, VLM / OLM)
  • Hookworm (cutaneous larva migrans, CLM)
  • Cryptosporidiosis · Giardiasis
  • Toxocara — eggs need 2–4 wk to embryonate, survive soil for years; seroprevalence ~14–30%; VLM mainly 2–7yo pica kids (fever, cough, hepatomegaly, eosinophilia) / OLM (blindness)
  • Hookworm CLM — L3 skin penetration (barefoot on sand); serpiginous creeping eruption; self-limiting
  • Crypto — oocysts immediately infectious & chlorine-RESISTANT → use AHP/steam; soap-water not sanitiser
  • Giardia — dog/cat assemblages (C/D/F) mostly NOT zoonotic; human risk mainly water & assemblage A
Trap Cat is the ONLY definitive host (dogs shed NO oocysts); fetal risk only from PRIMARY maternal infection in pregnancy — seropositive-before = protected.

Fungal & Other Zoonoses

  • Dermatophytosis (ringworm) — Microsporum canis; direct contact + fomite/spore; children & immunocompromised at risk; high shelter-outbreak potential +
  • Sporotrichosis
  • Ringworm = #1 zoonosis BY VOLUME in shelters; M. canis spores persist 12–18 mo; up to 20–30% cats asymptomatic carriers
  • Dx: DTM culture is gold standard; tx = lime-sulfur dip + systemic itraconazole/terbinafine (combo > either alone)
  • Sporotrichosis — Sporothrix schenckii, dimorphic (mold in environment, yeast in tissue); traumatic inoculation (thorns) OR cat scratch/bite (Brazil epidemic); infected cats → ulcerative nodular skin lesions ± nasal involvement; human = lymphocutaneous form (nodule + ascending lymphangitic chain); itraconazole first-line both species +
Trap Wood's lamp catches only ~50% of M. canis — a negative does NOT rule out ringworm; culture required.

Viral Zoonoses & Anthroponoses

  • H5N1 (2024–25) — raw milk from infected dairy cows → fatal neuro disease in cats; never feed raw dairy; N95 + report; cat→human transmission RARE
  • Monkeypox (Mpox) — prairie dogs & small exotic rodents implicated in 2003 US outbreak (via infected Gambian pouched rats at a pet distributor) → shelters taking exotic rodents should be aware +
  • Anthroponoses (reverse zoonoses, human→animal) — MRSA (bidirectional, fomites/direct contact — screening NOT routine, reserve for outbreak/cluster investigation); M. tuberculosis (owner→dog, classic teaching example); C. difficile (survives dry surfaces days–wks, fomite spread); Group A Strep/S. pneumoniae (human→dog/cat); SARS-CoV-2 (→cats, dogs, mink) → staff with active infection limit close animal contact +

Risk Reduction & Occupational Health

Elimination / substitution not accepting high-risk species when alternatives exist; no raw meat diets
Engineering controls isolation ward ventilation, HEPA for ringworm, handwash sinks at room exits
Administrative controls written SOPs, staff health screening, mandatory bite reporting, training
PPE gloves, N95 for aerosol risks, gown/eye protection, dedicated footwear — LAST line, not first
  • Written zoonosis risk program (ASV = Association of Shelter Veterinarians Standards of Care)
  • Bite / scratch first aid — immediate copious washing + wound care; assess rabies exposure (see Rabies subdomain); amoxicillin-clavulanate first-line, NOT first-gen cephalosporins +
  • Adoption counseling · PPE guide (PPE = personal protective equipment)
Release/adoption decision — 3-factor test+
(1) Risk of transmission animal → caregiver
(2) Risk disease actually develops if exposed
(3) Risk of a fatal outcome if it does
Low on all three → release with disclosure, NOT automatic exclusion
  • Hand hygiene (soap + water) = single most impactful control; sanitiser weak vs oocysts/non-enveloped agents
  • Bite first aid — pressure-irrigate punctures with 20-mL syringe + 18-G needle; >8 h delay = usually already infected; 2–30% get infected
  • High-risk adopters AVOID — <6 mo, active diarrhoea, fleas/ticks, long nails, aggressive → pick a different pet, don't deny adoption
  • Reporting — unusual/high-consequence zoonoses → state public health vet (animal side) + NNDSS (CDC's National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System, human side, fed by state/local health depts) +
  • Necropsy = high-risk procedure — non-essential personnel excluded +
  • Needlestick/sharps — never recap by hand (one-handed scoop method if unavoidable); sharps container mandatory; Needlestick Safety & Prevention Act (2000) cut hospital percutaneous injuries 38% in yr 1 +
Trap Two testable bite specifics: 20-mL syringe + 18-G needle for punctures, and the 8-hour rule for established infection.

One Health Framework for Zoonotic Disease

  • Definition & pillars (human · animal · environmental health — WHO / FAO / WOAH / CDC)
  • Shelter medicine's role in One Health
  • Emerging / re-emerging — shelter as sentinel
  • Triad = human + animal + environment (WHO/FAO/WOAH/CDC); no pillar solvable in isolation
  • Shelter roles — surveillance sentinel · herd immunity (rabies vax) · parasite control · adoption education · TNR vax
  • Emergence drivers: wildlife-interface land use, climate → vector range, live-animal trade, AMR

📄 Journal Articles

  • A Novel Approach for Feline Sporotrichosis Pathogen Detection Based on Loop-Mediated Isothermal Amplification (LAMP): Sporothrix was detected with high sensitivity — 96.77% overall, 100% using blood samples, 77.78% using nasal swabs, and 92.31–100% using swab and adhesive-tape samples of cutaneous lesions.
  • A One Health Perspective on Heartworm Disease: Allergy Risk in Owners of Infected Dogs (Gran Canaria, Spain): 46.4% of dogs tested positive for D. immitis, and owners of infected dogs had a significantly higher prevalence of allergic disease than owners of uninfected dogs (65.89% vs 24.64%). Age, sex, breed, and owner demographics were not associated with infection or allergy, but habitat was — indoor-only dogs had lower infection rates, while owners of infected dogs in mixed indoor/outdoor environments had the highest allergy rates, suggesting cohabitation with an infected dog may be a novel atopy risk factor.
  • Antimicrobial Resistance in European Companion Animal Practice: A One Health Approach: a major obstacle to combating AMR in Europe is the absence of a mandatory, uniform surveillance system — inconsistent data collection creates knowledge gaps and hinders investigation and policymaking. Existing laws and regulations are insufficient, and current policies fail to enforce strict antimicrobial stewardship across all EU countries; urgent action is required.
  • Companion Animal Veterinary Personnel Have Occupational Risk for Tularemia, and a One Health Role for Tularemia Prevention in Kansas: 43% of vets reported diagnosing tularemia in cats and 13% in dogs, and 7% reported having had tularemia themselves. Glove use was 100% for high-exposure-risk procedures (e.g. lancing abscesses in suspects), but other PPE (eye protection, surgical masks) was inconsistent. Antibody titer and PCR were the common diagnostics, but 42% of vets reported never submitting a confirmatory test, mainly due to owner finances; knowledge of reporting regulations was inconsistent, though 91% discuss tularemia's public-health risk with owners.
  • Detection of Selected Vector-Borne Pathogens in Domestic Animals, Ectoparasites, and Their Owners in a Rural Community in Southwest Guatemala: over a 2-week community-level study, all human and dog blood samples were negative for Bartonella. Ehrlichia spp. was amplified from 60% of dogs, with sequencing confirming Ehrlichia in 2 dogs and in ticks/fleas collected from those dogs — Ehrlichia is common in dogs in this rural community, and Anaplasma was circulating in poultry.
  • Development of a Dynamic Stochastic Compartmental Model of Zoonotic Toxocariasis Transmission: the model developed can serve as a foundational tool for testing public veterinary-health interventions aimed at reducing the burden of toxocariasis.
  • Diverse Human Dimensions Affect the Management of Public and Animal Health Impacts of Free-Roaming Dogs in Australia — a One Health Solution: proposes a typology of free-roaming dogs to help situationally define animal/public health and welfare issues, identifies human dimensions affecting management and the discipline specialities an effective One Health approach requires, and sets out audience segmentation and research/policy priorities to encourage best-practice management for each type of free-roaming-dog impact.
  • Focus on Brown Dog Tick–Transmitted Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever in Dogs and People — Shared Threats and Solutions: RMSF has re-emerged in northern Mexico since the early 2000s, with at least 9,152 human cases and case mortality up to 50%, concentrated where people are impoverished with marginal medical access. Roaming dogs in a neighbourhood track with human cases, canine seroprevalence, and tick abundance; dogs and people share haematological/clinical-chemistry findings and diagnostic results. Suspected cases should get immediate doxycycline; co-infection with other R. sanguineus-transmitted pathogens is common.
  • GPS Tracking of Free-Roaming Dogs and Human Spillover Risk of Echinococcus granulosus in Highly Endemic Peru: human echinococcosis prevalence of ~5–7% in Peru is likely an underestimate; canine E. granulosus prevalence was alarmingly high (85% positive). All dog home ranges overlapped their owners' households, and even seronegative-dog households overlapped with nearby positive-dog home ranges — spillover risk exists even without an obviously infected pet.
  • High Parasite Prevalence Driven by the Human-Animal-Environment Interface (Urban Southern Chile): parasite prevalence was 28% in humans, 26% in owned dogs, and 44% in environmental dog faeces; anti-Toxocara canis IgG was present in 33% of humans. Soil contamination was found in up to 30.5% of park samples (Toxocara, Trichuris vulpis); zoonotic Giardia duodenalis and Blastocystis subtypes were detected in humans.
  • High Prevalence of Toxoplasma gondii Infection in Free-Roaming Dogs From Ecuador — a Proxy for Sentinel Surveillance of Zoonotic Threats in LMICs: overall seropositivity of 39.7%, with no significant regional or urban/rural differences.
  • Molecular Diagnostics and Control of Zoonotic Dermatophytosis: First Detection of Trichophyton indotineae in a Dog in Africa: of 90 dogs examined for skin lesions (alopecia, crusts, pruritus, scales), 52.2% were fungal-culture positive (70% in cats). Microsporum canis was most prevalent (60%), then Trichophyton mentagrophytes (20%); T. indotineae — typically a human pathogen — was also isolated (Egypt), suggesting host-range expansion and an emerging public-health threat. Animals under 1 year had significantly higher infection risk than adults.
  • Molecular Identification and Genetic Diversity Analysis of Cryptosporidium spp. Infecting Dogs From Central and Northern Jordan — Detection of Zoonotic Genotype IId: overall canine infection rate 18.9%; cryptosporidiosis was significantly associated with indoor housing, cohabitation with other animals, and raw-food diets. Of positive dogs, 58.1% were C. canis, 34.9% C. parvum (zoonotic), and 7% C. baileyi.
  • Mycobacterium bovis Infection in Cats: Zoonotic Transmission: M. bovis was confirmed in 2 cats, in their owners (no livestock contact history), and in a veterinarian who sustained a needlestick injury during sample collection.
  • Pet Treats, Salmonella, and Antimicrobial Resistance — a One Health Problem: 4 pig-ear treats from Brazil were Salmonella-positive, including 3 colistin-resistant isolates and Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, and Enterobacter hormaechei harbouring the mcr-1.18 resistance gene; one carbapenem-resistant E. coli was found on a bully stick. Treats from North America or bought in grocery stores had lower contamination risk.
  • Raising Awareness of Canine, Feline and Human Dirofilariosis in Aveiro, Portugal — a One Health Perspective: canine prevalence 4.7% (peaking at 16.7% in Vagos); feline and human seroprevalence 8.9% and 3%. Positive samples occurred in both high- and low-risk areas, supporting chemoprophylaxis in all municipalities.
  • Shelter Dogs as Reservoirs of International Clones of Escherichia coli Carrying mcr-1.1 and bla(CTX-M) Resistance Genes in Lima, Peru: first report of mcr-positive E. coli in dogs from Peru; stray and shelter dogs may be a reservoir for spread of WHO priority pathogens and polymyxin/beta-lactam resistance genes.
  • The Impact of Free-Roaming Dogs on Lemurs in Madagascar — a Decade of Research and Intervention: free-roaming domestic dogs in and around protected areas both predate and displace native wildlife, reducing forest wildlife where present; dogs also vector pathogens with lethal consequences for lemur populations and serve as important AMR vectors.
  • The Risk Factors Associated With the Carriage to Critical Antimicrobial-Resistant Escherichia coli in Healthy Household Dogs — a One Health Perspective: resistance rates were 13.3% (amoxicillin-clavulanate), 6.0% (ceftazidime), and 18.3% (enrofloxacin). Dogs bought from pet stores had 6.04× higher odds of ceftazidime resistance than adopted dogs; prior hospitalisation increased enrofloxacin-resistance risk, while cohabiting with a healthcare worker or occasional contact with free-roaming animals was protective against resistance.
  • Toward One Health Integrated Companion Animal Health Surveillance: Barriers and Solutions According to Expert Opinion: key themes for designing a future surveillance system included reluctance to share data, complexity of extracting/processing data, securing funding, need for a consolidated vision/value/targeted outputs, risk of data misrepresentation, and the need for strong governance.
  • Veterinary Clinicians as One Health Messengers: Opportunities for Preventing Zoonoses While Promoting Biophilia in the United States: identifies two constraints on vets as One Health communicators and proposes reconceptualising the human-animal bond as a community-level resource (like natural capital) and expanding the veterinary extension workforce to include agents facilitating local conservation/public-health information exchange.
  • Dogs as Reservoirs for Leishmania donovani, Bihar, India, 2018–2022: aggressive sand-fly population reduction has pushed flies toward non-human hosts, making the dog's role in L. donovani transmission increasingly important; 12% of dogs were infectious to sand flies during winter/rainy seasons, though visceral-leishmaniasis patients remain the primary transmission source.
  • High Seroprevalence for SARS-CoV-2 Infection in Dogs: Age as Risk Factor for Infection in Shelter and Foster Home Animals: 20.22% of 111 dogs had IgG against the SARS-CoV-2 N protein by ELISA; adult dogs had 4.16× the odds of positivity versus young dogs. High population density and repeated COVID-19 exposure emerged as risk factors.
  • Human Exposures to Brucella canis From a Pregnant Dog During an International Flight — Public Health Risks, Diagnostic Challenges and Future Considerations: shelters and breeding programs should routinely screen for brucellosis, since imported/domestic animals often have unknown health histories; testing intact dogs before breeding, purchase, or domestic/international transport is important.
  • Incorporating Public Health Competencies Into Veterinary Medical Education: dual-degree programs (public health + DVM) are hypothesised to enhance interprofessional capability; students most valued competencies in epidemiology, biostatistics, and behavioural health, and dual-enrolled students self-reported significantly higher knowledge of disease measurement, ethical/legal principles, and epidemiological data interpretation.
  • Knowledge and Practices Related to Louse- and Flea-Borne Diseases Among Staff Providing Services to People Experiencing Homelessness in the United States: most staff agreed lice/fleas are a problem for this population; about half knew these vectors transmit disease, but most couldn't accurately identify which diseases, and fewer than a quarter could describe an appropriate management protocol.
  • Operational Risk Assessment Tool for Evaluating Leishmania infantum Introduction and Establishment in the United States Through Dog Importation: recommended dog risk-management strategies include sand-fly insecticides/repellents, sterilisation, and treatment.
  • Rapid Increase in Seroprevalence of Borrelia burgdorferi Antibodies Among Dogs, Northwestern North Carolina, USA, 2017–2021: canine seroprevalence rose from 2.2% (2017) to 11.2% (2021), and incident seropositivity from 2.1% to 7.6%; the spatial pattern shifted from clustered to dispersed. Elevation, slope, aspect, and forest-cover density were associated with seroprevalence at various household-buffer scales in 2017; slope alone was associated with household-level seroprevalence in 2021.
  • Salmonella Vitkin Outbreak Associated With Bearded Dragons, Canada and United States, 2021–2022: most US patients (66.7%) were under 1 year old, and 72.7% reported bearded-dragon exposure; 38.5% of patients were hospitalised.
  • The Emergence of Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever in the Southwestern United States and Northern Mexico Requires a Binational One Health Approach: RMSF case fatality is 5–7% among thousands of annual Mexican cases, but often exceeds 30%; vets and physicians should alert the public to risk, prevention, and the importance of urgent treatment if symptomatic. Novel management approaches include vaccine development/deployment, acaricide-resistance monitoring, and modelling to guide targeted dog-population management.
  • Transmission of Brucella canis in a Canine Kennel Following Introduction of an Infected Dog: the outbreak began with a seropositive imported intact male dog showing clinical signs; genetic clusters remained stable over time, helping trace new isolates. International movement of dogs from endemic countries poses a threat to canine populations; serological screening and whole-genome sequencing were valuable for outbreak investigation.
  • Urban Tick Exposure on Staten Island Is Higher in Pet Owners: pet owners were more likely than non-owners to have ever found a tick on themselves or a household member (63% vs 46%); dog owners were more likely than cat-only owners to have found a tick on their pet. Pet owners were more likely to know ticks transmit Lyme disease and to avoid gardening, but less likely to wear or adjust protective clothing.
  • Clinical and Epidemiologic Features of SARS-CoV-2 in Dogs and Cats Compiled Through National Surveillance in the United States: 94% of pets were exposed to a person with COVID-19 before infection. Clinical signs were present in 74% of passively identified pets versus 27% of actively identified pets; average illness duration was 15 days (cats) and 12 days (dogs), and average time between human and pet symptom onset was 10 days. Viral nucleic acid was detectable 3 days post-exposure; antibodies from 5 days, peaking at 9 days (cats) and 14 days (dogs).
  • An Integrated, Tentative Remote-Sensing Approach Based on NDVI Entropy to Model Canine Distemper Virus in Wildlife and to Prompt Science-Based Management Policies: human-settlement expansion is thought to affect CDV spread by shaping wildlife distribution/density/movement; prevalence was 60% in red fox, 14% in wolf, 47% in badger, and 51% in beech marten (detected by real-time PCR).
  • Benefits, Companion Animal Zoonotic Disease Prevalence and Public Perceptions of Pet Ownership Among People Experiencing Homelessness in Northern California: up to 25% of unhoused individuals own pets, gaining substantial benefits but facing unique challenges (housing/transport access, social stigma). Ectoparasitic-disease seropositivity in pets did not vary by housing status. Unhoused owners more often reported protective benefits, housing challenges, finding fleas, using bottled water for pets, and pets sleeping in their bed; housed owners more often reported companionship/entertainment benefits and challenges with pet-sitting/consistent parasite prevention.
  • Cats and SARS-CoV-2: A Scoping Review: covers susceptibility, induced immunity, infection prevalence, clinical manifestation, and human-to-cat and cat-to-cat transmission; key research gaps are lack of large-scale studies, underrepresentation of stray/feral/shelter cats, no non-experimental cat-to-cat transmission studies, and limited data on cats' role relative to other species.
  • Estimating the Dog Population, Responsible Pet Ownership, and Intestinal Parasitism in Dogs in Quito, Ecuador: human:dog ratio was 58:1 among stray dogs versus 3.5:1 among dogs kept indoors; more dogs per household combined with poorer living conditions was linked to less responsible pet ownership. 28% of households had a dog with intestinal parasites; Ancylostoma was the most frequent parasite in indoor-kept dogs.
  • Evidence of Exposure to SARS-CoV-2 in Dogs and Cats From Households and Animal Shelters in Korea: viral RNA detected in 2 of 1,018 pets (cats); 2.38% of dogs and 5.2% of cats were ELISA-positive, and 2.08% of dogs and 4.91% of cats were PRNT-positive.
  • Serological and Molecular Detection of Bartonella henselae in Cats and Humans From Egypt: Current Status and Zoonotic Implications: B. henselae wasn't isolated by culture from any cat blood/saliva/claw sample but was molecularly identified in 8% of cat blood samples; human seroprevalence was 46% (higher in females), higher in cat owners (51.4%) than in those with only cat-contact history (42.9%), and higher in rural (79.5%) than urban (24.6%) regions.
  • Seroprevalence of Toxoplasmosis Among Shelter-Housed Felines in a Philadelphia Suburb: of 84 cats, 24 (28.6%) were seropositive for T. gondii — 9 IgM-only, 9 IgG-only, 6 both — with most of the population seronegative.
  • Urban Landscape and Infection Risk in Free-Roaming Cats: of 291 trapped cats (southeast Spain) tested for T. gondii, Coxiella burnetii, Chlamydia felis, and FIV, 60% were seropositive for a single pathogen and 19% for two or three. Seroprevalence: C. felis 8%, C. burnetii 37%, T. gondii 42%; FIV seropositivity was low, and adults were more likely than young cats to be C. burnetii-seropositive. C. burnetii tracked with % urban area and human population size; C. felis with water-surface area; T. gondii only with agricultural land.
  • Serological Screening for Antibodies Against SARS-CoV-2 in Dutch Shelter Cats: of 240 cats sampled across 28 shelters, 2 were seropositive but PCR-negative — shelter cats are unlikely to be a SARS-CoV-2 reservoir or pose a significant public-health risk.
  • Shelter Cats Host Infections With Multiple Trypanosoma cruzi Discrete Typing Units in Southern Louisiana: seropositivity rate at least 7.3%, with 24.6% PCR-positive; seropositivity increased with age (incidence ~7.2%/year) while PCR positivity decreased with age. Vets should have greater awareness of T. cruzi in pets and evaluate spillover risk to humans.
