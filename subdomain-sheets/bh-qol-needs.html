Quality of Life & Needs Assessment

Quality of Life & Needs Assessment · Behavioral Health (16%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield summary of the QOL & Needs Assessment subdomain — welfare frameworks & assessment definitions grounded in the ASV Standards of Care 2022; my added / clarified points are marked +.

The Six Determinants of Emotional & Behavioural Needs

  • The six — environment · species · genetics · personality · prior socialisation · life experiences (overlapping, non-hierarchical)+
  • Non-hierarchical & overlapping — a deficit in one (genetics, poor early socialisation) reshapes how the animal responds to the others
  • Staff competency = Read body language → Describe objectively → Interpret & respond; train ALL personnel, not only behaviour staff

Physical & Emotional Health Are Bidirectional

  • Two-way: emotional/behavioural health ↔ physical health
  • Undiagnosed chronic pain / disease degrades affective states
  • Chronic negative states (shelter distress) impair immune function & slow medical recovery

What Quality of Life Is

  • QOL (quality of life) = balance of positive vs negative affective states — the more pleasant & the less unpleasant, the higher the QOL+
  • Shelter mandate: every attempt to sustain QOL — but, like happiness, no universal definition
  • Affective states = comfort–discomfort + pleasure; broad, complex, deeply individual
  • QOL, wellbeing & welfare used interchangeably; both physical AND emotional factors contribute
  • Individual: same environment = pleasure for one animal, fear for another → assess the animal in front of you, not the species average

The Five Freedoms

  • Five Freedoms — from hunger/thirst · from discomfort · from pain, injury or disease · to express normal behaviour · from fear & distress (avoiding negatives)+
  • Define essential welfare but target the absence of negatives only
  • Positives also needed for a "life worth living" → extended into the Five Domains

The Five Domains

  • Five Domains — 4 functional domains (nutrition · environment · health · opportunity/agency) feed the 5th, mental state → welfare; adds POSITIVE states+
  • Derived from Five Freedoms (after Mellor); ASV's actual shelter welfare framework
  • Adds a spectrum per domain — not just absence of pain but presence of comfort / fitness / positive experience
  • Two innovations vs Freedoms: (1) spectrum per domain; (2) positive welfare possible even with an unmet need
  • Welfare scoring — 4 dimensions characterise any affective state's significance: intensity (how severe) · duration (how long) · frequency/probability (how often) · scope (how many animals affected)+
Trap A single unmet need can swing welfare either way: a cage-rest cat with a healing pelvic fracture (pain + restricted agency) can be net-POSITIVE in an enriched foster; a well-fed, healthy dog in long-term kennelling (lost agency) can be net-NEGATIVE. Also — intense-but-brief ≠ automatically worse than low-intensity-but-chronic (score all 4 dimensions together).

How the ASV Ranks Its Statements

  • ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) tiers — Unacceptable · Must · Should · Ideal+
  • Unacceptable = avoid/prevent without exception · Must = necessary for humane care
  • Should = strongly recommended, expected in most circumstances · Ideal = implement when resources allow
  • Tiers set the strength of each recommendation: "Must" is a floor for humane care, not an aspiration

Assessment versus Evaluation

  • Assessment = ongoing, objective observation across the stay; evaluation = structured test (SAFER, Assess-a-Pet, Match-up II)+
  • Timing: behaviour most reliable AFTER acute intake stress settles — NOT the first 24–48 h (arousal distorts)
  • Record objectively (posture, distance, actions) not anthropomorphic labels — lets the next staffer respond consistently
  • Objective = "cowering in back corner, tail tucked below pelvic midline"; unacceptable = "spiteful / furious / sad"
  • Capacity for Care = all resources (housing, trained staff, medical, outcomes) to deliver Five-Domains welfare; exceeding it degrades welfare population-wide

QOL Assessment Tools

  • HHHHHMM (Villalobos Pawspice, end-of-life) — Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility, More good days than bad+
  • HHHHHMM: each item scored /10 (total /70; >35 ≈ acceptable QOL); an end-of-life scale only
  • McMillan mental-wellness = broad, whole-life model — NOT end-of-life (distinct from HHHHHMM — don't conflate)
  • Kiddie & Collins / Lamon = validated QOL/welfare tools for kennelled shelter DOGS
  • Profoundly & unresolvably compromised QOL → re-evaluate outcome; euthanasia may be appropriate to prevent unresolvable suffering

Behavioural Welfare Indicators

  • Behavioural observation on daily rounds = the primary welfare-monitoring tool; translates Five Domains to cage-side
  • Voluntary appetite = clearest signal; cat anorexia >48–72 h = emergency; unbroken cat hiding >24–48 h = welfare review
  • Stereotypies (pacing, circling, weaving, bar-chewing) = chronic stress/frustration, NOT "boredom" — intervene, don't just observe
  • Grooming: absent = depression/pain; focal over-groom = anxiety or site pain. Posture: hunched/guarded = pain; lateral unguarded sleep = positive
  • Play, exploration & soliciting interaction = most reliable POSITIVE markers; sustained absence despite enrichment = compromised QOL
Trap Absence of distress ≠ good welfare. A formerly reactive dog now lying flat, unresponsive to its name is behavioural depression / learned helplessness, not "settling in" — track POSITIVE indicators (play, appetite, voluntary engagement) too.

Ethical Decision-Making Tool

  • Combines 3 ethical lenses — Utilitarianism (overall wellbeing) · Deontology (rights) · Justice (fairness) — and scores each stakeholder (animal, staff, public) against all 3.
  • Purpose: guides the conversation on a hard case (e.g. euthanasia vs continued care) rather than prescribing a single "right" answer.
  • ICAM (International Companion Animal Management Coalition) — international coalition producing guidance on humane, evidence-based companion-animal population management.+

📄 Journal Articles

  • Impact of Living Environment on Attachment Behaviour in Domestic Cats From Private Homes and Shelters: cats from private homes showed more exploratory and play behaviour, particularly after reuniting with their caregiver, indicating a positive influence of the familiar person's presence. Shelter cats showed reduced play, more passive behaviour, and more frequent purring — particularly after reunion; physical contact increased following separations (indicating attachment), and meowing was most frequent during absences and reunions, possibly reflecting stress and renewed social contact.
  • Instruments to Assess Disease-Specific Quality of Life in Dogs: A Scoping Review: of 41 publications identified, only 12 had undergone formal evaluation — just 2 showed appropriate test-retest reliability, 7 acceptable internal consistency, 9 checked at least one aspect of face/content validity, and 8 tested a hypothesis contributing to construct validity. None of the instruments had been thoroughly evaluated across all psychometric properties, so guidelines are recommended to raise the standard of instrument development and validation in veterinary QOL research.
  • Prevalent and Severe Conditions That Compromise the Welfare of Shelter Dogs: Opinions From Taiwanese Experts: the most prevalent welfare-compromising conditions were limited access to toys, insufficient foraging opportunities, and intermittent excessive barking noise. The most impactful conditions for individual dogs were depressive disorders, fear-related or anxiety disorders, and limited space; the physical environment and behavioural interactions domains had the most significant and prolonged negative effects on welfare.
  • The Development and Piloting of a Quality of Life Assessment Tool for Dogs: in a 3-part pilot with 14 dogs — questionnaires, investigator-administered questionnaires combined with video observation, and participant-recorded videos — the team identified challenges, particularly around the feasibility of participant-recorded videos, though participants generally found the time commitment acceptable.
  • A Mixed-Methods Analysis of Similarities and Differences in Animal Shelter Staff, Dog Behavior Professionals, and the Public in Determining Kenneled Dog Welfare: shelter staff, animal behaviour professionals, and the general public watched kenneled dogs and rated their welfare. Professionals gave slightly lower welfare scores than the public; shelter employees and professionals used body language and behaviour to explain their scores more than the public did. All three groups mentioned enrichment as improving welfare, but shelter employees and professionals mentioned it significantly more often.
  • Rethinking the Utility of the Five Domains Model: the model has under-acknowledged limitations — it relies on expert/stakeholder opinion with little transparency around how individuals were selected; quantitative scoring is typically attempted despite no clear principles for aggregating welfare measures and little attempt to account for uncertainty; repeatability of findings has rarely been measured; and it doesn't consider indirect/unintentional impacts on non-target animals. These raise concerns about testability, repeatability, and potential for manipulation. Refining the model could partially address these limitations — it's useful for systematically considering all sources of possible welfare compromise/enhancement, but is not, in its current state, fit for purpose as an assessment tool.
  • Testing the Accuracy of Wearable Technology to Assess Sleep Behaviour in Domestic Dogs: A Prospective Tool for Animal Welfare Assessment in Kennels: comparing wearable-sensor measurements to behavioural observation, significant differences appeared for certain behaviours such as inactivity and activity during diurnal recordings, but agreement between the wearable technology and directly observed data ranged from 75–99% for recorded behaviours.
  • Welfare and Quality of Life Assessments for Shelter Cats: A Scoping Review: of 687 unique citations, 30 met inclusion criteria (a QOL or welfare analysis in shelter cats), identifying 7 validated ethogram-based and 2 validated physiological-based assessment tools. The Cat Stress Score (CSS) was the most commonly used stress tool (19 publications), but its stress level only correlated with physiological-stress measures in 1 of 4 studies; assessments incorporating both physical and behavioural metrics gave the most comprehensive, inter-rater-reliable evaluation. Cats generally acclimate to the shelter within a few weeks, and the acclimation period was less stressful for cats given hide boxes and/or human interaction.
  • Beyond "Doing Better": Ordinal Rating Scales to Monitor Behavioural Indicators of Well-Being in Cats: ordinal rating scales of key behaviours are a useful way to summarise observations using common language. The paper presents a method evaluating four expressions of cat welfare on a 0–5 scale — modified Fear/Anxiety/Stress score, Response to Petting score, Participation in Play score, and Food Intake Summary score — all showing almost perfect average inter-observer agreement and excellent average inter-observer reliability (attributed to standardised training programs).
  • Dog Welfare Using the Shelter Quality Protocol in Long-Term Shelters in Minas Gerais State, Brazil: predominant welfare hazards in shelter management were a lack of rehoming and admission policies, preventive healthcare programs, and trained staff; in shelter environments, hazards included bedding inadequacy, surfaces difficult to keep clean, behavioural problems, and a lack of rehabilitation programs.
  • Pampered Pets or Poor Bastards? The Welfare of Dogs Kept as Companion Animals: compares companion-dog welfare with that of modern village dogs. Typical suburban/urban companion dogs experience good welfare on several measures — security, satisfaction of nutritional needs, proper vet care — but also suffer human-created challenges, including unrealistic social demands that can lead to anxiety, depression, or aggression, and ill-devised breeding schemes that produce breeding-related diseases.
  • Quality of Life Measurement in Dogs and Cats: A Scoping Review of Generic Tools: most published generic QOL assessment tools don't show reliability or validation, with minimal to no statistical analysis behind them.
  • Space, Time, and Context Drive Anticipatory Behavior: Considerations for Understanding the Behavior of Animals in Human Care: animals are likely to show anticipation in locations related to the anticipated event, often in temporally predictable time frames, and before specific contexts they experience in day-to-day management — under certain circumstances, anticipatory behaviours likely drive observed behavioural or space-use patterns of animals in human care.
  • Using a Delphi Method to Estimate the Relevance of Indicators for the Assessment of Shelter Dog Welfare: the Delphi technique is widely used to establish expert consensus. In Delphi round 1, consensus was reached (standard deviation in expert weightings ≤5) easily for the welfare-score weightings of the four principles — Good Feeding, Good Housing, Good Health, and Appropriate Behaviour. Delphi round 2 used the coefficient of variation for consensus; no statistical differences were found between the two Delphi methods for attrition rate, response rate, or number of participants. Continuing rounds until consensus is reached is recommended, balancing time against participant fatigue.
  • Welfare Through Competence: A Framework for Animal-Centric Technology Design: grounded in contemporary animal welfare science, established technology-design practices, and applied animal-centred design expertise, this framework combines the Five Domains model with the Coe Individual Competence model, providing a structured approach to defining animal-centric objectives and refining them through a design project.
  • Deep Learning Classification of Canine Behavior Using a Single Collar-Mounted Accelerometer: Real-World Validation: a single collar-mounted accelerometer could detect behaviours including drinking, eating, licking, petting, rubbing, scratching, and sniffing; the device's position on the collar had no measurable impact on performance.
  • Welfare and Quality of Life Assessments for Shelter Dogs: A Scoping Review: 16 different assessment tools are used to evaluate welfare and QOL in shelter dogs. The Multi-Operator Qualitative Behavioral Assessment and the Quality of Life Assessment allow quick welfare assessments by shelter staff, while the Shelter Quality Protocol and Shelter Quality Protocol 2 are more comprehensive welfare assessments requiring some shelter-management experience or training.
