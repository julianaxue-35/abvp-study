Rabies

Rabies · Community & Public Health (11%) · Shelter Medicine

Rabies — virtually 100% fatal once clinical; highest-yield topic in this domain. Exam focus: what counts as exposure, the 10-day rule, quarantine durations, PEP + RIG, DFA testing. Pre-filled points marked +.

Overview & Virology

  • Virtually 100% fatal once clinical — a neurotropic (nerve-seeking) encephalitis; family Rhabdoviridae, genus Lyssavirus, enveloped negative-sense RNA virus — envelope makes it easily inactivated by soap & water, bleach, alcohol (contrast the hardy, non-enveloped parvo).+
  • G protein = cell entry + sole virus-neutralising-antibody target = the antigen in every licensed vaccine (N, P, M, L are the other four).
  • US wildlife reservoirs: raccoon (east), skunk (central/CA), fox (TX/AK), bats (all 49 states) — bat variants cause most US human deaths; dog-maintained rabies eliminated in the US.
  • Cross-protection is complete among RABV variants → variant ID is epidemiology only, never changing vaccine or PEP choice.
  • Hawaii is rabies-free — the only US state with no rabies in any species, maintained by strict quarantine/import rules; US human deaths are rare (~1–3/yr), almost all bat-associated+
Trap Cats (not dogs) are the #1 reported rabid domestic animal in the US — cat vaccination lags. Dogs remain #1 globally (>95% of ~59,000 human deaths/yr, Africa & Asia).

Pathophysiology & Transmission

  • Exposure = saliva or neural tissue into a wound / mucous membrane — bite is the dominant route; blood, urine, feces, and contact with intact skin are NOT exposures and do not require PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis).+
  • Myocyte replication → retrograde axoplasmic transport up nerves (~50–100 mm/day) → CNS → centrifugal spread to salivary glands & skin.
  • Face/neck bites incubate faster (shorter route to CNS); incubation 3–12 wk typical, range 2 wk–6 mo (rarely 1 yr).
  • Centrifugal (salivary-gland) phase = why saliva is infectious & enables ante-mortem nuchal-skin biopsy / saliva RT-PCR.
  • Non-bite routes (rare) — aerosol (lab accidents; bat-cave/spelunker exposure) and organ transplant from an unrecognised donor case are both documented +
Trap A bat found in a room with a sleeping, unattended, or intoxicated/impaired person (or a child) counts as a presumptive exposure even with no witnessed bite or visible wound — bat teeth are tiny enough that a bite can go unnoticed. Capture the bat for testing if possible & start PEP.

Clinical Signs & Diagnosis

  • Three stages (dog/cat): prodromal (behaviour change, mydriasis) → furious (aggression, pica, hypersalivation, seizures) → paralytic/"dumb" (jaw drop, dysphagia, ascending LMN paralysis, death) — not always in order.
  • First clinical sign → death = 2–10 days; an animal alive & well >10 days is unlikely rabid (but can't be excluded without DFA).
  • Cattle/large animals often present paralytic-only: pharyngeal paralysis, bloat, choke-mimic → keep rabies on any large-animal CNS differential.
  • DFA on fresh brain (brainstem, cerebellum, hippocampus) = gold standard (~near 100%); Negri bodies only ~70–80% sensitive; RT-PCR (saliva/nuchal skin/CSF) for ante-mortem.
  • Wildlife-specific signs — abnormal daytime activity in a nocturnal species, uncoordinated gait, unprovoked aggression, self-mutilation; a grounded/flightless bat active by day in an unusual location is classic +
  • Small rodents & lagomorphs (rats, mice, squirrels, rabbits) are rarely involved in rabies transmission — bites almost never require PEP per CDC, though state health department consultation is still advised +

Flow 1 — Shelter Animal Bites a Person

Animal bites a person at the shelter
Dog / cat / ferret (any vaccination status)
10-day quarantine appropriate housing + trained staff. Do NOT vaccinate during this window.
Healthy @ day 10 → vaccinate & release
Sickens/dies → euthanise (NEVER gunshot) + DFA now
Wildlife / bat
10-day rule does NOT apply cannot be observed
Capture & euthanise (never gunshot) → submit for DFA + start PEP pending results
Staff MUST document: bite incident, disposition decision, animal's vaccine hx, staff exposure event — all communicated to the health dept+

Flow 2 — Shelter Animal Exposed BY a Rabid/Suspect Animal

Shelter animal bitten/exposed by a rabid or suspect animal
Currently vaccinated
Immediate booster + 45-day observation
Unvaccinated / unknown
120 d (4 mo) dog/cat, 6 mo ferret quarantine — OR owner-elected euthanasia + DFA
Vaccinate within 96 h of exposure, at the start of isolation+
Trap Overdue but ≥1 documented prior vaccine → still managed on the vaccinated pathway (booster + 45 d). No documented vaccine history at all → treat as unvaccinated, regardless of the owner's claim.

Person bitten/exposed
Step 1 — wound care: soap + water wash ~15 min single biggest risk reduction, do this immediately
Previously vaccinated (PrEP)
2 doses only — Day 0 & 3. NO RIG.RIG can suppress the anamnestic response in already-vaccinated people+
Unvaccinated
RIG 20 IU/kg — infiltrate into/around wound Day 0 (remainder IM at a distant site, up to Day 7) + vaccine Days 0, 3, 7, 14
Report to local/state health dept (mandatory, all states) — PEP decision made jointly with attending physician+

PEP & Vaccines

  • PEP is ~100% effective if prompt & complete — unvaccinated person: wound wash + RIG (rabies immune globulin, 20 IU/kg, infiltrate into/around wound) on day 0 + vaccine days 0, 3, 7, 14; previously-vaccinated person: 2 doses (days 0 & 3), NO RIG.+
  • Vet vaccines are all killed or recombinant — no licensed MLV rabies vaccine for dogs/cats in the US; 1st dose ≥12 wk, booster at 1 yr, then q1 or q3 yr.
  • 1-yr vs 3-yr products are immunologically identical (label/regulatory difference, not protection); a 1-yr product 13 mo out = overdue → treat as unvaccinated.
  • Human vaccine into deltoid (adult) / anterolateral thigh (child) — NEVER gluteus (poor response); RIG never in the same site/syringe as vaccine.
  • Cats: give rabies in the right distal hind limb — chosen for FISS (feline injection-site sarcoma) risk, so a tumour there stays amputable; non-adjuvanted recombinant preferred (adjuvanted = higher FISS risk).

Public Health & Law

  • NASPHV Compendium (National Association of State Public Health Veterinarians — Compendium of Animal Rabies Prevention & Control) is the authoritative reference for quarantine periods, vaccine schedules & exposure management; rabies is notifiable in all US states.+
  • Report suspected/confirmed animal rabies + all human exposures to the local/state health dept.
  • Quarantine authority rests with the health dept / animal control, not the shelter vet (the vet advises & supplies clinical info only).
  • Euthanasia for DFA = IV pentobarbital or deep-anaesthesia IC only; gunshot destroys the brainstem → non-diagnostic specimen.
  • National surveillance flows via states → CDC; published as the annual JAVMA rabies surveillance report.
  • Transport & contracts — animals moved between jurisdictions must have current rabies vaccination unless <12 wk old; shelters operating under animal-control contracts must maintain written protocols consistent with local ordinance & health department expectations +

One Health Framework for Rabies

  • Zero by 30 (WHO/WOAH/FAO): end dog-mediated human rabies deaths by 2030; sustaining ≥70% dog-vaccination coverage breaks the transmission cycle.
  • ≥70% domestic-dog vaccination coverage is MORE effective than expanding PEP availability alone — coverage breaks the transmission chain at the source, whereas PEP only manages individual exposures after the fact +
  • Four pillars: dog vaccination · responsible ownership & population management · accessible human PEP · wildlife ORV + integrated surveillance.
  • ORV (oral rabies vaccine baits) contains wildlife rabies — e.g. blocks westward spread of the raccoon variant.
  • Shelters = vaccination points (HVLC clinics, TNR w/ rabies vax) + surveillance nodes; shelter vet = clinician + public-health + educator.
  • Global ~59,000 deaths/yr (mostly children, Africa/Asia) — disproportionately rural, low-income populations without PEP access; dog vaccination beats expanding PEP alone on cost-effectiveness.
  • US spends ~$300 million/yr on rabies prevention (PEP, animal vaccination, surveillance) +

📄 Journal Articles

  • An Assessment of Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Concerning Rabies Among Communities With a High Disease Risk in Timor-Leste: dog ownership was high (65.3% of households), and almost all owning households let their dogs free-roam (98.1%); a small proportion (2.3%) had imported dogs from Indonesia. About 52.3% had heard of rabies, and most of those (86.7%) were knowledgeable about the disease. Roughly a third of households reported a dog-bite event, but only two-thirds of those bitten in the last 12 months sought professional medical care — limited community awareness, but positive attitudes toward rabies prevention.
  • Assessing Veterinary Professionals' Perspectives on Community Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Regarding Dog Rabies (Turkana County, Kenya): 42.4% of surveyed veterinary professionals believed fewer than half of the community was knowledgeable about rabies, and 75.8% thought fewer than half could recognise clinical signs in dogs. Any/all professional types can contribute valid surveillance data for rabies control efforts, but overall rabies-related KAP among the Turkana community was unsatisfactory across all sub-counties.
  • Cave Dogs Around Major Urban Areas of Arequipa, Peru, Threaten Rabies Elimination Program: monthly field visits to 4 peri-urban localities with reported feral dogs identified dog caves by tracking footprints and other field signs, revisiting each cave 3 times monthly to record live and dead dogs/puppies. Feral dogs use caves — some apparently self-constructed — for resting, hiding, and reproduction; the high number of puppies and dead adult dogs indicated high population turnover, and these dogs are often unreached by mass rabies vaccination or sterilisation programs.
  • Comparing Vaccination Coverage and Dog Population Demographics Among Four Pilot Dog Rabies Vaccination Strategies in Uganda: of the 4 strategies tested (static-point, school-based, dog-livestock integrated, and dog-human-health-services integrated), integrating dog vaccination with human health services outperformed the others — achieving the highest mean coverage and consistently exceeding 60% across all 3 parishes using that strategy. A human-animal integrated delivery strategy may be an effective approach for rabies control.
  • First Report on Dog Bite Epidemiology and Rabies Diagnosis in Stray Dogs: A One Health Study From Puducherry: dog bite cases in the region rose from 16,652 (2020) to 20,063 (2023), with December consistently reporting the highest average bites; bites affected dogs, goats, cattle, and cats. Of 25 dog brain samples tested, 76% were rabies-positive; cases were near-equal by sex, 68.4% occurred in dogs under 3 years, and the highest monthly case counts were in May and October.
  • Free-Roaming Dog Populations and Movement Methodologies for Global Rabies Elimination: Knowns and Unknowns — A Scoping Review: several free-roaming dog (FRD) enumeration techniques were used, with photographic mark-recapture the most common. Home-range estimates were influenced by the technique used and population size, and the advantages/disadvantages of each technique remained unreported — leaving scope for misleading conclusions when comparing methods.
  • Global Perspectives on Rabies Control and Elimination: A Scoping Review of Dog Owners' Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices: substantial gaps exist in dog owners' knowledge, beliefs, and behaviours around rabies prevention. General awareness is high, but knowledge of transmission, clinical signs, and the disease's fatal nature is inconsistent and varies widely across studies. Vaccination uptake ranged from 1% to over 90%, with no study reporting full coverage; a strong positive correlation existed between vaccination practice and awareness of its benefits, and common barriers were lack of information, vaccine accessibility, distance to clinics, and personal constraints.
  • High Awareness, Low Compliance: Rabies Knowledge and Dog Ownership Practices Among Pastoralists, Marsabit County, Kenya: 86.1% of respondents (87% of dog owners) showed adequate rabies knowledge, but only 18.8% of owners met the threshold for responsible ownership and rabies vaccination coverage was just 22% — a significant knowledge-practice gap. Willingness to pay for dog sterilisation surgery predicted rabies knowledge; gender was the only significant predictor of responsible ownership, with females having lower odds.
  • High Prevalence of Multi-Viral Co-Infections and Low Rabies Seropositivity in Stray Cats of Shenzhen, China: overall pathogen positivity was 89.68% — FPV most prevalent (61.9%), then FCV (57.14%), FCoV-I (46.83%), and FHV-1 (23.02%) — with no rabies virus nucleic acid detected. Co-infection reached 62.7% (mostly dual infections, 33.33%); widespread feline pathogens with frequent co-infection alongside low rabies immunity indicate a potential public-health risk.
  • Identification of Raccoon Rabies Virus Variant in a Stray Kitten: The Role of Veterinary Practitioners in Detection and Reporting of a Non-Native Zoonotic Pathogen — Nebraska, 2023: a kitten found in a midwestern city was identified with raccoon rabies variant — a reminder of rabies risk in unvaccinated animals or those with unknown vaccination status, including strays.
  • Impact of General Anaesthesia on Immune Response to First Rabies Vaccination in Seronegative Domestic Cats: 35 healthy female cats undergoing elective spay were given an inactivated, adjuvanted rabies vaccine either at the end of anaesthesia or outside the peri-anaesthetic period. All cats seroconverted to titres above 0.5 IU/mL with no significant difference between groups — anaesthesia did not blunt the vaccine response.
  • Rabies Surveillance in the United States During 2023: 3,760 animal rabies cases were reported to the national surveillance system, a 5.1% increase from 2022. Of positive samples, 8.2% were domestic animals and 91.8% wildlife; raccoons (10.4%), bats (5.1%), and cats (1.2%) were the most frequently rabid domestic/wild animals cited.
  • Rabies Vaccinations at the Rural–Urban Divide: Successes and Barriers to Dog Rabies Vaccination Programs: herd immunity was not achieved in the Lusaka campaign, likely due to challenges in pre-campaign community sensitisation and distance to vaccination sites under the central-point approach. Since most dogs resided at very low densities, large-scale vaccination approaches face unique, high-logistical-cost challenges in settings like Zambia; prioritising vaccination in higher-density free-roaming dog populations could maximise effectiveness where resources are limited.
  • Vaccination Protects Animal and Human Health and Reduces the Economic Burden of Preventable Cases: Rabies in Vaccinated Dogs and Cats (2002–2022): only 2.56% of rabid dogs and 0.54% of rabid cats had a history of rabies vaccination, with 15 true vaccine failures identified over the study period. Up-to-date dogs and cats were 130.8× and 93.6× less likely to contract rabies than unvaccinated counterparts, and current vaccination practices are projected to prevent $166 million in public-health/healthcare costs over the following decade.
  • Dog Ecology and Rabies Control Including Canine Vaccination Coverage: Impacts From a Survey in Madagascar: of 400 surveyed dogs across 123 households, 84.5% were owned; 67.8% of owned dogs were 1–5 years old and 95.6% were kept for guarding, with 45% having free roaming access outside the premises. Most owners knew a bite could transmit rabies; of 19 reported bites, 73.6% were by the owner's or a neighbour's dog, and 6 involved children aged 7–15. Rabies vaccination coverage was only 34% among owned dogs, despite most being accessible to their owners for preventive vaccination.
  • Dogs on the Move: Estimating the Risk of Rabies in Imported Dogs in the United States, 2015–2022: the incidence rate of rabies was 16× higher in dogs imported from dog-rabies-variant-enzootic countries than the estimated rate for domestically acquired rabies in the general US dog population.
  • Evaluation of Country Infrastructure as an Indirect Measure of Dog-Mediated Human Rabies Deaths: a One Health approach using publicly available economic, environmental, political, social, and public-health data identified variables strongly correlated with country-level rabies burden estimates. The resulting STOP-R index offers a way to address the data gap and monitor progress toward eliminating dog-mediated human rabies deaths.
  • Rabies Surveillance in the United States During 2021: 54 US jurisdictions reported 3,663 rabid animals, an 18.2% decrease from 2020. Texas, Virginia, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, New York, California, and New Jersey together accounted for over half of all reported cases. Wildlife made up 91.5% of cases (bats 33.9%, raccoons 28.1%, skunks 18.9%, foxes 8.6%); rabid cats (5.9%), cattle (1.1%), and dogs (1%) accounted for 94% of domestic-animal cases.
  • A One Medicine Mission for an Effective Rabies Therapy: proposes that 4 categories of disease process need to be managed therapeutically — viral propagation, neuronal degeneration, inflammation, and systemic compromise — and introduces the Canine Rabies Treatment Initiative, a non-profit applying a One Medicine approach to diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic options for rabies in naturally infected dogs.
  • Dog Ecology and Demographics in Several Areas in the Philippines and Its Application to Anti-Rabies Vaccination Programs: dog:human ratios per 1,000 people were 256.3 (rural), 213.8 (semi-rural), 208.7 (urban), and 170 (small islands), for a national dog population of 23.29 million. Adjusting targets, resource allocation, and vaccination approach in at least 2 locations in 2018 led to a 3–4-fold increase in dogs vaccinated per city compared to previous years.
  • General Insights on Obstacles to Dog Vaccination in Chad on Community and Institutional Level: Chad has been a recognised rabies-endemic country since 1961 but has no national control strategy, and vaccination coverage is very low. Barriers span economic (vaccine cost, transport to sites), socio-cultural (belief that vaccination affects dog meat's therapeutic properties; dogs considered impure in the local Muslim faith, prohibiting handling), and institutional levels (vaccine unavailability in veterinary services, lack of communication about vaccination law, no rabies content in veterinary-agent training, and lack of intersectoral collaboration).
  • Where Rabies Is Not a Disease: Bridging Healthworlds to Improve Mutual Understanding and Prevention of Rabies: in western India, low-caste communities worship a goddess regarded as the "mother of rabies" following a dog bite to a person or livestock; this belief may shape attitudes and behaviours toward rabies prevention. Some reluctance toward mass dog vaccination was expressed because it is seen as interference in the goddess's control over dogs — addressing these cultural perceptions is likely critical to effective rabies control in the region.
  • Barriers and Opportunities for Canine Rabies Vaccination Campaigns in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: 62.4% of respondents said their dog had been vaccinated against rabies in the last year. Reported barriers included lack of awareness that dogs needed rabies vaccination (18.1%) and not knowing where to find vaccination (15%); the median price owners were willing to pay was $0.91 USD, and the median distance they were willing to travel was 1 km.
