Personnel Management

  • Hire for fit — skills can be trained; personality & common sense cannot+
  • Take your time — better to run short-staffed than rush a bad hire; gut feelings count; value team players
  • Communication: active listening + clarification loop (repeat back, confirm); fair pay = retention baseline
  • References: check past employers — key Q "would you hire this person again?"
  • Interview = facility tour: watch how they handle animals; cover euthanasia/adoption/infectious-dz policy
  • Probation protects the org, not the employee — terminate promptly if it's wrong, even before the period ends
  • Evals: good staff → appreciation/retention; poor staff → document specifics (= legal base for termination)
Trap Skills are trainable; common sense & personality are not — pick the good-attitude candidate over the skilled one with a bad attitude.

Volunteer Management

  • Same liability as paid staff — volunteer status does NOT lower the shelter's duty of care or legal exposure
  • Document training or you're as liable as if untrained; onboarding order: background check → orientation (shelter policy/protocol · biosecurity · emergency procedures) → documented training before unsupervised work
  • Report to a designated staff supervisor, never straight to the ED or Board
  • Scope: no vet-judgment tasks (meds, triage, euthanasia); can't exceed state practice-act limits; recognition = their only pay
  • Health disclosure at onboarding — immunocompromised, pregnant, or animal-allergic volunteers placed by risk+
  • Retention = match task complexity to skill; those who feel competent but appropriately challenged retain better than the overwhelmed or bored; learn each person's WIIFM motivation; rule-breakers exit via the same documented, progressive process as staff+

Governance & Authority

  • Board vs operations — Board sets mission, finances & long-range plans; does NOT run day-to-day operations+
  • Board → staff only via the ED; Board never signs off individual euthanasia decisions
  • Authority/credentials only to the trained — non-credentialed staff cannot make medical decisions
  • Resource-allocation decisions belong to personnel who understand the shelter's priorities & its C4C+
  • Vet must be consulted on all medical/behavioral-health policies — not optional
  • Police/municipal-run shelters: educate non-"animal people" leadership on "what's in it for them"
Trap A board member directing kennel/intake staff (change feeding, accept this animal) = governance failure & boundary violation, regardless of intent.

The Chain of Command

  • ED (Executive Director) is staff's sole interface with the Board — staff never bypass the ED+
  • Structure: Board → ED/CEO → Animal Care · Operations · Admin & Fundraising+
  • Staff never go over the ED's head to the Board, even when they disagree with an ED decision
  • Worst dysfunction arises when the Board–ED relationship is weak or uncooperative

Media Relations

  • Spokesperson — one designated media point person; vet comments only if authorized+
  • Refer ALL inquiries to the point person; assume reporters want accuracy — truth builds credibility
  • Cruelty case: refer + decline unless authorized; off-record interview or letting a reporter into a sensitive area = wrong
  • Hit the media first with a positive story — don't wait for a crisis to force coverage

Interview Rules

  • Two traps — never say "no comment"; never repeat the reporter's negative words+
  • Get the questions in advance; assume you're always being recorded
  • State the key point first — editors cut from the end; weave 2–3 core messages throughout
  • Keep answers concise (court-testimony style); reporter gets the last word — stay upbeat, never sarcastic
  • Don't know? Say so & report back before deadline; never fabricate stats

Community Engagement

  • WIIFM (What's In It For Me?) — every small "yes" makes the next yes easier+
  • More time donated → more ownership & loyalty; start with small achievable asks
  • Fees settled BEFORE service (full / reduced / at-cost / pro bono) — never after the fact
  • Local vets on the mailing list; shelter vet writes columns / invite them to teach
  • Vets resent advice solicited then ignored — genuinely act on input or don't ask
Trap Providing food at after-hours practitioner tours is explicitly recommended to boost attendance — a testable detail.

Strategic Planning & Protocols

  • Strategic plan: define essential programs/goals, then allocate resources; review regularly — often quarterly
  • Consult industry orgs (ASV, NACA) when building policies/protocols
  • Mandatory training (paid staff · team members · volunteers): zoonotic-disease info (absolute), animal handling, body language, bite prevention → cover the key zoonoses (ringworm, rabies, leptospirosis, pasteurellosis, salmonella, MRSP/MRSA), PPE requirements, highest-risk groups (immunocompromised/pregnant), and the post-bite/scratch protocol; annual refresher+
  • Document all training — undocumented training = untrained (same legal exposure)
  • Broader curriculum: communication, data mgmt, husbandry, staff wellbeing/resilience, DEI
  • Legal compliance (local/state/national) reviewed regularly — not excused by mission or budget

Policies vs. Protocols

  • Policy = high-level framework (intake, euthanasia, adoption); protocol = detailed how-to for each task
  • All personnel must have access to protocols at all times; review/update regularly
  • Consistent intake/euthanasia criteria remove the "gray zone" — the top source of staff conflict
Trap Policies safeguard Capacity for Care (C4C) — they stop animal needs overwhelming resources; the exam may frame a policy question as a C4C question.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Shelter-medicine programming at AVMA-accredited colleges (AAVMC competency survey): 88% offer formal shelter-medicine coursework (programs typically established ~2012); 118 instructors total (median 2/school), only 5% tenured. 21% of schools had discontinued their program — mainly lack of faculty and lack of a shelter relationship. Median program covered 7 of 9 AAVMC (American Association of Veterinary Medical Colleges) competency domains; many covered all 9.
  • 2024 compensation, duties & benefits of US shelter-med veterinarians: salaries rose across all categories — largest for leadership/consulting, smallest for academic; part-timers' hourly rates rose sharply while hours fell. Leadership earned most, then faculty. Clinical salaries ran 5% below AVMA-reported 2022 private-practice pay and 7% below companion-animal vets.
  • Veterinarian leadership relationships in shelters (collaborative dynamics): ineffective communication was the top challenge cited by veterinarian non-leaders. Differences in priorities / conflicting goals was the top challenge for veterinarian leaders (66%); non-veterinarian leaders most cited managing expectations and demands (55%). Communication was the primary driver of successful relationships across all groups (68%).
  • Veterinarian of Record Relationships (VoRR) — expectations gap: veterinarians were significantly more concerned than non-veterinarian administrators about meeting the legal veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR) requirements, and expected higher compensation than administrators expected to pay — a financial/legal/ethical disconnect that can make vets hesitant to take VoRR roles.
  • Vet student & veterinarian perceptions of shelter-med employment: 40% of first-year and 43% of final-year students were likely to consider shelter-med work. Outreach / access-to-care clinics and loan-forgiveness encouraged interest; deterrents were compassion fatigue, burnout, stress, weekend work, euthanasia decision-making, and lower expected salaries.
  • Shelter-medicine sustainability — academic perspective: four themes threaten sustainability — external pressure from leadership/stakeholders, funder expectations, time horizons, and perceptions of shelters and shelter veterinarians.
  • Clinical-year student journals on a shelter-med rotation (qualitative): students found the rotation beneficial to their growth, practical and applicable, helpful for self-identifying knowledge gaps, and perspective-changing on human–animal welfare — evidence of authentic learning that builds more practice-ready, culturally competent graduates.
  • Online asynchronous shelter-surgery coursework (pre-/post-test): among 400 students, post-test scores rose significantly vs pre-test (p<0.001); no difference whether completed in ≤30 days or >30 days.
  • Dog foster volunteer retention: foster volunteers are generally satisfied with organizational support, but less positive about volunteer–organization communication, training beyond the basics, and the social supports of a foster-volunteer network — the key gaps to close for retention.
  • Emotional aspects of dog fostering — both ends of the leash: higher fostering-related emotional stress increases thoughts of quitting, threatening retention of frequent (high-value) fosters. Organizational support aimed at the human volunteer can ease this and improve retention.
  • Shelter volunteer satisfaction — the importance of "voice": volunteers with fewer opportunities for voice were significantly less satisfied overall. Satisfaction with voice and with the experience was higher at limited-intake shelters with higher save rates.
  • Dog welfare in Ireland post-COVID-19 (welfare-org view): organisations perceived poor public awareness of dog husbandry, weak legislation enforcement, negative puppy-farm impact, rising financial and volunteer burden, more households acquiring dogs, rehoming difficulties, and financial threats to their own viability.
  • Socioeconomic position & dog ownership (Denmark): ~20% of families own a dog; ownership itself wasn't linked to income/education/occupation once life-course and lifestyle were accounted for. Paying a high price was commoner in high-socioeconomic neighbourhoods and high-income families; low-income owners with limited labour-market involvement more often had mixed-breed dogs. Higher-education owners kept dogs for companionship; lower-education owners more often for instrumental reasons / social support (especially those frequently involuntarily alone).
  • Cat–human relationships, US vs Japan (cross-cultural): US citizens held significantly more positive attitudes toward pet cats than Japanese citizens, though owners in both were highly attached. Owner attachment predicted cat sociability toward the owner in the US but not Japan; US pet and café cats spent more time near people than their Japanese counterparts.
  • Human health benefits of non-conventional companion animals (review): benefits depend on the owner's perception of the animal, not its species. Non-conventional pets can provide social support (as attachment figures), social opportunities and daily routines, cognitive fulfilment, and restorative benefits.
  • Shelter-vet compensation (2024 survey, US): vs 2018 all salaries rose — leadership/consulting increased the most, academic the least. Part-time hourly rate rose sharply while part-timers worked ~2 fewer hours/week. Ranking held from prior surveys: leadership highest income, then academic shelter vets. Clinically-employed shelter vets earned 5% less than AVMA-reported 2022 all-private-practice salaries and 7% less than companion-animal practice. 2024 medians: clinical $122,500 · part-time $85/hr · leadership $168,000.
