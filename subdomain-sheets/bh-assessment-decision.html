Assessment & Decision Making

Assessment & Decision Making · Behavioral Health (16%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield exam cheatsheet for the Assessment & Decision Making subdomain — validity limits, traps, and decision rules; clarified points marked +.

Intake, Environment and Housing

  • Assessment is continuous — begins BEFORE admission; take a behavioural history at/near intake for every animal regardless of source (reason for surrender + prior behaviour); log aggression as an objective description of actions + circumstances.
  • Pre-admission info can divert intake: outpatient behaviour care, rehoming support, spay-neuter, return-to-field.
  • Prey housed away from predators at ALL times (olfactory + auditory + visual) — cats are predators but also prey to dogs.
  • Enrichment ranks equal to nutrition + medical care, never optional; core aim is choice + control.
Trap Unpredictability drivers = no daily routine, frequent enclosure disruption, and irregular continuous light/dark — the light/dark item is the one most often omitted. Intake staff use open-ended questions + objective language to cut social-desirability bias.

Dog Behaviour Assessment and Information Sources

  • Mixed, longitudinal model — formal evaluation tests predict future behaviour (esp. aggression) poorly, so combine objective history + observation across many contexts; foster & returns are the most accurate reflection of home behaviour.+
  • In-kennel: aggression toward people is HIGHEST in the kennel; toward dogs lowest there — monitor for early warning.
  • Untrained daily-care staff predicted only ~⅓ of adopter-reported problems vs ~75% for behaviour tests; ~half misread aggression as excitement (rarely the reverse) → people over-rate dogs.
  • Foster returns are ~20× more likely to be adopted/transferred; ~85% returned within 30 days re-adopt quickly. Sleepovers reveal little (dogs mostly sleep).
  • C-BARQ: 103-item under confidentiality → more disclosure of family-directed aggression + stranger fear; "will inform adoption" framing → LESS disclosure of owner-directed aggression.

Behaviour Evaluation Science

  • Validity depends on prevalence — Patronek & Bradley: at ~16% aggression prevalence, even 85% sensitivity/specificity leaves ≈48% of positive flags false ("no better than a coin flip"; up to 78% of flagged dogs false-positive). SAFER (Safety Assessment for Evaluating Rehoming) sens 60% / spec 50%, misses MODERATE aggression. For aggression favour high NPV (negative predictive value).+
  • Feasibility bar: ≤30 min, ≤2 handlers + a stimulus dog, documented protocol, simple scoring.
  • Good inter-rater reliability implies good intra-rater; evaluator sex + position (stand/kneel/sit) alter the dog's response.
  • mAAP sens 73% / spec 59%; shelter-vs-home evaluation identifies absence of aggression better than presence.
  • Food-guarding (assess-a-hand): predicts absence better (78% test non-guarders also non-guard at home); suspending it didn't change the ~9% return rate. Fake-dog test: poor for aggression, OK for friendliness.

Cat Behaviour Assessment

  • Core problem = feral vs merely frightened; holds to decide range 1 to >7 days (euthanasia possible after). A valid predictor cuts care-days + raises live release.
  • FTP (Feline Temperament Profile) = 10 sequential contact mini-tests; valid/consistent but NOT built to flag specific undesirable behaviours.
  • Fe-BARQ (Feline Behavioral Assessment & Research Questionnaire) — owner-report survey, feline analogue of the dog C-BARQ; screens playfulness, fearfulness & sociability trait dimensions rather than a single pass/fail score+
  • Feline-ality: assessed within 48 h, 8 items in a novel room, Independent–Gregarious + Valiance scales → 9 categories; linked to more adoptions/fewer returns — but no shelter study has yet validated the scales (the caveat).
  • Hiding box speeds stress reduction (approach cage front sooner); scan samples suit chronic fear, not discrete events like petting-induced aggression.

Risk, Behaviour Modification and Outcomes

  • LIMA (Least Intrusive, Minimally Aversive) + Humane Hierarchy; positive reinforcement over punishment. Respond promptly to significant safety risk — a credible report of severe human- or dog-directed aggression → euthanise as soon as legally possible (no benefit to holding).+
  • Behaviour meds: strongly consider for emotional-welfare concerns, acute intake/housing stress, or chronic problems (separation, fear of people); need objective dx, goals, vet advice.
  • Timing: owner-relinquished assessed at intake (↓LOS); stray the day before hold ends; if hold >5 days, assess after day 3 (plasma cortisol peaks days 1–3).
  • Single-incident euthanasia only if truly egregious (severe attack); else corroborate in other contexts. Thin-slicing is accurate for experts, error-prone for non-experts. Fast-track = good surrender report + high sociability.
  • Adoption exclusions: must be desexed; none to known animal/child abusers, applicants intoxicated at application, or for use as food.

Bite history: documentation, placement, and legal considerations

  • Dunbar Bite Scale — levels 1–6 (1 = air snap … 4 = deep puncture … 6 = fatal); Level 3+ narrows placement (no infants/toddlers). Most US states impose a legal duty to disclose known bite history to adopters — document disclosure and adopter sign-off.+
  • L3 = 1–4 punctures none deeper than ½ canine → no infants/toddlers + signed consent; L4 = ≥1 deep puncture or head-shaking wounds → experienced owner only, no young children.
  • L5 (multi-bite L4 attack) / L6 (fatal) → euthanasia is the supported outcome.
  • Document: date, victim age (adult/child/infant), Dunbar level, body location, circumstances, warning signals present, before + after, source + credibility.
Trap A credible surrender report of a severe (Level 4) bite carries weight — don't discount it as owner "exaggeration". But a vague, unattributed rumour alone is insufficient grounds for euthanasia — evaluate specificity (date, victim, circumstances, source).

Welfare and Ethical Decision Making

  • Three viewpoints of welfare — biological functioning · affective/emotional state · natural living; no single gold-standard measure. Frameworks: Five Freedoms, Welfare Quality (4 principles), Five Domains. QBA (Qualitative Behaviour Assessment) shows high inter-rater agreement.+
  • Cortisol: plasma peaks days 1–3, urinary peaks ~day 17; previously-kenneled dogs have LOWER urinary cortisol than naive dogs.
  • Behaviour alone is not a sole stress predictor (correlates poorly with plasma cortisol); some stereotyping dogs had better welfare, and stereotypy can persist as a behavioural scar.
  • Fix fundamental causes (reduce stressors, change environment) — pharmacology must not replace environmental change; restriction is a potent stressor, predictability + control protect welfare.
  • Moral stress (unable to do what's right) drives error, compassion fatigue, burnout — org structures beat individual resilience alone.

Daily rounds

  • Six rounds questions — Who are you? How are you? Are you where you should be? What can we provide today? Where are you going? Are you meeting the Five Freedoms/Domains? Run rounds before cleaning & feeding; reduces LOS (length of stay) and prevents crowding.+
  • Team = operations + medical + behaviour, led by a population manager, with protected time.
  • Run before cleaning + feeding to catch inappetent animals; in group housing sometimes during feeding to confirm access.
  • Purpose: create urgency, move animals through the system, prevent crowding.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Advancing Canine Welfare Assessment in Kennels: Evaluating the Validity of Field Instantaneous Dog Observation (FIDO) Scoring in Commercial Breeding Kennels: the FIDO tool may help identify dogs whose responses to unfamiliar people suggest a need for intervention to reduce fear — important for welfare, especially for dogs intended to be rehomed at the end of their breeding careers.
  • Beyond Cortisol! Physiological Indicators of Welfare for Dogs: Deficits, Misunderstandings and Opportunities: welfare assessment requires multiple physiological indicators reflecting distinct biological systems; in practice, indicators are often used in isolation with limited salient dog descriptors, failing to adequately reflect the canine experience. Age, body weight, and sex must be considered when interpreting biomarkers and should be reported; the authors advocate a practical, evidence-based approach using noninvasive collection methods.
  • Dog Guardian Interpretation of Familiar Dog Aggression Questions in the C-BARQ: Do We Need to Redefine "Familiar"?: singleton dogs with familiar-dog-aggression scores were more likely to regularly participate in social activities with other dogs, and had marginally lower fear-related C-BARQ scores than singletons with no familiar-dog-aggression score and dogs living in multi-dog households.
  • Effects of Maternal Care on Detection Dog Behavioral Outcomes: maternal behaviours declined across the three weeks studied, though individual differences among dams were stable. Counterintuitively, less maternal care predicted greater offspring success in the detection-dog program — consistent with other working-dog research showing more maternal care can associate with lower selection. Maternal care was shaped by an interaction of delivery type and parity, and by dam temperament.
  • Evaluation of the Factorial Structures of the Canine Behavioral Assessment and Research Questionnaire (C-BARQ) in France: the French version of the C-BARQ is a valid tool for assessing canine behaviour, though cultural, contextual, and demographic differences should be considered when interpreting findings.
  • Initial Psychometric Evaluation of the European Portuguese Version of the Lincoln Canine Adaptability Resilience Scale: the study presented criterion and construct validity of the European Portuguese L-CARS (variance 0.83) — a step toward giving Portuguese-speaking clinicians and populations a validated, accessible tool to evaluate patients; further studies are needed.
  • Investigating the Ability of Standard Tests to Detect Improvements in Behavior After a 9-Week Training Program in Shelter Dogs — A Pilot Study: in 12 shelter dogs, pre/post temperament-test scores for human sociability, playfulness, and total score showed no difference, but dog sociability scores did shift, and scores improved for walking on a loose leash, sit, down/stay, and coming when called; no differences appeared for accepting a friendly stranger, waiting politely for petting, walking through a crowd, or supervised separation. Inter-rater agreement was high for both tests. The proposed basic-education test reliably detects improvements from basic-education programs, but the temperament test used has minimal sensitivity to those same changes.
  • Navigating Challenges in Applied Animal Behaviour and Welfare Research: A Focus Group Study: three themes captured participants' challenges — the discipline of animal behaviour and welfare, conducting/sharing research, and researcher welfare and networking. Many challenges were shared across participants regardless of region, though certain socio-demographic groups raised specific issues (e.g. networking opportunities, travel barriers to conferences) more often; some participants believed collaboration among researchers from different regions could help overcome regional barriers.
  • Successive Negative Contrast: An Appropriate Approach to Measure Affective State in Dogs?: successive negative contrast (SNC) is a shift in response when animals unexpectedly face reward devaluation to levels below those of animals always receiving low-value rewards, proposed as a welfare-assessment tool. Overall, the inconsistency of SNC within and across dog populations raises questions about its suitability for evaluating dog welfare.
  • Testing the Level of Cortisol in Dogs: there's a growing trend toward non-invasive matrices (saliva, hair, urine) that reliably reflect acute and chronic stress responses while minimising animal stress during sampling; blood sampling is used less due to its own stress-inducing effect. Cortisol concentration can be influenced by many factors — environment, living conditions, genetics, social context.
  • The Impacts of Household Factors and Proxies of Human Social Determinants of Health on Dog Behavior: dogs from single-dog homes had increased odds of dog-directed fear and poorer trainability, particularly in early adulthood. Sleeping in the owner's bed was associated with increased aggression toward strangers and a greater reduction in trainability and excitability with age. Dogs in the most densely populated neighbourhoods had greater odds of showing aggression toward strangers than dogs in sparsely or moderately populated neighbourhoods.
  • Development of a Modified C-BARQ for Evaluating Behavior in Working Dogs: examines the factor structure, internal reliability, and content validity of a modified C-BARQ evaluating 4 new domains beyond the original — playfulness, impulsivity, distractibility, and basophobia (fear of falling) — successfully expanding the C-BARQ's measurement capacity for dimensions particularly relevant to many working-dog populations.
  • Evaluating the Generalizability of a Standardized Behavior Test in Predicting Programmatic Success of Guide Dog Puppies: used the Guide Dogs for the Blind Association (GDBA) UK Puppy Profiling Assessment to test whether it predicts success in a different US guide-dog population. 5 of the subtests were associated with success in guide-dog training, but no subtest assessed at GDBA provided predictive value for a dog's ultimate success — underscoring the importance of continuing to assess the suitability, predictability, and reliability of standardised behaviour testing across working-dog programs.
  • Exploring Domestic Cat Behavior Using the Fe-BARQ: identifies environmental, developmental, or genetic factors that could contribute to behavioural issues, potentially affecting human-cat relationships. These insights give prospective owners a glimpse into life with a cat, help manage behavioural challenges, and help gauge cat welfare by assessing playfulness, elimination-pattern changes, aggression, and stress-related behaviours.
  • Factors Associated With Behavioral Euthanasia in Pet Dogs: human-directed aggression, especially toward adults living in the household, was the most frequently reported reason for behavioural euthanasia, followed by aggression toward other animals (especially other household dogs). Most of these dogs had bitten and broken skin, and many had bitten in multiple or severe incidents. Most had lived in their homes and displayed problem behaviours for over a year before euthanasia, which occurred across a wide age range (under 1 year to 18 years).
  • Methods of Behavioral Testing in Dogs: A Scoping Review and Analysis of Test Stimuli: content analysis distinguished 29 subcategories of behavioural-testing stimuli, grouped into 3 major categories — human-oriented, environmental, and motivator-oriented stimuli — providing a practical overview of published behavioural tests and their applications to help researchers select/design appropriate tests.
  • Prediction of Working Outcomes in Trainee Dogs Using the Novel Assistance Dog Test Battery (ADTB): developed a machine-learning tool to predict working outcomes; the ADTB is a reliable tool for predicting working outcomes in trainee assistance dogs, letting assistance-dog organisations reduce training costs by using model predictions to guide which dogs to withdraw from training.
  • Who's Testing and Who's Being Tested: A Cautionary Tale of Citizen Science Within Applied Animal Behaviour Science: assessed the reliability of game-like tests (Alone Time Test, Three-Toy Test) for behavioural flexibility in dogs. In the Alone Time Test, the tester was associated with outcomes on 4 items in shelter dogs and 3 in long-term owned dogs; in the Three Toy Test, a series of items were contingent on the first item, and that item's outcome was related to the tester — a caution about tester effects in citizen-science behaviour testing.
  • Feline-Ality™ in Real Life: A Program Evaluation: the ASPCA's Meet Your Match® Feline-ality™ program, designed to match cats with owners by personality/lifestyle, was evaluated for implementation. The process evaluation identified and quantified 8 possible implementation errors, and results indicated a 1.6% overall success rate — a lack of substantive effect may indicate the program doesn't perform as intended, but is at least as likely to reflect a very high implementation error rate rather than a flawed underlying concept.
  • Influence of Sex of Stranger on Responses of Shelter Dogs During Canine Behavioral Evaluations: among 257 dogs (excluding 26 undersocialised dogs from one home), dogs tested with a male stranger had significantly higher (more concerning) scores than those tested with a female stranger, with a small-to-moderate effect size. Dogs responded differently to male vs female strangers during testing, but from a practical standpoint this doesn't warrant adjustments to how shelters conduct or interpret stranger-directed aggression tests.
  • Sociality of Cats Toward Humans Can Be Influenced by Hormonal and Socio-Environmental Factors: Pilot Study: male cats that began living with humans earlier had more contact with an experimenter; individual male cats with low testosterone levels were more likely to interact with an experimenter.
  • The Relationship Between Feline-ality™, the Interpersonal Circumplex, and Cat Characteristics: both tools measure two personality dimensions and predict successful interactions; the study also explored possible confirmation bias in the IAS-R as applied to cats. Neither Feline-ality™ nor the IAS-R correlated with cat age, sex, or colour, and there was no evidence of confirmation bias in the survey. Feline-ality™'s two personality dimensions were strongly correlated with each other, and one was moderately correlated with a similar IAS-R dimension.
  • Changes in Sociability of Shelter Cats: at first assessment, more than 3/4 of cats showed very friendly or friendly behaviour. Of 88 cats evaluated, 63.6% didn't change their score during their shelter stay; among those that did change, permanently improved scores were significantly more common than permanent deterioration, with only 3.4% showing temporary change. First-assessment sociability predicted last-assessment sociability. Cats generally improved sociability toward a familiar person during their stay; less-sociable cats appeared to stay longer, but improvement may increase adoption potential.
  • Conspecific and Human Sociality in the Domestic Cat: Consideration of Proximate Mechanisms, Human Selection and Implications for Cat Welfare: sociability is the ability/tendency of individuals to reside in social groups and display affiliative behaviours within them; individuals vary in tendency toward affiliative vs agonistic interactions, in both free-living and human-managed populations. Ontogeny, human selection, and a range of proximate factors (social/environmental parameters, individual cat and human characteristics) moderate cats' sociability, with consequences for individuals' adaptability across the diverse lifestyles they may occupy — where social-capacity limitations prevent sufficient adaptation, welfare compromises may occur.
  • Do Canine and Human Personality Assessments Predict Successful Adoptions?: examined whether dog personality, human personality, and dog-human personality-domain combinations predict successful adoptions. Human-animal attachment increased as the percentage of training focus for dogs increased, in both rater groups; but dog personality, human personality, and their combinations did not have good discriminative ability for post-adoption attachment and retention — the mechanism behind successful shelter-dog adoption is complex, and personality alone can't sufficiently determine it.
  • Do You See the Same Cat That I See? Inter- and Intra-Observer Reliability for Qualitative Behaviour Assessment as a Temperament Indicator in Domestic Cats: intra-observer reliability was high to very high for principal component 1 (PC1) and moderate to high for PC2/PC3; inter-observer reliability was high for PC1 but low for PC2/3. Individual concordance with the "gold" observer ranged from moderate to high. QBA could be a reliable temperament-assessment tool given the high intra/inter-observer reliability on PC1, the dimension explaining most of the behavioural variation in cats' temperament.
  • Evaluating ADHD Assessment for Dogs: A Replication Study: a questionnaire developed to assess ADHD-like signs in dogs showed good internal consistency and test-retest reliability for both subscales; agreement between owner and trainer ratings was fair to moderate. The Dog ARS is a reliable tool to assess ADHD-like behaviour, but in its current form is not suitable for detecting diagnosable individuals — it lacks items assessing functional impairment, and including owner-expert ratings in the evaluation process would be necessary.
  • Temperament Assessment Algorithm in Dogs: uses a temperament assessment algorithm to evaluate dog temperament from behavioural observation, emphasising 24 selected traits and behaviours.
  • The Effects of Owner and Domestic Cat (Felis catus) Demographics on Cat Personality Traits: housing type, total number of cats in the household, and owner animal preference all significantly affected many personality-trait scores. Unexpectedly, cat breed, owner age, neutering status, and country of residence showed distinct clusters in the multifactor model but had no significant effect on personality traits, as did coat colour, owner gender, and cat sex — contradicting some prior research.
  • What Will Happen to This Dog? A Qualitative Analysis of Rehoming Organisations' Pre-Adoption Dog Behaviour Screening Policies and Procedures: compared organisations' pre-adoption behaviour-screening practices and justifications with available scientific evidence, considering relinquishment risk, quality-of-life impact, and human-safety risk. Evidence supported 10 factors screened for; of those, 7 related only to relinquishment risk, 2 (resource guarding, knowledge of basic commands) to human-safety risk, and 1 (fearfulness) to both — meaning that for more than 85% of characteristics organisations deemed important to adoptability, supporting scientific evidence is lacking.
  • Reliability and Validity of Seven Feline Behavior and Personality Traits: the validity and reliability of the questionnaire and its factors were good, strengthening owner-completed questionnaires as a method for collecting behavioural data from pet animals; breed differences suggest a genetic background for personality.
  • Unanswered Questions and Hypotheses About Domestic Cat Behavior, Ecology, and the Cat–Human Relationship: degree of socialisation as a kitten likely affects cats' responses to positive/negative experiences with unfamiliar humans and their later formation of feline-human relationships — with major implications for shelter cat adoptions, though this needs ethically approved testing on cats of known socialisation status. Observations of human-cat interactions have yielded many correlations testable via non-invasive manipulation of human behaviour at home; a review of early social-cognition findings in cats raises further unanswered questions, and two aspects of domestic cat ecology (wildlife effects and space use) are considered, of interest to both the public and conservationists.
