Common Behaviour Problems

Common Behaviour Problems · Behavioral Health (16%) · Shelter Medicine

Exam cheatsheet for the Common Behaviour Problems subdomain — highest-yield traps, numbers, and decision rules; clarified points marked +.

Medical and Neurological Contributors, and Senior Pets

  • Brain regions — (limbic · forebrain · brainstem — signs when damaged)
  • Specific contributors — neoplasia · seizures · toxicosis · degenerative · inflammatory · urogenital
  • CCDS (canine cognitive dysfunction syndrome) — onset, pathology, reassess
    • DISHA screen: Disorientation · altered Interaction · Sleep-wake change · Housesoiling · altered Activity+
  • Treatment of CCDS — selegiline (MAOB-I, ↑ dopamine) · diet (Hill's b/d) · always add enrichment · adrafinil (wakefulness-promoting adjunct) can be tried alongside+
  • Rule out medical/neuro before labelling behavioural — change precedes severe neuro signs by weeks–months; brain = 2nd commonest neoplasia site
  • Tumours: primary (meningioma) = insidious/slow; secondary/metastatic = ACUTE; dogs >7y but age can't exclude
  • CCDS onset: dogs ≥11y (spatial memory 6–9y, frontal lobe atrophies first); cats 10–11y; reassess q6mo
TrapSelegiline NEVER with MAOIs, SSRIs or TCAs (also tramadol, SAMe, tryptophan, buspirone) → serotonin syndrome. Anxiolytic disinhibition can paradoxically ↑ aggression in a senior that would otherwise have retreated.

Compulsive Disorders and Stereotypies

  • Origins — normal behaviour → maladaptive (grooming, ingestion, predation, locomotion)
  • Risk factors — early environment · enrichment gaps · conflict · punishment
  • By category — locomotor · ingestive (pica) · self-trauma (ALD acral lick dermatitis) · hyperesthesia
  • Pharmacology
    • Clomipramine = most serotonin-selective TCA (tricyclic antidepressant); SSRI/TCA (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor) first line — continue ≥ 6 months+
    • If SSRI/clomipramine response is insufficient, memantine (NMDA-receptor antagonist) add-on has shown ~64% efficacy within 2 weeks+
  • Treatment principles — reward not punish · predictable routine
  • Onset prepubertal 3–6mo or pre-social-maturity 12–36mo, after conflict/change/distress; mechanism = abnormal serotonin → SSRI/clomipramine 1st line
  • SSRI/TCA monotherapy ↓ compulsive behaviour 50–75% by 4wk; may DOUBLE dose at 4–6wk if insufficient (watch serotonin syndrome)
  • Dopamine AGONISTS (apomorphine, amphetamines) INDUCE stereotypy; antipsychotics block them
  • Feline hyperesthesia hallmark: rippling THORACOLUMBAR skin + EPAXIAL spasm, can't interrupt; feline pica material order wool→cotton→synthetic→rubber→paper
TrapIf a compulsive disorder responds to meds, continue ≥6 months (often lifelong); premature taper triggers immediate relapse.

Separation Anxiety and Destructive Behaviours

  • Separation anxiety: diagnosis
    • Separation anxiety — distress signs bound to owner departure/absence (vocalising, destruction at exits, housesoiling, salivation)+
  • Prognosis and pharmacology — (cross-ref Behaviour Medications hub)
  • Four-pillar management — shape independence · ↓ departure/homecoming anxiety · decouple departure cues · anxiety reduction
  • Destructive behaviours — canine chewing · canine digging · feline scratching (visual mark + interdigital SCENT)
  • SA = dx of exclusion; 3 classic signs occur ONLY in owner's absence: vocalisation · destruction (at exits) · housesoiling
  • Meds: daily SSRI/TCA + benzo (alprazolam/diazepam) or clonidine 1h PRE-departure; fluoxetine/clomipramine 75% vs amitriptyline 56%
  • Mock departures ALWAYS shorter than the dog's anxiety latency; keep homecomings low-key, ignore until calm
TrapConfinement frustration mimics SA. True SA is consistent and occurs ONLY on departure — suspect another cause if signs occur while the family is home or only randomly.

Housesoiling and Marking

  • Canine housesoiling — incomplete house-training · submissive/excitement urination (never punish)
  • Feline housesoiling: three-way split
    • House-soiling triage: (1) medical → (2) inappropriate toileting (litter/box aversion, substrate/location) → (3) marking / spraying+
  • Marking (urine spraying) — conflict / anxiety driven
  • Marking treatment — ↓ stimuli · Feliway diffuser · SSRI/clomipramine
  • Punishment WORSENS housesoiling in both species; never punish submissive/excitement urination (improves with age)
  • Litterbox rule: 1 per cat +1; ≥1.5× cat length; scoop 1–2×/day; weekly hot-water clean (no strong disinfectant)
  • Marking posture: VERTICAL surface, tail ERECT, treads hind legs, sprays; postpuberty. Rx: fluoxetine 1mg/kg; clomipramine 0.25–0.5mg/kg = only cat-registered drug in AU
  • Feliway = FACIAL pheromone (environmental); Felifriend = ALLOMARKING (cat-to-cat intro) — different indications
TrapCastration ↓ spraying in 90% of males; spaying ↓ spraying in 95% of females.

Canine Aggression

  • Three umbrella categories — affective/social (offensive vs defensive) · predatory (moving stimulus, no arousal) · play (play-bow)
  • The 13 aggression types
    • Resource guarding — possessive aggression over food, objects, resting sites, or people+
    • Barrier frustration — aggression at a fence/window/leash barrier (frustration / redirected)+
  • Behavioural pathology criteria · Risk assessment and prognosis
  • Treatment plan (7 steps) — medical → educate → safety → management → BMOD → tools → meds; NO physical punishment
  • Fear body-language: tail DOWN, ears BACK, weight shifted AWAY; can look offensive behind a barrier or at distance
  • Resource-guarding onset ~16wk; adult onset if ↑appetite (reducing diet, hyperadrenocorticism); control usually in 2–3mo
  • Household intraspecific: same-sex, esp FEMALES; spayed females over-represented. Territorial (home/yard/car) vs protective (needs family PRESENT — friendly to strangers when family absent)
  • 6 prognostic factors: latency to arousal · environment · complexity · harm potential · predictability · emotional stability
TrapDominance is the MOST over-diagnosed type (needs confident temperament + assertive signalling + aggression to family WITHOUT fear). Most owner-directed aggression is really CONFLICT-related or RESOURCE GUARDING. Caution: OHE in females can ↑ reactivity/aggression toward family and unfamiliar dogs.

Feline Aggression

  • The 10 aggression types — (petting-induced, redirected, play, fear, territorial, inter-cat, maternal+ …)
  • Pharmacology — SSRI/TCA; buspirone can DISINHIBIT → ↑ aggression; benzos → HEPATOTOXICITY reports in cats
  • Play = MOST COMMON aggression toward owners; SILENT stalking; peaks 9–12mo; hand-raised highest risk; chase toys NOT hands (a same-age cat can be curative)
  • Petting-induced = 2nd most referred; bites over the LOWER BACK once stroking exceeds tolerance; respect the threshold, never restrain
  • Redirected = classic ACUTE hostility between amicable housemates; settling takes hours–days; SEPARATE + gradual reintro (scent before sight), never "fight it out"
TrapTwo amicable housemate cats suddenly fight → most likely REDIRECTED aggression from an outside trigger (cat at window, strange noise, visitor); identify and block the trigger.

Behaviour Forensics and Counselling

  • Behaviour forensics — fighting dogs · hoarder and puppy-mill dogs · evaluation protocol
  • Counselling and history-taking — history-taking protocol
  • Decision frameworks — 4-tier: management → BMOD → meds+BMOD → euthanasia; meds alone rarely durable for anxiety problems
  • Fighting dogs: LESS aggressive to PEOPLE, test dog–dog; OPPOSITE-sex stimulus FIRST then same-sex; intro through fence barrier, leashes slightly taut
  • 83% of ex-puppy-mill breeding dogs still show problems (fear, housesoiling, compulsive) after rehoming
  • Hoarding/neglect indicators — destructive chewing, excessive vocalisation/grooming, escape-related behaviour, multiple/generalised phobias, and signs of medical or behavioural deprivation; often against a backdrop of inconsistent household treatment+
  • Problem history = 5 Ws (what/where/when/who/why); adopter counselling ↑ success ~27.5%; 51% report >1 problem at 1wk; 2wk = problem-emergence window
TrapFake-dog test with a full-mouthed bite + hold/shake = unsafe around animals; with no evidence of BMOD efficacy, euthanasia is the recommended outcome.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Association between androgen receptor gene and behavioral traits in cats (Felis catus): the androgen receptor gene (AR) glutamine-repeat polymorphism (exon 1) was analysed in 280 neutered/spayed mixed-breed cats. Cats with short alleles had higher “purring” scores; short-allele males scored higher for “directed calls/vocalisations”, and short-allele females higher for “stranger-directed aggression”. Alleles of 20–22 repeats were cat-specific — cats cared for by humans from birth may survive without vocal communication, increasing long-allele frequency.
  • Before, during and after the pandemic: effects of the COVID-19 lockdown on welfare, behaviour and body condition of domestic cats: the Behavioural Score was higher before the pandemic than in the two later periods, i.e. more behavioural problems (e.g. restlessness) before lockdown. Overweight cats (BCS 4–5) were fewer when cats had free outdoor access and more physical activity; body condition score (BCS) and overweight numbers rose during and after confinement, as did attention-seeking behaviour.
  • Behavioral changes in domestic dogs associated with exposure to secondhand smoke: a cross-sectional study: dogs exposed to cigarette smoke showed more fearfulness, lower activity, more aggression toward people and other animals, poorer trainability and more anxiety — exposed dogs are at higher risk of behaviour problems.
  • Behavioral problems of Uruguayan domestic cats according to caretaker reports and demographic characteristics: 1/3 of caretakers reported ≥1 behaviour problem (68% reported 1, 28% reported 2, 4% reported 3 or more); 80% rated problems minor, 18% moderate, 2% serious. Most frequent: aggression, then coexistence problems with other animals/humans, shyness/fear, house soiling. Age significantly predicted prevalence; mature adults (7–10 yr) had the highest relative percentage of reported problems.
  • Burying and smelling — a comparative analysis of faecal and urinary elimination behaviours in neutered adult cats: cats invested more burying effort for faeces than urine, with females investing more than males after defecation. Faeces were deposited mainly in peripheral zones away from resting and feeding areas, while urine was spread more evenly. Sniffing occurred significantly more after defecation than urination in both sexes.
  • Cats in a Cat Café: individual cat behaviour and interactions with humans: elevated structures mattered most at high customer occupancy, when cats chose vertical levels or cat-only rooms. Cat–cat interactions were infrequent and mostly affiliative (avoidance = conflict reduction); cat–human interactions were absent for 44.4% of observation time, and cats ranged from engaging to avoiding. Cat cafés need complex vertical space, hiding places and private areas to reduce stress and support group stability.
  • Dog owners’ perceptions of their pet dogs’ behaviour when owners became pregnant: 5.4% of owners reported a behaviour change in their dog during pregnancy, and 26.9% said it began before they knew they were pregnant. Behaviours increased significantly in 4 of 5 categories. Only pre-pregnancy guarding around unfamiliar people and fear/anxiety toward other dogs predicted a reported change — no other dog, owner or pregnancy variable did.
  • Introducing new cats to multi-cat households: representative survey of Danish cat owners: 56% put cats together from the start; the rest used recommended techniques (separation, barriers, scent exchange, exploring each other’s areas, calming pheromones/supplements/food). Associations with later reactions were weak overall. When both cats were juvenile/young adult (under 4 yr), separation, area exploration, barriers, scent swapping and calming pheromones were associated with agonistic reactions in month 1; when both were mature (5+ yr), barriers, same-room play and extra resources were associated with affiliative relationships.
  • Owner-reported prevalence and severity of canine aggression in the United States: a descriptive study: 57.5% of dogs had ≥1 rating of level 2 (moderate) or higher severity. Aggression was directed at unfamiliar people in 38.8%, familiar people in 5.4%, unfamiliar dogs in 49.9% and familiar dogs in ~14.5%.
  • Understanding the influence of early-life stressors on social interaction, telomere length and hair cortisol in homeless kittens: kittens separated from their mothers had significantly higher hair cortisol and longer relative telomeres, with no correlation between hair cortisol and telomere length. (Telomere = protective repetitive DNA–protein region at chromosome ends.)
  • Unwanted feline scratching in the home: re-examining its relationship with stress and marking: contrary to the stress/marking view, scratching was more often linked to positive emotional states and normal maintenance behaviour than to distress or territoriality. Cats in dog-free homes scratched more — the opposite of a stressor-based account.
  • Canine separation-related behaviour at six months: dog, owner and early-life risk factors (‘Generation Pup’ longitudinal study): separation-related behaviours (SRBs) were less likely if puppies at ≤16 wk were restricted to crates/rooms overnight and slept ≥9 h/night. More likely with poor house-training at ≤16 wk, treats or novel kibble as training rewards, and more owner punishment/aversive techniques for ‘bad’ behaviour. Owners who ‘fussed’ over the dog on return after ‘bad’ behaviour had dogs 6× more likely to show SRBs.
  • Effects of a brief separation from the owner in the home: fearful vs control dogs: dogs explored more in phase 1 (owner present), directed more behaviour at the door in phase 2 (owner gone), followed the owner out at the end of phase 2, greeted on return in phase 3, and spent more time near the owner in phase 3 than phase 1. Reduced exploration during absence and incomplete recovery in phase 3 were possible stress indicators.
  • Impact of hypersensitivity–hyperactivity syndrome (HSHA) on quality of life of dogs and their owners: owners of HSHA dogs reported significantly lower quality of life (more stress, physical injuries, emotional distress). HSHA dogs had lower well-being and more injuries and digestive problems than controls — early diagnosis and effective management are needed to improve welfare and prevent relinquishment or euthanasia.
  • Safe or sound? Factors influencing outdoor access, cat behaviour and hunting history: of 631 owners near Davis, California, 36.7% kept cats indoor-only, 34.1% gave supervised/controlled access and 29.2% free-roaming access. Over half of outdoor-cat owners would reduce access near busier streets, with more dangerous animals, if the cat stopped coming home, or if it harmed wildlife. Higher odds of free-roaming: age 7–10 yr (vs under 1 yr), obtained from the street (vs shelter), within 500 ft (150 m) of traffic, living with a dog or child, or aggression at home. Lower odds: scratching areas at home or living with 1–2 other cats. Among outdoor cats, hunting in the last year was more likely with unsupervised free-roaming or outdoor access between 6:00–11:59 am.
  • The prevalence of behaviour problems in dogs in the United States: 99.12% of dogs showed ≥1 moderately serious–serious (3–4) problem or ≥2 moderate–serious (2–4) problems. Dogs with moderate–serious behaviours at least twice within a category: aggression 55.6%, separation/attachment 85.9%, fear/anxiety 49.9%, housesoiling 4.1%.
  • Unlocking friendships: the immediate post-adoption human–cat relationship and cat personality: of 133 respondents, 60.1% adopted females and 63.9% adopted cats ≤6 months old. 75.9% had doubts about initial care, 34.6% had no other cats, and 50.4% were unsure how to introduce the new cat to other pets. Common events: meowing and scratching/biting an adult (39.1% each). Problem personality combinations included agreeableness with hiding, not allowing touch, scratching/biting a child, skittishness, introversion with meowing, soliciting with inappropriate urination, and aggressiveness with skittishness.
  • US and Canadian cat caregivers’ ratings of cat–cat interactions: a video-based survey: overt positive interactions (allo-grooming, co-sleeping) were reported more for related dyads, longer co-living, neutered males, indoor-only cats and a single feeding area. Overt negative interactions (fighting, striking) were reported more for older dyads or larger age gaps, animal-directed aggression, declawed cats and a single litter box. Caregiver and expert ratings were similar, though caregivers rated some affiliative behaviours more positively.
  • Case report: androgen-secreting adrenocortical tumours in eight cats: hormonal abnormalities resolved and signs improved in the 4 cats that had adrenalectomy (all survived >1 yr); medical treatment, including trilostane, barely helped clinical signs or testosterone. Consider endocrine disease, with a detailed physical exam, in cats evaluated for inappropriate urination or aggression.
  • Feline litter box issues associated with cat personality, breed and age at sterilisation: fearful cats were more prone to both forms of litter box problem. Low sociability toward cats, male sex and being intact were associated only with house soiling; older age only with litter box fussiness. The most important variables (sociability toward cats, breed, activity/playfulness) did not reach the cutoff for even a small effect size.
  • A feline semiochemical composition influences the cat’s toileting location choice: cats urinated and defecated significantly less in the litter tray sprayed with the cat anal-gland–derived semiochemical than in the control-sprayed tray.
  • Can a semiochemical deter cats from trash cans?: 5 cats, crossover design, 10-min choice test between two garbage cans (one semiochemical-sprayed, one control): cats spent less time exploring and near the treated can.
  • Case distribution, sources and breeds of dogs presenting to a US veterinary behaviour clinic, 1997–2017: 72.2% presented for aggression, 20.1% for anxieties/fears/phobias, 7.4% for miscellaneous problems. Dogs from breeders decreased while dogs from shelters, rescues or strays increased over 20 years. Herding and terrier groups were over-represented over 10 years.
  • Cat–human bond: satisfaction and behavioural complaints among Italian respondents: house soiling was the most frequent complaint (4.7%), predicted by management factors (number of cats) and cat factors (age, anxiety, health problems). House soiling, aggression (intra- and inter-species), fear, hyperactivity and eating disorders reduced caretaker satisfaction; perceived health problems did not. Traits such as “being affectionate” increased satisfaction.
  • Dog-to-dog bites inside and outside the biting dog’s household: the biting dog’s sex and size influence which body area is bitten and need for medical treatment. Small dogs: higher odds of head bites outside the household (3×) and limb bites inside it (7.8×) vs large dogs. Females: 3.1× odds of head bites inside the household vs males. Victims of large dogs inside the household had 6.3× odds of seeking treatment vs victims of small dogs; victims of male dogs outside the household had 2.5× odds vs victims of females.
  • Dogs exhibiting high levels of aggressive reactivity show impaired self-control: police dogs showed higher aggression and poorer self-control than privately owned dogs, with no difference in cognitive inhibition. Across groups, high aggressive reactivity was linked to impaired self-control, and biting dogs had worse self-control than non-aggressive dogs; cognitive inhibition was not associated with aggression.
  • How cat-behaviour advisors can improve clients’ willingness to adopt their advice: highly credible advisors elicited more positive attitudes and compliance intentions than less credible ones. Mild advice produced stronger compliance intentions than severe advice (guardians felt better able to do it). Guardians who strongly identified as cat guardians were more likely to adopt advice, believing other guardians would too.
  • Impact of changes in time left alone on separation-related behaviour in UK pet dogs: SRB prevalence fell from 22.1% to 17.2% (Feb–Oct 2020) as time left alone fell, but 9.9% of dogs developed new SRB signs by the October follow-up — dogs whose time alone decreased most during lockdown were at greatest risk.
  • Influencing elimination location in the domestic cat: a semiochemical approach: 4 of 6 parameters (daily elimination weight, urine weight, stool weight, elimination type, urine/stool quantity scores) showed a treatment effect — cats defecated significantly less in litter trays sprayed with the treatment than with control.
  • Owner-ascribed personality profiles distinguish domestic cats that capture and bring home wild prey: hunting cats that brought home prey scored high for ‘extraversion’ or low for ‘neuroticism’ vs non-hunters. The number of prey items brought home was not affected by any personality factor.
  • Relationships among morphological, environmental and social factors and aggressive profiles in Brazilian pet dogs: using the C-BARQ (Canine Behavioral Assessment and Research Questionnaire): weight, skull morphology and sex, plus owner gender and age, training regimen and household habitat, significantly affected the probability of no aggression vs high aggression. Aggression was not associated with body mass index. Behaviour reflects genetics, physiology, life experience and environment interacting through development.
  • The behavioural impact on cats of transitioning from a clay-based to a plant-based litter: 6 days may be adequate to transition a cat to a new litter, though success may be specific to the litters tested.
  • Effectiveness of professionals and behaviour modification programmes, with or without medication, for canine aggression: aggressive dogs were often mixed breed, ~1/2 male (most neutered), and the sole dog in the household. Anti-bark collars and muzzles decreased the probability of successful treatment. 36% received behaviour modification (BMOD) using desensitisation and counter-conditioning (DSCC). Improved dog–owner communication, habituation, relaxation protocols and short, frequent training sessions were the most consistently beneficial techniques. No significant association between treatment response and specific medications.
  • Behavioural and physiological correlates of the Canine Frustration Questionnaire (CFQ): behavioural test measures (e.g. vocalising, lunging) correlated positively with CFQ scores. In 22 dogs with normal baseline salivary cortisol (<4 ng/mL), cortisol change and post-test cortisol correlated positively with the CFQ ‘Frustration coping’ score (PC5).
  • Changes in health and behaviour of pet dogs during the COVID-19 pandemic as reported by owners: owners in lockdown/quarantine were 1.8× more likely to report dog behaviour changes (more negative than expected) and 2.6× more likely to report health changes. Overall, changes were more often positive (30%) than negative (24%). Dogs were a support: 65% reported reduced tension and 47% valued dog walks. Dog-care difficulties rose for those in lockdown or without a garden.
  • Characteristics of plant eating in domestic cats: very few cats appeared ill before eating plants, but 27–37% (two surveys) frequently vomited afterward, suggesting GI disturbance in some. Young cats ate plants more often and appeared ill/vomited less than older cats. Short- and long-haired cats ate plants equally, arguing against the hairball-expulsion hypothesis.
  • Chewing behaviour in dogs — a survey-based exploratory study: 94% of owners provided edible chews, 83% inedible chew toys, 73% food-filled toys and 51% hard chewing material; edible chews were given 4–6×/week. 67% never had a problem from chew materials; veterinary treatment for a chewing problem was reported by 3.6%. Frequency of chew provision correlated positively with motivation to play, chewing objects, human–dog play and calm activities such as petting. Owners considered chews important for puppies and even more for adults.
  • Comparing behavioural problems in imported street dogs and domestically reared Danish dogs: former street (FS) dogs showed 9 of 45 listed behaviours more often than Danish-reared dogs — all related to fear, aggression and stress. Most common: fear of humans, stress, and problems when left home alone.
  • Do dog–human bonds influence movements of free-ranging dogs in wilderness?: older dogs, males and dogs that went missing more often made more intensive forays. Owners of free-ranging dogs are probably not important attachment figures, yet the dog–owner bond does influence roaming.
  • Effect of fluoxetine at 2–4 mg/kg daily in dogs with hypersensitivity–hyperactivity syndrome (HSHA): retrospective study: improvement was high or perfect in 48/89 dogs for motricity, 63/89 for sleep and 44/89 for satiety. 54/89 (61%) had adverse events, very few severe. Fluoxetine 2–4 mg/kg PO q24h may be a safe, effective treatment for some dogs with severe HSHA.
  • Laser light pointers for companion cat play: association with guardian-reported abnormal repetitive behaviours (ARBs): laser-play frequency was significantly associated with all surveyed ARBs except overgrooming. Indoor-only cats and young (1–2 yr) cats were more likely to show ARBs. Strongest links: chasing lights/shadows, ‘obsessively’ staring at lights/reflections, and fixating on a specific toy.
