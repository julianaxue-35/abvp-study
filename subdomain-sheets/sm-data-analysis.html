Data & Analysis

Data & Analysis · Shelter Management (18%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield cheatsheet — surveillance, epi metrics, LOS/ALOS + C4C formulas, and live-release-rate methods. Clarified points marked +.

Disease Surveillance

  • Two required parts — (1) ongoing systematic collect + analyse health data, AND (2) prompt dissemination to those who can act+
  • Purpose — quantify pop health, set measurable disease priorities, catch outbreaks early, justify grants/funding
  • 8 steps — short monitoring list → baseline endemic rates → annual medical profile → high-incidence (FURI) ≥ monthly → count only FIRST episode/period → separate denominators (intake) & numerators (medical) → monthly review
  • Prereqs — individual animal ID + working medical records + standardised disease definitions + prompt reporting
  • Snapshot to report — incidence/prevalence of major infectious dz, natural-cause mortality, % euthanised for medical reasons
Trap Collecting + analysing but NOT promptly disseminating to those who can act is not surveillance — both parts required.

Basic Metrics

  • Incidence vs prevalence — incidence = NEW cases over a time period; prevalence = ALL active cases at a point in time+
  • Incidence = new cases ÷ susceptible at-risk pop; a RATE over TIME
  • Prevalence = snapshot PROPORTION at a POINT; counts new + existing active cases
  • Morbidity = illness frequency; mortality = NATURAL deaths only (euthanasia is a separate outcome category)
  • Recurring disease — only the FIRST episode/period counts as incident; later episodes in same animal excluded (else rate inflated)
Trap Census finds 15/100 cats with URI today = 15% PREVALENCE, not incidence — incidence needs new cases tracked over a defined period.

Data Integrity Rules

  • Denominators = intake data (animals at risk); numerators = medical data (diagnosed)
  • Keep clinical signs vs definitive dx in SEPARATE fields — else one episode double-counts
  • #1 cause of data failure = failure to reach a definitive diagnosis (undiagnosed can't be counted)
  • Quality over quantity — collect appropriate, good-quality data only; don't waste effort on poor-quality or unused data that won't inform decisions+
Trap Crude unadjusted cross-shelter incidence comparisons mislead — confounded by intake profile, geography, facility design, species mix.

Length of Stay & ALOS

  • Length of stay (LOS) — welfare indicator + capacity lever; ↓ ALOS ↑ intake capacity with the same housing+
  • ALOS = Σ LOS of all animals ÷ number contributing, per stated period; compute dogs/cats & subgroups separately
  • ↑ LOS → ↑ disease risk, ↑ stress/behavioural decline, overcrowding
  • Stratify by source, health status, breed, season, volunteer availability to isolate the bottleneck
  • Reducing ALOS is the highest-leverage lever — improves welfare, cuts disease risk, adds capacity with zero new housing
Trap Court-hold subgroup ALOS 45 d vs 5 d for surrenders → fix the legal/procedural impediment, don't stop intake from that category.

Capacity for Care Formulas

  • Daily admissions = humane housing units ÷ ALOS (days) (C4C = capacity for care)+
  • Intake capacity = (humane housing spaces × days) ÷ ALOS
  • Halving ALOS DOUBLES capacity with same housing — 60 units ÷ 6 d = 10/day (300/mo); at 3 d = 20/day (600/mo)
  • NACA staffing floor = 15 min/animal/day, feeding + cleaning ONLY; a MINIMUM, not a target
  • Staffing audit — (census × 15 min) target hours vs actual hours = the shortfall; understaffing → biosecurity breaches → ↑ disease
Trap The 15-min NACA standard is bare survival — medical care, enrichment, behaviour & socialisation all need staff time ABOVE it.

Three-Axis Analysis of Disease Frequency

  • Sudden ↑ disease frequency = protocol breakdown (or need for a new one); analyse all 3 axes together
  • Host — age, vax status, intake source → which subpops are susceptible
  • Location — holding ward vs adoption floor, iso vs general → transmission routes, housing design
  • Time — season/kitten season, post-event, staffing change → precipitating & sustaining causes

Live Release Rate

  • Live release rate (LRR) — core outcome KPI; live outcomes ÷ total outcomes (ASV Terminology 2017: 3 calculation methods)+
  • M1 — live outcomes ÷ total live intakes (intake-based; OIE categorisation shifts denominator)
  • M2 — live outcomes ÷ total outcomes, excluding current residents (disposition-based)
  • M3 — (total intakes − euthanasia) ÷ total intakes (tracks reform progress)
Trap LRR is comparable across shelters/time ONLY if the same method is used — same data yields different rates by method.

Data Collection, Analysis & Communication

  • Key performance indicators (KPIs) — track LRR, ALOS, incidence, mortality; report on a set review cadence+
  • Written protocols specify WHAT · WHERE · WHEN · BY WHOM; everyone trained + accountable for accuracy
  • Stratify incidence by age/source/time — an aggregate rate hides a high-risk subgroup
  • Monthly reviews sustain data quality + timely admin notification; active outbreaks → more frequent
  • Front-line staff insight needed to interpret the numbers — new cleaning protocol, housing change, low-volunteer period
Trap A low overall incidence can mask a very high rate in one subgroup (e.g. neonatal kittens in a single ward).

📄 Journal Articles

  • Model selection — dog intake vs socioeconomic & geographic conditions: In urban areas, higher owner-unknown (stray) intake was associated with lower education level and greater cultivated-land area; in non-urban areas it was higher where there was more vacant housing, more cultivated land and higher minimum temperature. Urban owner-relinquished intake was associated with more occupied housing, while non-urban owner-relinquished intake was higher where there were more low-income households.
  • Portuguese shelter trends 2018–2024 (intake, LOS, outcomes): 58.9% adopted, 41.1% in long-term housing (capacity pressure). Length of stay (LOS) was the strongest adoption predictor — longer stays sharply cut adoption odds. For dogs, female sex and owner or animal-protection-association surrender raised adoption likelihood. Microchipped animals were 71× more likely to be returned to owner, though few entered with identification. Live release rate 79.7%, save rate 84.7%, population balance calculation 75.6% → reduce LOS, enforce microchipping, apply species-specific management.
  • Socioeconomic status & relinquishment reasons: Median Index of Relative Socio-Economic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) decile of relinquishers = 4/10 (below-average status). Cats 59.4%, dogs 40.6% of relinquishments; more from lower deciles 1–5 (cats 62.6%, dogs 65.8%). Median age of relinquished cats 5 months, dogs 16 months. Human-factor issues (housing, financial, human health) cited in 86%. Financial reasons were more common in lower socioeconomic groups; human-health and family issues more common in higher groups.
  • Social vulnerability & shelter intakes/outcomes: Animals tended to enter from higher-vulnerability areas but were adopted out to homes across all vulnerability levels. Strays and authority-seized animals were more likely to come from high social-vulnerability-index (SVI) areas and showed a larger intake–adoption SVI gap than owner surrenders. Animals from high-SVI areas were NOT euthanized at higher rates than those from low-SVI areas.
  • Geographic transitions of urban cats via adoption centres: Intake was mostly stray cats (59%) plus owner-relinquished (38%); outcomes mostly adoption (84%). Highest intakes from high-development, low-income neighbourhoods; lowest from low-development, high-income. Most outcomes went to high-income neighbourhoods, trending toward homes further from the shelter. Returned cats came more often from homes close to the shelter, and returns were more common in high-income, high-development areas. Return-to-owner (RTO) proportion was higher in low-income areas near the shelter and high-income areas distant from it.
  • Reasons behind relinquishment — content analysis 2018–2023: Of 2836 records, top reasons were behaviour issues (28%), housing/moving (18%), unable to care (16%), too many pets (10%), financial (6%), owner allergies (5%). Top behavioural reasons: aggression (32% of behavioural surrenders), social conflict (28%), soiling (13%). Housing/moving and financial reasons were unchanged since the pandemic, but "unable to care" rose significantly in 2022–2023.
  • What helps dogs stay shorter (Polish shelter): An accelerated failure time model was preferred for studying LOS. Male sex, mixed breed, dark fur, large size and older age were associated with longer time to adoption.
  • Consistency of surrender intake information (mixed-method): Of 40 possible surrender reasons, the unique reasons chosen per scenario ranged 12–16 — wide variation for the same narrative. Agreement was also low for breed and colour. Staff used varied methods, e.g. asking the owner for their most influential reason vs inferring the underlying reason.
  • Foster-rescue vs cat-café illness & LOS: Café cats had significantly higher sickness frequency and slightly longer LOS than foster-care cats. Sex and housing status were significant LOS variables; foster cats and male cats were adopted faster, and kittens had significantly shorter LOS than adults. In the café, negative behavioural and medical comments by staff significantly influenced LOS.
  • Animal sheltering — scoping review (institutional ethnography): Euthanasia-related stress dominates staff research, yet focus-group participants emphasized decision-making tensions rather than the act itself. Relinquishment research relies heavily on surveys and database records with significant limitations. LOS studies focus on animal characteristics (breed, colour), overlooking work processes like veterinary treatment and behaviour modification.
  • Big data in commercial dog-breeding oversight: Demonstrates harnessing big data to inform regulatory decisions and improve welfare in commercial dog breeding, introducing a method to transform publicly available data into an accessible format.
  • Dutch cat shelters 2006–2021: Feline intake fell ~39% per 1,000 residents and euthanasia declined ~50%. LOS trended down and RTO rose alongside the risk-based live release rate.
  • Stray & owner-relinquished cats in Australia (2018–19): Estimated 179,615 admissions (7.2/1,000 residents) to pounds, shelters and rescues — ~5% reclaimed, 65% rehomed, 28% euthanized; reclaim rates low across all agencies. Councils running their own pound had nearly double the euthanasia rate (~46%) vs welfare organizations (25%). Rescues rehomed ~35% of all cats rehomed. Upper-quartile councils with >50 cats in Victoria and New South Wales had estimated euthanasia rates of 73–98% and 67–100%.
  • Taiwan shelter trends 2012–2020: Intakes and outcomes fell significantly over time. In-shelter euthanasia was positively associated with intakes/outcomes as it facilitated flow-through. Adoption and trap–neuter–vaccinate–return replaced euthanasia as the main outcomes; each rise in human fertility rate increased the monthly adopted-to-intake ratio by 1.10%. Veterinary workload exceeded the legal limit (100 animals/vet) in two counties in 2018, rising to six counties by 2020.
  • Cat coat colour, personality & the cat-owner relationship (Mexico): 211 owners; cats generally perceived as bold and friendly. Gray cats scored highest for shy/aloof/intolerant, orange for trainable/friendly/calm, tabbies for bold/active, tricolor for stubborn, bicolor for tolerant. Cat–owner interaction correlated positively with active/friendly personality and negatively with aloofness; emotional closeness positively with active/bold/friendly; perceived cost negatively with boldness.
  • Direct & indirect factors influencing cat outcomes: Four hypotheses predicted cat outcomes from (1) cat characteristics, (2) location of origin, (3) type/date of intake, (4) LOS. Modifying outcomes (e.g. raising adoption probability) is most effective when multiple factors are combined.
  • Emergency dog fostering during COVID-19: Foster use rose March→April 2020 then returned to baseline by June. Just under two-fifths of foster carers were community members with no prior shelter tie, and these were >4× more likely to adopt their foster dog; carers fostering with intent to adopt did so ~75% of the time. Low-resource shelters relied on people with prior relationships while high-resource shelters recruited new community members; the lowest-resourced shelters transferred more dogs out, whereas more-resourced shelters rehomed directly to adopters.
  • COVID-19 fostering boom — ephemera or chimera?: Foster-carer numbers rose during the pandemic, especially among people working from home, with higher education, younger, male, and without their own dog.
  • US shelter intake/outcome trends 2016–2020: Total intake and euthanasia fell significantly for both dogs and cats. Adoption, RTO, return-to-field and (for cats) transfer as a percentage of intake all rose significantly. Live release rate (by total intakes and by total outcomes) rose significantly for both species.
  • Web-based RTO-focused data-analysis tool: 70% of returned-to-owner strays traveled at most 1 mile (1.6 km) from home and 42% within a block; at-large adult strays with a microchip had a 71% RTO rate vs 39% without one.
  • Pet-ownership rates in four underserved communities: Rural communities had an 11.5% higher overall pet-keeping rate and a 19% higher dog-ownership rate than urban communities, while cat ownership was similar across all four sites (~19.4%). Overall community-specific pet-keeping rate 56.8%.
  • Dog euthanasia vs live release factors (US): A size × geographic-region interaction was associated with the hazard of euthanasia (p=0.0204); age group and skull type were each associated with euthanasia vs live release (p<0.0001). Phenotypic characteristics of dogs predict their euthanasia hazard.
  • Predicting LOS & optimal shelter allocation (two-phase): Dog age (puppy, super-senior), multicolour, and both large and small size were important predictors of LOS.
