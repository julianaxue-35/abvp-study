Disaster & Emergency

Disaster & Emergency · Companion Animal Homelessness (12%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield cheatsheet for the Disaster & Emergency subdomain — federal law, four phases, ICS/NIMS/NRF, six missions, ownership rules & Katrina reunification. Pre-filled points marked +.

Key federal legislation

  • PETS Act (2006) (Pets Evacuation and Transportation Standards Act) — mandates regional planning authorities & communities include companion animals in emergency planning (a statutory mandate, not guidance)+
  • Stafford Act (Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief & Emergency Assistance Act) — amended post-Katrina; plans must address pets & service animals; authorises FEMA funding/resources for household pets+
  • Good Samaritan immunity — shelter vets in emergency response get the same immunity as any vet; does not waive competent-care or documentation duty.
  • FEMA course familiarity recommended for all shelter vets who may respond.
Trap · PETS Act = mandate, not guidanceAn option calling companion-animal inclusion optional or discretionary is wrong — it is a statutory mandate.

Types of disasters

  • Natural — hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, fires, earthquakes.
  • Human-made — large-scale cruelty cases, workplace violence, toxic/chemical spills, HAZMAT.
  • Advance planning needed for both — protects animal welfare and human health/safety.

11 animal categories requiring emergency planning

  • Service animals — must ALWAYS stay with their owner per the ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act); the only category with an absolute co-housing mandate+
  • Family pets — keeping them safe drives owner/community mental health & evacuation compliance.
  • Livestock — commercial (community economics) + backyard/hobby; accommodate or evacuation fails.
  • Working animals (law-enforcement/SAR) — strong handler bond, manage as handler team units.
  • Wildlife & facilities (zoos/aquaria); animal facilities (vet hospitals, shelters, research, breeders); travelling events (circuses, petting zoos).
  • Animals used illegally (hoarding, fighting) surface during disasters; free-roaming/feral (box-trapped = extremely fearful).

The four phases of disaster management

  • 4 phases (cyclical) — Mitigation → Preparedness → Response → Recovery; mitigation is ongoing+
  • Mitigation — ongoing/preemptive; risk assessment (hazards, vulnerabilities, consequences).
  • Preparedness — written plan + drills + resources; must cover animals in foster; NRF/NIMS/ICS training done before disaster.
  • Response — initiate ICS as soon as disaster is anticipated (not after declared); intake = cursory exam, vax (rabies)+parasite control, decontamination.
  • Recovery — days to years; locals can't foster/adopt → adoption events outside area, transfer, shelter-neuter-return; debrief + update plan.

The ICS / NIMS / NRF hierarchy

  • ICS General Staff (Incident Command System) — 4 sections: Operations · Planning · Logistics · Finance/Administration (report to the Incident Commander)+
  • NRF — national roles/relationships; escalation ladder local ICS → state → federal when overwhelmed.
  • NIMS — overall system that ICS operates within; train before disaster.
  • EMAC — 50 states share personnel/equipment laterally before federal escalation.
  • CART/SART — community/state animal response teams deploy trained personnel via ICS; set up MAA/MOU with local govt before disasters.
  • Command Staff (Safety, Liaison, Public Info) ≠ General Staff.
Trap · phases are not ICS sectionsMitigation/preparedness/response/recovery are the 4 disaster phases, NOT ICS General Staff sections.

Unified Command vs. Unity of Command

  • Unified Command — multiple agencies/jurisdictions co-author ONE Incident Action Plan; no agency loses legal authority (top of structure).
  • Unity of Command — every responder reports to ONE supervisor; prevents conflicting orders (field level).
Trap · conflicting field orderOrder from another agency's responder → keep your assigned task, report only to your designated supervisor. Unified Command does not override Unity of Command in the field.

The six emergency response missions

  • 1 Activation + rapid needs assessment — via lead agency/ICS; shelters report pet numbers, capacity, needs.
  • 2 Evacuation/transport — owners have primary responsibility; move animals + owners together; protect from temp extremes.
  • 3 Sheltering — 3 models: co-located, cohabitation, pet-friendly.
  • 4 Vet medical response — out-of-state transfer needs vax/testing/CVIs + state-official sign-off; out-of-state vets need licensure.
  • 5 ASAR (animal search & rescue) — coordinate with human SAR; may continue after human SAR ends.
  • 6 Decontamination — soap-and-water bath then clean rinse = gold standard; coordinate HAZMAT + Safety Officer.
Trap · OSHA respirator = 3 steps, all pre-incidentMedical screening → fit test → training, ALL completed before the incident (not during response).

Ownership issues during disasters

  • Ownership unknown — animal is handled as a stray until claimed; consult legal counsel (stray laws + lost-property statutes) before adopting out or fostering+
  • Owner known — get permission to treat, release of liability, indemnification, records, + definite term (forfeit if not renewed); owner covers costs as able.
  • Holding times = stray-animal laws PLUS lost-property statutes.
  • At intake — licensed vet exams every animal; photo + physical ID; document all care throughout custody.
Trap · no fast-track adoptionConsult legal counsel before adopting/fostering disaster animals; immediate adoption of unclaimed animals is wrong.

Hurricane Katrina: reunification rates and the shelter effect

  • ~200,000 pets displaced in the aftermath.
  • No temporary pet shelter → only 5% reunited (one note cites 15–20%).
  • Temporary co-located/pet-friendly sheltering → 40.2% and up to 70.1%.
  • Fukushima 2011 — abandoned dogs' urine cortisol 5–10× controls, still high at 10 wks; trauma not rapidly reversible.
  • Trauma profile — uncontrollable aversive events → generalised fear/arousal, hypervigilance, passive avoidance/resignation.
  • Human safety = first priority of any response plan; animal stress ↑ dog-bite risk.
Trap · high-yield stats 5% → 40.2% → 70.1%Temporary pet-inclusive sheltering is the single most impactful intervention for owner-pet reunification.
