Adoption & Placement

Adoption & Placement · Companion Animal Homelessness (12%) · Shelter Medicine

High-yield cheatsheet — open/conversational adoption, canine & feline decision science, evidence-based marketing, and post-adoption returns; pre-filled points marked +.

The Conversational Adoption Philosophy

  • Open / conversation-based adoption outperforms restrictive, high-barrier income screening; a return is a learning opportunity, not a screening failure.+
  • Two founding assumptions — every adopter is an individual (strengths + weaknesses); adopters mean well and can learn good care.
  • Counsellor builds a partnership, not gatekeeping — align the adopter's expectations to the specific animal.
  • Returned animal → re-adopt to same person as a better match, or convert them to foster / advocate.
  • Matching programs (deliberately matching adopter to pet) → better outcomes but may increase ALOS (average length of stay)+
Returns are not failures Blacklisting returners or framing returns as screening failures is contrary to conversational philosophy.

Adoption Fees & Income Requirements

  • No evidence that reduced or waived adoption fees worsen owner–pet attachment or increase return rates; income thresholds are not evidence-based welfare safeguards.+
  • Reduced fees → no change in adult-cat owner–pet attachment and no change in return rates.
  • Low advertised price is a top predictor of which shelter a visitor chooses to visit.
Income thresholds Premium fees / income screens as a welfare safeguard have no evidence base — they cut access without improving outcomes.

Visitor Dynamics & Geographic Catchment

  • Fast-track / spontaneous adoptions are retained as well as planned ones — no long-term retention difference, so do not slow adopters down.+
  • Most adopters search within 30 mi (48 km); up to 90 mi (145 km) is the exception — collect zip codes at the door.
  • 2/3 rule — up to two-thirds of visitors don't intend to adopt that day; a good experience converts them to future adopter / foster / volunteer.
  • Show all animals (even not-yet-available) → builds public trust and partnership.

Canine Adoption Decision Sequence

  • Two phases — browsing is morphology-driven (in-kennel training has no effect); the meet-and-greet is behaviour-driven, where calm proximity, lying down, and a single favourite toy nearly doubled adoptions.+
  • Browsing: visitors stop at ~1/3 of dogs, 15–70 s each; only ~1% taken out; morphology = small, puppy, long/light coat.
  • Meet-and-greet: avg 8 min, ~1/3 adopted; small enclosed area = highest rate; teach lie-down calm next to the bench/chair.
  • Over-arousing play before the meet-and-greet works against adoption — train relaxation, not play.
In-kennel training Has NO effect on browsing (only morphology predicts stopping) — invest training in meet-and-greet skills.

Feline Adoption: Environmental & Labelling Factors

  • Label bias — cats labelled "owner-surrendered" are adopted faster than "stray" despite identical behaviour; black cats show a longer LOS (length of stay).+
  • Frequency of viewing the same cat (not duration) predicts adoption; top-tier cage + toy = more interest.
  • Vertical cage + shelf + towel hide → lower stress + better outcomes; horizontal single-housing = fewest adoptions, most stress.
  • Clicker-train cats to approach the front of the pen and show exploratory (vs inactive) behaviour — presenting as friendly/active raises adoptability+
  • Preferred: kittens, exotic status, unique patterns, lighter colours; black cats wrongly judged less friendly / more aggressive.
Owner-surrender vs stray The label alone speeds adoption — one of the most exam-tested points; not explained by behaviour.

Online Profile Best Practices

  • Photo: outdoor, animal standing, direct eye contact; add a toy for cats. Video rates cats more adoptable than photos alone.
  • Remove breed labels — NYC study: reduced LOS for all dogs, no change in returns.
  • Use concrete/analytic wording, not narrative; "make you proud, independent, lively, eager, clever" shorten LOS; avoid "only dog, dominant, sensitive."
  • Mere-exposure effect → repost often to boost appeal; never use "overlooked / still here / waiting 6 months" (stigmatising, lengthens LOS).
Warnings belong in counselling Matching caveats in the public profile raise LOS — reserve for individual counselling; never blame the previous owner.

Return Rates & Timing

  • First week = highest-risk window — ~41% of dog returns occur in week 1; ~20% of adopters no longer own the pet at 1 year, and only ~49% of those are returned to the shelter.+
  • Return rate 7–24%; behaviour = leading cause of non-retention (54–74% of returned dogs vs 38% of returned cats).
  • Return timing (dogs) — after wk 1 (~41%), week 2 ~27%; ~37% are lost from home between 2 weeks and 2 months+
  • ~50–70% of adopters report a behaviour concern within week 1.
  • Non-returners cite fear of being judged a failure → position the shelter as a non-judgmental resource.

Aggression & Return Risk

  • 11× → 5.5× — with no behavioural advice, caregivers are 11× more likely to return a dog for human-directed aggression; basic management advice halves the risk to 5.5×.+
  • Human-directed aggression is the adopter's primary concern.
  • Let adopters actively practise skills with the animal during counselling — not passive verbal instruction.
Halves, not eliminates "With advice" = 5.5×, not 1× — an option saying advice removes return risk is wrong.

Key Counselling Points at Adoption

  • Dogs = 5: housetraining · SRP prevention · intro to resident pets · physical/mental stimulation & training · individual personality.
  • Cats = 4: litterbox · scratching posts · settle-in time · intro to resident pets.
  • A 5-min housetraining talk at adoption → greater success at 1 month; keep it to a few high-priority topics (compliance drops if too many).
  • SRP (separation-related problems) in ~30% of adopted dogs; top-compliance tips = no interaction right before leaving + food-filled toy when left alone.
  • Post-adoption support — offer ongoing help via website info, a designated phone line / email, and follow-up calls+
5 dog vs 4 cat Swapping the counts or adding a 5th cat point is a classic wrong option.

📄 Journal Articles

  • A qualitative exploration of owner experiences following dog adoption: 27 interviews using thematic content analysis. Six themes were described: adjustment period duration; behavioural indicators during adjustment; behaviours indicative of adjustment; factors influencing adjustment; owner change in routine; and adjustment concerns. More than 50% perceived the adjustment period to last more than 4 months. Behaviours noted include behavioural extremes (lethargy and restlessness), play, tail wagging, greeting, and learning their routine. Prior experience and medical needs impacted the adjustment duration; owners also adjusted aspects of their lifestyle and raised concerns about being the right fit for their dogs.
  • Challenges and Benefits of Cat Fostering: A Focus Group Study With Volunteer Cat Fosterers in Aotearoa New Zealand: Study aimed to explore the motivators of volunteer cat fosterers, challenges identified and the perceived benefit of being a foster caregiver. Thirteen foster caregivers participated in the focus group, data were analysed using qualitative content analysis. Primary motivators included a general love for animals, wanting an alternative to cat ownership, and the desire to help and make a difference, which was underpinned by altruistic values. Cat foster caregivers experienced social and emotional benefits along with the satisfaction of making a meaningful impact on their fosters’ lives. The benefits fulfil the basic psychological needs of autonomy, relatedness, and competence.
  • Investigating the relationship between canine training classes and post-adoption return rates in North American shelters: Aim of the study was to investigate the relationship between attending post adoption training classes and return rates. 204 dogs attended training, results showed no significant difference in return rates between dogs who attended training and dogs who did not attend training. All returned dogs, those did not attend training were more likely to be returned for animal-related reasons, the ones attended training classes may help to reduce behavioral returns, shown by the shift in return reasons.
  • New Strategies of Canine Post-Adoption Support: Methods for a Prospective Longitudinal Cohort Study: Longitudinal data on dog health and behavior following adoption to assess the development of issues, the impact of support given, and relinquishment risk. Data collected from 5498 adopters, call success ranging from 72% at 4 months to 89% at 2 days post adoption.
  • No Callback Required: Themes and Factors Associated with New Adopters Declining Free Dog Behavior Support: Recent dog adopters experienced at least one behaviour of concern (potentially aggressive behaviour, SRP, or other behavior concern). For 68.8% of calls, the behaviour support offered was declined. It appears there is declining support significantly more likely at 2 weeks post adoption compared to 2 days.
  • Not-So-Prestigious: Social Status Sensitivity Reduces the Adoptability Ratings of Non-Pedigree Cats in an Online Task: People scoring high on perceived status attainment and social status showed a reluctance to adopt cats presented in the non-pedigree condition, though it did not translate into an increased preference for pedigree cats. Cuteness ratings followed a similar pattern with adoptability, as the perception of cuteness of non-pedigree cats declined with heightened status sensitivity. While cuteness remained relatively constant across different PSA scores in the pedigree condition, higher SA slightly increased the reported cuteness ratings of pedigree cats.
  • Observed Benefits of a Rehome Web Application For Harder-To-Adopt Pet Populations: The analysis focused on harder to adopt animals, defined as senior cats, dogs and large dogs, and compared adoption rates between animals listed on rehome and those surrendered to shelters. Animals on rehome – the website had longer LOS than animals in the shelter. Animals on rehome had lower probabilities of adoption at any given time compared to those in shelters. Overall, adoption rates were higher in shelters, although considerable variability existed among shelters, whereas rehome’s performance remained relatively stable. In some regions, rehome had higher adoption rates than the corresponding local shelter.
  • Optimising Shelter Outcomes Using Technology, Foster Care and External Networks A Community Case Study of Community- Centred Adoptions in an Australian Cat Shelter: Between 2018 – 2024, shelter based adoptions reduced from 80 – 60%, with adoption through foster care, pet shops and community engagement becoming increasingly important. Many different online programs that help to streamline remote adoption processes, eliminated the need for in person visits and reduced adoption barrier, notably decision fatigue. Offsite adoption doubled compared to pre-pandemic levels, peaking at 40%, with foster care consistently outperforming pet shop placements. The median onsite stay for cats and kittens was 6 days in 2023 and 28 days for those in foster care; in 2024, shelter stay reduced to 5 days and 24 days for those in foster care.
  • People and Pets in the COVID-19 Pandemic and the Cost-of-Living Crisis: Identifying Trends in the Intake, Adoption and Return of Companion Animals During Times of Uncertainty: Nearly 200,000 animals were taken in by the RSPCA and over 140,000 animals were adopted or released over the four-year period from 2018 to 2022. When controlling for the brief closure of RSPCA sites, fewer dogs and cats were taken in and adopted during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic, and the intake and adoption of animals were lower during the COLC than before. A downward trend in the return of animals was observed, and the principal reasons for return were problem behaviours, owner unpreparedness and a change in circumstances.
  • Post-Adoption Behavior and Adopter Satisfaction of Cats Across Socialization Likelihoods: Prospective behavioral study surveyed adopters of cats assessed via FSA and a randomly sampled control group of cats with intake and adoption rates during the same period. Adopters of FSA cats reported being slightly less satisfied than control cat adopters. As the likelihood of being undersocialized increased, cats were more fearful, less affectionate, and less amenable to approach and petting (more so by strangers, but also by their owners), and adopters were less likely to report a traditional home environment as the best environment for their pet than control cats. Cats never suspected of being undersocialized experienced better welfare than undersocial cats, and their adopters are more satisfied. Despite there is an increased chance to find adoptive homes for poorly socialized cats, but it doesn’t appear to be in the best interest of many of these cats or adopters. FSA 1+ 2 cats should be slated for TNR/barn placement unless significant progress is made in shelter.
  • The Effect of Kennel Viewing on the Adoption of Slow-Track Dogs: Kennel viewing enhances opp for increasing adoptions of dogs with LOS 9 days. Adoption counts increased, and the adoption rate increased compared to the time when viewing was inhibited.
  • The role of breed and personality descriptions in influencing perceptions of shelter dog adoptability: Descriptions significantly increased perceived adoptability, while breed labels decreased it. Specific breed designations like chihuahua mix, chow mix, and JRT mix -ve influenced adoption interest, whereas lab mix improved ratings. Personality descriptors such as affectionate, calm and friendly boosted perceived adoptability, notably energetic reduced adoption interest. The research suggests removing breed labels and incorporating +Ve personality descriptions to enhance adoption outcomes and potentially reduce LOS.
  • Understanding the behaviour of adopted northern Canadian community dogs through a mixed-methods approach: Study was done via C-BARQ survey, and an exploratory factor analysis was performed. A substantial proportion of owners reported that northern dogs displayed behaviour problems post-adoption, which may stem from dogs experiencing frustration, related to a lack of control over their new environments and a misalignment with their previous reality.
  • A Mixed Methods Exploration Of Opportunities For Barriers And Bias During Off Site Animal Adoption Events: A total of 484 participants reported on how they conduct adoption events and their adopter selection procedures. Commonly, adoption events were held weekly in locally owned stores, with volunteers engaging directly with potential adopters. < 50% of respondents that adopters could take pets straight home from the event, adoption process does not require a home visit (57.7%). Generally larger organization held adoption events more frequently, were more likely to let adopters take animals straight home, and did not require a home visit. Both types of organization showed a potential for implicit bias when selecting adopters.
  • Black Dog Bias in the Courtroom: A Potential Disadvantage?: Black dogs are often viewed as less friendly, less emotionally stable, and more aggressive than their other-colored counterparts. Other research, however, argues that BDB does not exist; rather, bias against certain dogs is associated with phenotypic traits or breed. Child witness when comforted by a black dog, appeared to be less sad, less emotional, and happier, suggesting that transference did not occur.
  • Comparison of the Number of Dog Adoptions in a Pilot Program That Restored Limited Visitor Access to Kennels: A Community Case Report: The study found substantially higher adoptions during viewing periods compared to other times. Specifically, kennel viewing periods showed an 82% increase in adoptions while appointment-only periods showed only a 4% increase relative to the previous year. Viewable kennels produced a disproportionately high adoption rate for large dogs.
  • Effects of single- or pair-housing on the welfare of shelter dogs: Behavioral and physiological indicators: Pair housed group demonstrated greater reduction in cortisol levels and had shorter adoption timeframes by ~ 4 days. Well matched pair housing can have both proximate and ultimate welfare benefits for shelter dogs, with minimal aggression concerns and evidence of affiliative behaviors between compatible pairs.
  • Exploring Factors That Influence Public Engagement of Adoptable Pets on Facebook: Findings suggest that posts on social media can successfully promote animals available for adoption, but the type of animal (dog vs cat) and environment (home vs other) can influence the likes and shares of the posts. COVID 19 and a shift to more online activity may have influenced engagement, posts from fall 2019 had more likes, while posts in fall 2020 had more shares.
  • Rabbit intakes and predictors of their length of stay in animal shelters in British Columbia, Canada: The majority of rabbits were surrendered by owners 40.2%, with most rabbits being surrendered for human-related reasons (96.9%). Rabbit intake was found to be the highest in May. Most rabbits were adults (46.7%), non-brachycephalic (66.7%), erect-eared (82.5%), short furred (76.2%), and subsequently adopted (80.3%). The median LOS of rabbits was 29 days, highlighting the pressing need to improve their time to adoption. The intake year, intake month, source of intake, age, cephalic type and breed size significantly predicted time to adoption for rabbits.
  • The Increasing Adoption Potential of Undersocialized Cats and Kittens- Blessing or Curse?: Adopters of 134 kittens identified as undersocialized and 591 control kittens (not suspected of being undersocialized), were surveyed about their kitten’s behaviour and owner satisfaction. Control kittens were rated as less fearful and more likely to engage when approached by a stranger/enjoy a stranger petting, but no difference in ratings of affection, relationship with their owner, adopter satisfaction or what environment the adopter states the kitten would be happier. Results showed although it is becoming more possible to place undersocialized cats in homes, these environments may still be prone to fear, particularly towards strangers, but both the kitten and owners have good QOL and satisfaction post adoption.
  • Too much too soon? Risk factors for fear behaviour in foster kittens prior to adoption: The main behavioural outcomes of this study were whether kittens showed caregiver-reported fear behaviour during interactions with unfamiliar people and unfamiliar objects just prior to adoption. Kittens who were reported fearful at intake into foster care had significantly greater odds of displaying fear behaviour towards unfamiliar people and objects than kittens who were not reported fearful at intake. Kittens who received exposure to cognitive enrichment based non-social stimuli had greater odds of displaying fear towards people compared to kittens who did not receive cognitive exposure. Kittens whose foster parent had a high score for emotional stability had greater odds of displaying fear towards people compared to those who had a low score for emotional stability. Kittens received high exposure to social stimuli had reduced odds of displaying fear towards people than kittens who received low social exposure, as predicted. High social exposure was ineffective at reducing fear lvls towards unfamiliar objects and high cognitive enrichment based non-social exposure appears to have exceeded kitten limits for optimal behavioural development.
  • Cross-sectional survey exploring current intake practices for dogs admitted to animal shelters in Texas: a descriptive study: Staff reported varying handling approaches based on dog demeanor, using treats and gentle techniques for calm animals, but escalating to muzzles and catch poles for aggressive dogs. After exams, ~ 45% housed dogs on adoption floors.
  • Factors Affecting the Likelihood of Dogs and Cats Returning to Their Owners at a Municipal Animal Shelter in the United States: Dogs and cats that were microchipped, older, healthy, or neutered, and animals brought to the shelter via another public agency were more likely to be returned to their owner (RTO). Animal sex and season of impound did not affect either dogs' or cats' RTO likelihood.
  • Shelter dog behavior after adoption: Using the C-BARQ to track dog behavior changes through the first six months after adoption: CBARQ questionnaire was sent on day 7, 30, 90 and 180 post adoption. High prevalence rates for various behaviours, including stranger-directed aggression (81.7%), owner directed aggression (32.3%), dog directed aggression (75%) and SRP. Over the study period, stranger directed aggression, excitability, touch sens, training difficulty, and chasing increased, while SRP and attachment decreased. Psychotropic meds used in shelters predicted increased stranger-directed agg post adoption.
  • The Influence of Brief Outing and Temporary Fostering Programs on Shelter Dog Welfare: Brief outings and temp fostering stays increased dogs’ likelihood of adoption by ~ 5 and 14 X respectively. While intervention dogs had longer pre-intervention shelter stays, post intervention stays averaged just 10 days before adoption. Programs performed better with greater community involvement and adequate resources.
  • Understanding the Motivations of Foster Caregivers at Animal Shelters: The 18- to 29-year-old caregivers were most likely to expect companionship, emotinoal support, and to meet other community members through fostering. Male caregivers were less likely to foster for emotional support than females, while dog caregivers and non-pet-owners had greater odds of expecting companionship.
  • When and Why Cats Are Returned to Shelters: Cats that are older at the time of adoption or had a bite history had an increased risk of return, whereas cats that were in foster care prior to adoption had a decreased risk of return. ~ 50% of the cats were returned in the short term (<30 days). Cats were more likely to be returned for reasons such as behaviour, unwanted, and other pet in the short term and personal reasons, costs, euth, and stray in the long term return.
  • Decision Factors Considered By Potential Dog Adopters During Shelter Visitation: Decision making process during dog adoption found dog behaviour was the top decision factor that potential adopters considered, which was significantly greater than chance. Behaviour was followed by the dog's age and lifestyle fit.
  • Increasing Engagement in Kitten Fostering Programs: Lessons Learned From High Kitten Intake Zip Codes in Los Angeles County: Foster caregivers included 283 predominantly Hispanic/Latinx adults aged 18+ who resides in LA county and lived in one of 12 zipcodes with a high rate of kitten shelter intake. Survey showed that more than ¼ of participants had engaged in fostering on their own without an animal shelter/rescue program. 1/3 of the total sample, > 2/3 of participants who had already fostered cats and kittens on their own were open to fostering kittens in partnership with an animal shelter. Majority of individual who were interested in fostering had not seen ads for fostering programs. Spanish-language participants were significantly less likely than expected to have encountered program advertisements. Most prevalent concerns about fostering were centered on the time, cost and space rquired to engage in fostering. Text, email, social media and mail were among the most preferred methods for marketing and communications, with some variation between Spanish- and English-language respondents. Should consider promoting program ads using animal welfare and cost-focused messaging approaches and improving the dissemination and marketing of Spanish-language materials.
  • Intensive Adoption as a Management Strategy for Unowned, Urban Cats: A Case Study of 25 Years of Trap–Assess–Resolve (TAR) in Auckland, New Zealand: 14,611 unowned cats in 2019 trapped, 64.2% were adopted, 22.2% were euth if unsocialized or in grave ill health, 5.7% were neutered and returned to the site, and 7.9% had other outcomes, such as being transferred to other shelters. Adoption rates increased over this time, > 80% in 2018 & 2019. The cost of processing each cat from capture to adoption rose from NZD 58 in 1999 to 234 by 2017. ~ 80% of colonies were around residential areas. ~ 22% of cats required vet treatment after capture, common ailments included furi, rw, dental, and trauma. 52% were young kittens < 10 weeks old, 80% were< 1 year old, only 2% were estimated to be > 5 years old.
  • Investigating the effects of matching to caretakers’ preference on satisfaction in different types of adopted dogs using the Kano model alongside with more traditional methods: This study aimed to investigate whether matching to caretakers’ preference in some dog characteristics was associated with an increase in caretakers' declared satisfaction with their dogs.   • Selection Factors Influencing Eventual Owner Satisfaction about Pet Dog Adoption: Odds of eventual satisfaction are higher for participants who spent less than a week considering an adoption or were seeking a pet to provide companionship and affection.   • Single vs multiple cat adoptions: A trade-off between longer adoption times and social bonding in shelter cats: Cats who are adopted as part of a multi-cat outcome spent 3 days (42%) longer on the adoption floor than those adopted individually, independently of other factors such as age and coat colour. This difference increased to 13 days (185%) longer if the cat had a notification indicating they must be adopted together with another cat. While behaviour data show that these pairs of cats engage in significantly more affiliative behaviour with each other than cats who were adopted singly, there was a large discrepancy between which pairs the shelter classified as multi-outcome and those who would be classified that way based on behaviour alone. Decisions to place cats together should be made carefully given the potential adverse impacts of keeping cats in the shelter together.
  • The Effect of Baby Schema in Cats on Length of Stay in an Irish Animal Shelter: Baby schema (characteristic facial features of infants, related to perceived cuteness) has an effect on cats' length of stay (LOS) in an animal shelter. Subjective cuteness in survey 1 was the only variable of influence on LOS. A novel objective cuteness measurement was developed which reflects the eye shape associated with subjective cuteness scores. Cuteness may not be as influential on cats LOS in shelters as hypothesized.
  • Characterizing unsuccessful animal adoptions: age and breed predict the likelihood of return, reasons for return and post-return outcomes: Behavioral issues and incompatibility with existing pets were the most common return reasons. Age and breed group (dogs only) predicted the likelihood of return, return reason and post-adoption return outcome. Adult dogs had the greatest odds of post-adoption return and post-return euthanasia. Toy and terrier breeds were 65% and 35% less likely to be returned compared with herding breeds. Pit bull-type breeds were more likely to be returned multiple times and euthanized post-return.
  • Good-looking vs. Obedient, Which Would You Rather Take Home? The length/colour of the coat and sex had no effect on early adoption. Behaviour was also important during the prospective adopter/owner's interaction with the dog.
  • Shelter and rescue programmes associated with higher live release and lower return rates for dogs: The study identifies three key factors associated with improved results: avoiding breed designations for mixed-breed dogs, maintaining robust foster networks, and implementing matching systems.
  • The role of artificial photo backgrounds of shelter dogs on pet profile clicking and the perception of sociability: 4.74% of presented slides culminated in participant link-clicking. Sociability scores also did not predict link clicking. Assessment of participant-related factors and dog identity revealed that link-clicking and sociability scores of photographs were influenced by differences between dogs themselves and unaffected by participants’ awareness of study hypotheses. We conclude that artificial background types did not affect participant responses.
