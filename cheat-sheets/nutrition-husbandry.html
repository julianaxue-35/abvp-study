Nutrition & Husbandry

Nutrition & Husbandry · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Shelter nutrition must be complete, balanced & life-stage-appropriate — feed to energy needs, score every animal by palpation, and respect the feline metabolic quirks (obligate carnivore, poor glucuronidation, hepatic lipidosis from fasting) that turn routine husbandry into an emergency. Quality of life (QOL) is the net balance of an animal's pleasant vs unpleasant feelings.

Complete & Balanced Diets (AAFCO)

  • Feed one diet: a complete, nutritionally balanced, life-stage-appropriate diet of the highest quality possible, fresh water always available — consistency reduces stress, inappetence & GI (gastrointestinal) upset.
  • AAFCO statement: the AAFCO (Association of American Feed Control Officials) nutritional-adequacy statement on the label shows how the diet meets requirements (must not be expired); "complete & balanced for all life stages" suits puppies/kittens, adults & pregnant/nursing animals.
  • Dog vs cat: dog = practical omnivore (a meat-only diet is not balanced); cat = obligate (true) carnivore needing higher dietary protein plus pre-formed taurine, arginine, vitamin A & arachidonic acid (cannot synthesise them adequately from plants).
  • Food recalls: manufacturer- or FDA (US Food and Drug Administration)-initiated; remove recalled product immediately — never feed it.
⚠ Trap: a taurine-deficient diet in cats causes dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) + central retinal degeneration — the classic obligate-carnivore distractor against dog nutrition.

Life-Stage & Energy Needs (RER/MER)

  • RER (resting energy requirement) = baseline energy at rest; MER (maintenance energy requirement) = RER × a life-stage/activity factor.
  • Calculate RER & MER for over- or under-weight animals; formal MER calculation is accurate but seldom practical at shelter scale.
  • Calorie density: ~350–500 kcal per cup of dry food; ~120–190 kcal per 5.5 oz (156 g) can of wet food.
  • Treats — 10% rule: treats should not exceed 10% of daily caloric intake.
+ check: some notes allow "10–25%" of calories as treats; the accepted ceiling is the 10% rule — use 10%.

Feeding Management & Schedules

  • Adult dogs: portion-feed ≥1×, ideally 2×/day. Large, deep-chested dogs fed ≥2×/day to lower GDV (gastric dilatation-volvulus / bloat) risk.
  • Puppies <6 mo & high-demand dogs (pregnant, nursing, underweight, debilitated) — fed more frequently or offered dry food free-choice by age/condition.
  • Adult cats: ≥2 meals/day; many prefer small frequent meals. Meal-feed when intake must be controlled or monitored.
  • Group-housed cats: free-choice dry is practical (canned spoils at room temp) but non-eaters go unnoticed for days & some overeat.
  • Cohoused feeding: feed separately or monitor closely — watch for guarding/bullying so every individual gets enough.
  • Turnover: confirm food is eaten & not spoiled; discard uneaten food ≥ every 24 h; discard contaminated/pest-infested food immediately.

Body Condition Score (1–9)

  • Scale: 9-point BCS (body condition score, Nestlé Purina scale) — 1–3 underweight, 4–5 ideal (target 5/9), 6–9 overweight-to-obese.
  • Ideal 5/9: ribs palpable but not visible under a thin fat layer; waist & abdominal tuck present.
  • Palpate, don't eyeball: a thick coat hides ribs; in cats also palpate the caudal-abdomen fat pad (cats deposit fat there early).
  • Surgical gate: BCS ≤3/9 needs veterinary clearance before general anaesthesia (low reserves, hypothermia, altered drug metabolism); stabilise BCS 1–2 before elective surgery.
  • Monitor: weigh & score at intake, then weekly for stays >7 days; flag the medical team if BCS drops ≥1 point.
  • Obesity: BCS 6–7 → cut calories 20–25%; BCS 8–9 → structured program (~RER at target weight), recheck q2wk. Safe loss 1–2% body weight/week.

Anorexia, Refeeding & Hepatic Lipidosis

  • Time thresholds: adult cat off food 3–5 days → hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver) risk; kitten <4 months off food >24 h → hypoglycaemia, weakness, death — examine promptly.
  • Food aversion: nauseated cats link a food's sight/smell to illness; offer food periodically rather than leaving it in the cage constantly.
  • Refeeding syndrome: refeeding a chronically starved animal too fast shifts phosphate, potassium & magnesium → weakness, seizures, coma, death. Reintroduce slowly with veterinary input & electrolyte monitoring.
  • Obese-cat trap: sudden food restriction mobilises fat faster than the liver clears it → hepatocyte fat accumulation. Never fast an obese cat aggressively; aim for gradual 1–2%/week loss. Signs: jaundice, vomiting, lethargy, ↑ liver enzymes (ALT [alanine aminotransferase], ALP [alkaline phosphatase]) & bilirubin.
⚠ Trap: a BCS 8/9 cat eating little for 5 days — the wrong answer is "continue caloric restriction"; correct = veterinary exam + nutritional support (high hepatic-lipidosis risk).

Neonatal & Orphan Feeding

  • Rewarm first: never feed a hypothermic (cold, core <94°F / 34.4°C) neonate — a cold gut can't move milk, which ferments → endotoxaemia; restore normothermia, then feed.
  • Environment: week 1 ~85–95°F (29–35°C), drop ~5°F (~2.8°C)/week to 70–75°F (21–24°C) by week 4.
  • Cadence: tube/bottle q2–3 h for the first days, q4–5 h to 3 weeks, then 4×/day to 6 weeks; wean at 3–4 weeks with warm semisolid slurry.
  • Volume: stomach capacity ≈ 40–50 mL/kg per feeding — never exceed (over-distension → reflux, emesis, fatal aspiration).
  • Orphans: weigh daily the first week & stimulate elimination; ~100 g at birth, gain 10–15 g/day (<7 g/day inadequate). Screen the dam/queen for FeLV (feline leukaemia virus) & FIV (feline immunodeficiency virus).
  • Fading kitten syndrome: hypothermia → GI ileus → gut fermentation/endotoxaemia → hypoglycaemia (<60 mg/dL) + dehydration → collapse. Rule out feline panleukopenia; necropsy is best for a definitive diagnosis.

Reproduction & Kitten Age Estimation

  • Feline basics: queens are seasonal, induced ovulators; cycle ~q2wk in season, receptivity ~4–7 days; gestation ~65 days, 3–5 kittens. Puberty: queens 5–9 mo, toms ~8–13 mo.
  • Pregnancy diagnosis: palpation day 14–17; ultrasound as early as day 10; serum relaxin ↑ by day 25; radiographs only after fetal skulls calcify ~day 43. Obvious distension → birth usually <2 weeks away.
  • Pound-a-month: kittens gain ~1 lb (~0.5 kg)/month, so a 3 lb (1.4 kg) kitten is ~3 months old; eyes open 7–10 days, walk/play 3–4 weeks.
  • Dentition (heavily tested): deciduous teeth erupt ~3–4 weeks (incisors in order I1, I2, I3); adult canines erupt ~5.5–6 months. Permanent canines + small testes ≈ 6–9 mo; mature testes ≈ 12–14 mo.
  • Housing limit: kittens <6–8 weeks should not be housed in the shelter (infection risk) — place in foster.

Food & Water Safety / Storage

  • Water needs: cats ~50 mL/kg/day; dogs ~50–80 mL/kg/day; fresh water always available. Dry-only cats need ~2× the water of wet-fed cats (wet food = 70–80% water) → concentrated urine & urinary-tract disease risk.
  • Dehydration: ≥5% = skin tent >1–2 s, tacky/dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes; ≥10% = depression, weak pulse, prolonged CRT (capillary refill time).
  • Dry food storage: sealed, pest-proof containers; label the date opened; use within the manufacturer's period.
  • Wet/canned: refrigerate opened cans; discard uneaten portions after 2 h at room temperature (4–6 h max under the feral-cat protocol).
  • No raw diets: the ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) advises against raw feeding in shelters — Salmonella/Campylobacter shedding, staff & immunocompromised risk, seldom balanced, documented outbreaks.
  • Bowl hygiene: individual bowls preferred (biosecurity); scrub food & water bowls daily — biofilm/algae = contamination; wash-disinfect-rinse between occupants.

Housing & Husbandry Basics

  • Cat units: every unit needs ≥1 hide box + an elevated resting surface. Hiding is negatively correlated with cortisol — a welfare solution, not a problem; don't remove the hide box because "the cat hides." Cats usually adjust in 2–5 weeks (some never).
  • Feral/fractious cats: squeeze panel + hide box; feed, water & clean from the safe side, minimising handling. Disposition is TNRM (trap-neuter-return-monitor) — don't hold ferals long-term (chronic stress).
  • Geriatric & grooming: soft bedding + warmth for orthopaedic disease; regular brushing/bathing limits faecal-oral spread (giardia, parvovirus).
  • Rabbits & exotics: moved to their own sheets to keep this one focused on dogs & cats — see the Rabbits and Exotic & Other Animals cheatsheets.

Shelter Toxicology

  • Feline metabolic basis: deficient hepatic glucuronidation (the UGT1A6 glucuronosyltransferase gene is a pseudogene; other UGT isoforms are low) slows clearance of phenols, aspirin/salicylates & acetaminophen (paracetamol) and contributes to permethrin sensitivity.
  • Permethrin/pyrethroids: dog flea spot-ons misapplied to cats → tremors, muscle fasciculations, hyperthermia, seizures — the classic feline toxicosis.
  • Analgesics: acetaminophen → methaemoglobinaemia + Heinz-body anaemia; aspirin & ibuprofen also unsafe in cats.
  • Household/plant: onions (incl. onion-powder baby foods) → haemolytic anaemia; lilies → nephrotoxic to cats.
  • Disinfectant residue: quaternary ammonium (QAC), phenols & pine oils groomed off fur → caustic oral/glossal ulceration, hypersalivation, anorexia. Avoid rodenticides in shelters (poisoned rodents can die in cat cages & be eaten).
+ check: it is a glucuronidation deficiency, not a total absence; and feline acetaminophen toxicity is multifactorial — deficient glucuronidation plus a lack of N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2), with the p-aminophenol pathway driving methaemoglobinaemia.
⚠ Trap: dog-labelled permethrin spot-on applied to a cat — the mechanism answer is the feline glucuronidation deficiency, not receptor density or haemoglobin fragility.
