Parasites

Parasites · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Shelter parasite control protects the population, the public, and each animal's adoptability. High-yield levers: know which stage is immediately infective (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) vs which needs environmental development; match the drug to the spectrum (fenbendazole misses Dipylidium & Echinococcus → praziquantel); and never forget the zoonoses — larva migrans, sarcoptic mange, Cryptosporidium.

Control Principles & Transmission

  • Three shelter concerns — protect the individual & population (limit environmental contamination); protect staff / visitors / adopters from zoonoses; stop untreated parasites reducing adoptability or burdening new owners.
  • ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) intake mandate — treat all dogs & cats for roundworms + hookworms at intake, from 2 weeks of age (zoonotic risk). Whipworm / tapeworm treatment at intake is often cost-prohibitive → targeted. Every dog should leave with a product effective vs hookworms + roundworms.
  • Broad-spectrum heartworm / flea spot-ons must NOT replace a primary dewormer.
  • Direct (single host) vs indirect (≥2 hosts / a vector). Indirect (Dipylidium via flea, heartworm via mosquito, Taenia via prey) cannot establish facility-wide without the vector / intermediate host → control the vector.
  • Immediately infective when shed → Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Tritrichomonas. Need environmental development first → Toxocara, Ancylostoma, Strongyloides, Trichuris, coccidia, Toxoplasma.
  • Environmental persistence — ascarid (roundworm) eggs are extremely resistant (nearly impossible to kill once in soil; bleach / ammonia work mainly by disrupting the outer coat → desiccation). Hookworm eggs die by desiccation; whipworm eggs hardy (bipolar plugs).
⚠ Trap: immediately-infective agents (Giardia / Cryptosporidium) demand isolation-level daily sanitation; ascarid / hookworm / whipworm / coccidia are shed non-infective, so no isolation is needed — but post-treatment feces are heavily contaminating and need prompt cleanup.

Deworming & Formulary

  • Pediatric schedule — deworm puppies from 2 wk, kittens from 3 wk, every 2 weeks to 12 wk, then monthly to 6 months; treat pregnant & nursing dams too.
  • Pyrantel pamoate — hookworms, roundworms; cheapest (cats 10 mg/kg); retreat in 2 wk then monthly. Safe shelter mainstay.
  • Fenbendazole — hookworms, roundworms, whipworms, Giardia, most tapeworms; 50 mg/kg PO (by mouth) once daily × 3–5 d. Does NOT cover Dipylidium or Echinococcus.
  • Praziquantel — tapeworms including Dipylidium; 5–7.5 mg/kg oral or injectable.
  • Milbemycin oxime — hook / round / whip plus heartworm prevention (a macrocyclic lactone).
  • Combinations — Drontal Plus (pyrantel + febantel + praziquantel); Profender (emodepside + praziquantel, feline).
  • Reduce Toxocara canis in pups by treating the pregnant bitch with ivermectin / doramectin at day 40–50 of gestation — reduces but does not eliminate shedding.

Intestinal Endoparasites nematodes

  • Hookworm (Ancylostoma caninum) — percutaneous, oral, paratenic & transmammary (A. caninum only); prepatent ~2 wk → nursing pups can shed & develop life-threatening regenerative anaemia / melena by ~14 days. A. braziliense causes most cutaneous larva migrans in the US.
  • Roundworm (Toxocara canis / cati, Toxascaris leonina) — egg ingestion, transplacental (T. canis), transmammary; T. canis prepatent ~5 wk (in-utero pups shed by ~3 wk). Leading cause of visceral + ocular larva migrans.
  • Baylisascaris procyonis — raccoon ascarid; severe / fatal neural larva migrans in humans (the deadliest).
  • Whipworm (Trichuris vulpis) — soil eggs, caecum / colon; prepatent ~3 mo; barrel-shaped egg with bipolar plugs.
  • Diagnosis — zinc-sulfate centrifugation fecal (test of choice); intermittent shedding + long prepatent periods → a single negative does not rule out infection.
  • Strongyloides stercoralis — zoonotic threadworm; percutaneous + transmammary; thrives in damp / soiled runs; passes first-stage larvae, not eggs → recover by Baermann; now rare with monthly macrocyclic-lactone preventives.
  • Ollulanus tricuspis — feline "vomit worm"; gastric nematode transmitted via vomitus not feces → diagnose by examining vomit, not a fecal float.
⚠ Trap: zoonotic larva migrans by agent — Toxocara = visceral / ocular; Ancylostoma braziliense = cutaneous; Baylisascaris = neural (often fatal).

Tapeworms & GI Protozoa

  • Dipylidium caninum — flea = intermediate host, so good flea control controls it; visible segments mean flea control needs review. Taenia uses prey → unlikely to cycle in a shelter. Echinococcus = significant zoonosis, not covered by fenbendazole → praziquantel.
  • Coccidia (Cystoisospora) — host-specific (cat coccidia don't infect dogs / humans); disease of naive pups / kittens. Ponazuril (toltrazuril sulfone) 20–50 mg/kg PO once daily × 1–3 d is coccidiocidal = drug of choice; sulfadimethoxine is the only labelled option but merely coccidiostatic. Cellular damage (signs) occurs before oocyst production → sick animal, negative float.
  • Cryptosporidium — C. canis (dogs) / C. felis (cats), host-adapted; oocysts immediately infective. Resistant to bleach / chlorine → use 6% hydrogen peroxide, steam >60 °C (140°F), or desiccation. Diagnose by modified Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) stain; no reliable drug — hygiene is key.
  • Toxoplasma gondii — cat = the ONLY definitive host; sheds oocysts only ~2 wk during acute infection; oocysts need >24 h to sporulate, so daily litter removal clears them before infective. A seropositive cat sheds less. Treat disseminated disease with clindamycin.
  • Neospora caninum — dog = definitive host (raw beef / venison); NOT zoonotic; congenital pups get ascending flaccid hind-limb paralysis ~3 wk; treat all littermates.
⚠ Trap: fenbendazole is broad but misses Dipylidium & Echinococcus — those need praziquantel.

Giardia & Tritrichomonas

  • Giardia duodenalis — flagellate; trophozoite (falling-leaf motility, two nuclei "owl-eyes", ventral disc) + cyst (hardy, transmissible). Cysts immediately infective when shed → rapid ward / fomite spread; survive in cool, moist environments & standing water; reinfection from cysts in the coat.
  • Assemblages A–H — dogs C/D, cats F (host-adapted); A & B are the potentially zoonotic ones, but risk is generally low.
  • Signs — small-bowel diarrhea (soft, pale, fatty, mucoid); many are asymptomatic shedders.
  • Diagnosis — saline wet-mount (trophozoites) + zinc-sulfate flotation (cysts; check several samples) + antigen ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) / SNAP. Confirm cure by flotation, NOT antigen (stays antigen-positive after cyst shedding stops).
  • Treat — fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h × 5 d + environmental control; treat clinical disease, not shedding; bathe on day 1 & at the end; if persistent, repeat the fenbendazole cycle — do NOT switch to metronidazole (equal efficacy but causes dysbiosis / chronicity, esp. juveniles). Cysts resist quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs) → kill by drying, steam / heat >60–70 °C (140–158°F), or accelerated hydrogen peroxide.
  • Tritrichomonas foetus (cats) — chronic large-bowel diarrhea (fresh blood, mucus, tenesmus) in young congregate-housed cats that look well between bouts. Diagnose by fresh direct smear (forward rolling, single axostyle), InPouch TF culture, or PCR (polymerase chain reaction); ~22–54% co-infected with Giardia. Treat ronidazole 30 mg/kg PO once daily × 14 d (reversible neurotoxicity possible).
⚠ Trap: metronidazole treats Giardia but NOT Tritrichomonas (use ronidazole) — and it isn't even first-line for Giardia (fenbendazole is).

Heartworm Disease

  • Agent / cycle — Dirofilaria immitis; two-host cycle: mosquito (intermediate host) matures microfilariae to infective L3 over 10–14 d → dog (definitive host) L3→L4→adult in the pulmonary arteries, prepatent ~6 months. A dog is its own reservoir; cats & ferrets are not.
  • Diagnosis — the antigen test detects circulating antigen of adult FEMALE worms (primary screen); microfilariae are found separately by a concentration test (Modified Knott). Run both (some dogs are antigen-neg / microfilaria-pos). Don't test before ~7 mo. Dogs on preventives can be false-negative (antigen–antibody complexes) → heat treatment of serum unmasks antigen. Look-alike: Acanthocheilonema (Dipetalonema) reconditum microfilaria = false positive.
  • Staging — thoracic radiographs are most objective: enlarged, tortuous, truncated pulmonary arteries; severe disease → classic "reverse D" silhouette.
  • Treatment (AHS American Heartworm Society) — melarsomine = only FDA-approved adulticide (worms ≥120 d), deep intramuscular into epaxial lumbar (L3–L5); the 3-dose protocol is safest / most effective (~99% vs 90.8%). Doxycycline 10 mg/kg BID (twice daily) × 28 d suppresses Wolbachia (endosymbiont essential to the worm), cutting pathology & making microfilariae non-infective. Strict exercise restriction throughout.
  • 30–120-day gap — macrocyclic lactones kill worms ≤30 d; melarsomine ≥120 d. Starting the full protocol at diagnosis clears all stages — delaying does not help.
  • "Moxi-doxy" — cost-constrained non-arsenical option (topical moxidectin monthly + doxycycline until antigen-negative). Caval syndrome — heavy burden obstructs the tricuspid valve → fatal in ~2 d unless worms are surgically removed.
⚠ Trap: the antigen test detects adult-female-worm antigen, not microfilariae — the reason AHS runs an antigen and a microfilaria test together.

Feline & Ferret HW · Prevention

  • Cats — low worm burdens; an infected cat poses no transmission risk to other cats. Melarsomine is NOT used (too risky) — supportive care + prednisolone. HARD (heartworm-associated respiratory disease): even a single worm's death can trigger a fatal acute pulmonary reaction / sudden death.
  • Feline diagnostics — no single test is sensitive; combine antibody + antigen + imaging. Antibody detects male OR female worms from ~2 mo (exposure only). Antigen detects only adult female, ~5.5–8 mo; 30–50% of infected cats have no female worm → antigen-negative. Routine screening is NOT indicated in asymptomatic shelter cats.
  • Ferret — highly susceptible; a single worm can be catastrophic (tiny heart); antigen often false-negative; bilirubinuria is the most consistent finding.
  • Prevention — ASV endorses AHS: year-round prevention for dogs, cats & ferrets. Macrocyclic lactones (ivermectin, selamectin, milbemycin, moxidectin) reliably kill only worms ≤30 d. A treated dog isn't "heartworm-free" until two antigen tests 6 months apart are negative.
  • Permethrin blocks mosquito transmission on relocated dogs — but is NEVER used on cats (severe / fatal toxicity). DEET is unsafe in dogs, cats & ferrets.
⚠ Trap: permethrin is fatal to cats — never apply a canine permethrin spot-on to a cat (or to a dog housed in close contact with cats).

Ectoparasites: Mites & Lice

  • Host specificity — only lice & Demodex are strictly host-specific; all other arthropods are opportunistic feeders. Lice survive only days off-host; nits aren't killed by insecticides → mechanical removal, treat ≥2× a week apart.
  • Sarcoptes scabiei var. canis — highly contagious, zoonotic (transient in people); intense pruritus (pinnae, elbows, ventrum); positive pinnal-pedal reflex; scrape has low sensitivity → treat on suspicion; isolate + PPE (personal protective equipment) for the first 3–5 d.
  • Notoedres cati (feline scabies) — highly contagious among cats, zoonotic; pruritus starts on the head / face / ear margins.
  • Demodex canis — acquired from the dam while nursing (only route); NOT contagious between adults. Generalized demodicosis = immunosuppression flag: juvenile-onset (<18 mo) is heritable → neuter before adoption; adult-onset (>18 mo) → work up underlying disease. D. gatoi (cats) IS contagious between cats. Isoxazolines are the treatment of choice; avoid ivermectin in MDR1/ABCB1 breeds (collies, shelties, Aussies).
  • Otodectes cynotis (ear mites) — "coffee-ground" aural debris; treat the whole litter / group, clean the canal, repeat. Cheyletiella ("walking dandruff") — dorsal scaling; survives up to 10 d off-host.
⚠ Trap: Demodex canis is not contagious between adult dogs (dam-to-pup only), whereas D. gatoi is contagious between cats — commonly reversed on the exam.

Fleas & Ticks

  • Fleas (Ctenocephalides felis) — animals acquire fleas from the environment, not by host-to-host jumping; the pupa is the hardiest stage, persisting for months and eclosing on heat / CO₂ / vibration → environmental control is as important as treating the animal.
  • Flea disease — flea-bite anaemia (young / old / debilitated) & FAD (flea-allergy dermatitis) with lumbosacral / tail-base alopecia. C. felis is the intermediate host of Dipylidium and transmits Bartonella, Rickettsia, haemotropic Mycoplasma, and Francisella tularensis. Nitenpyram kills adults immediately for an admission infestation.
  • Brown dog tick (Rhipicephalus sanguineus) — the shelter exception: all life stages feed on dogs and it completes its whole cycle indoors ("the bane of shelters, kennels & domiciles") → usually needs professional pest control. All other ticks (Ixodes, Dermacentor, Amblyomma) need rodent / bird / reptile larval hosts → won't persist indoors.
  • Removal — fine-tipped forceps (never bare hands), grasp close to the skin, steady traction, remove the mouthparts. Many tick-borne pathogens (e.g. Lyme) need feeding time → early removal prevents infection.
  • Emerging — Asian longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), in the US since 2017, can build massive infestations.
⚠ Trap: the flea is the intermediate host of Dipylidium caninum — treat fleas and deworm. Because fleas come from the environment (pupa hardiest), always pair adulticide with environmental control.

Vector-Borne & Zoonotic

  • Vector map — ticks: Anaplasma, Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme), Ehrlichia, Rickettsia rickettsii (Rocky Mountain spotted fever), Cytauxzoon. Fleas: Bartonella (cat-scratch), Rickettsia felis / typhi (typhus), Yersinia pestis (plague). Sandfly: Leishmania. Kissing bug: Trypanosoma cruzi.
  • Cytauxzoon felis ("bobcat fever") — tick-borne (bobcat reservoir); acutely fatal in domestic cats (fever, icterus, dyspnoea → death ~1 wk); "signet-ring" piroplasms; atovaquone + azithromycin + intensive support.
  • Hepatozoonosis — the dog is infected by EATING the infected tick, not by its bite. H. americanum (Amblyomma maculatum) → periosteal bone proliferation + extreme neutrophilic leukocytosis; H. canis (Rhipicephalus) milder.
  • Babesia gibsoni — small ring-shaped piroplasms; spreads among Pit Bull-type dogs via bite wounds; treated dogs are permanent carriers; atovaquone + azithromycin. B. canis (vogeli) — large, paired piroplasms; Rhipicephalus; imidocarb dipropionate.
  • Chagas (Trypanosoma cruzi) — southern US; myocarditis, right-sided cardiomegaly; fecal (stercorarian) transmission from the kissing bug or by ingestion.
  • Personnel safety — mange gloves + covering clothing for the first 3–5 d of treatment; clean shared gear; remove standing water, garbage, decaying matter & long grass to limit vector breeding.
⚠ Trap: Hepatozoon is acquired by ingesting the infected tick, not through the tick's bite — a classic distractor.
