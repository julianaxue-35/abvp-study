Facility & Environment

Facility & Environment · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

The building is itself a biosecurity and welfare instrument: plan it to Capacity for Care, move animals · air · people healthiest-first, and hit the ASV (Association of Shelter Veterinarians) numbers — enclosure minimums, 10–20 air changes/hour, negative-pressure isolation, 64–80°F — because design controls the disease that no amount of cleaning can fix afterward.

Planning & Capacity for Care

  • Good shelters are planned, not just built — a needs assessment matches the building to the population it will actually serve.
  • Animal intake profile (needs-assessment Step 3) — record intake by mode of entry (owner surrender, stray, transfer, seizure) and by animal type, monthly for a minimum of 2 years: two annual cycles capture seasonality (kitten season, holiday surges) → sized to real demand, avoiding both overcrowding and overbuilding.
  • C4C (Capacity for Care) — the most animals you can house while still meeting each animal's full standard of care (housing, medical, behavioural, husbandry time); a welfare ceiling, not a physical cage count.
  • LRR (Live Release Rate) — proportion leaving alive (adoption, transfer, return-to-owner/field) of total outcomes; an operational metric, distinct from C4C.
  • Intake profile feeds C4C: predicted intake × length of stay = the housing + staffing the building must support.
  • Space-usage mix — Animal Areas ~65% (animal housing ~45% = largest single share, training ~15%, medical ~5%) vs Public/Support ~35% (lobby ~11%, admin ~10%, community ~7%, support ~7%). Planning guides, not regulatory minimums.

Facility Layout & Traffic Flow

  • How animals, people, air & equipment move through the building is itself a biosecurity control — it breaks transmission chains cleaning cannot fix afterward.
  • Directional daily workflow — clean/handle the healthiest first: puppies & kittens (most susceptible) → healthy adults → intake (status uncertain) → sick/isolation last (most likely shedding).
  • Staff are the top fomite vector (contact many animals) — a fixed direction plus changing outer clothing/gloves and washing hands between zones is a primary control, not a nicety.
  • Surgery & recovery — site away from isolation (infection control) yet close to housing (short patient transport); the placement balances the two.
  • Drop boxes — unmonitored receptacles for after-hours live-animal intake are unacceptable (ASV): cited harms = human/animal safety risk, suffering, infectious-disease exposure, death. Use posted on-call numbers, police drop-off arrangements, or emergency-clinic care agreements instead.

Primary Enclosure Standards

  • Postural-adjustment standard (ASV core rule) — within the primary enclosure the animal can stand & walk several steps, sit normally, lie at full body length, and hold the tail completely erect.
  • Individual adult cat — <8 sq ft (0.75 m²) floor is unacceptable; ≥11 sq ft (1.0 m²) is ideal (notes add a ~2.5 ft / 0.8 m cube volumetric target).
  • Dogs — minimum kennel size varies by body size; aim wider than 4 ft (1.2 m) so nose & tail clear the side walls when turning.
  • Larger pens help behaviour: dogs given ≥97 sq ft (9 m²) showed more activity/visual exploration, fewer stereotypies, and more positive interaction than dogs in pens half that size.
  • Double / multi-compartment preferred for most animals — separates rest from elimination, allows touch-free spot cleaning, lowers fomite/staff risk. Single-compartment only for specific medical needs (then raise enrichment + supervised out-of-kennel time).

Housing "Unacceptables" & Set-up

  • Categorical ASV violations — enclosures needing forceful handling (catch poles, squeeze gates) for daily care; housing in spaces not meant for animals (bathrooms, hallways) except a brief emergency; tethering as confinement; wire-mesh/slatted floors & all-wire/chain-link sides; airline crates, carriers, or live traps as primary enclosures; stacking that raises stress, blocks ventilation, or lets waste contaminate units below.
  • Every cat & dog needs a hiding place — cats with one hide less & approach adopters more.
  • Cats — house units elevated off the floor (ideally eye level; lowers stress, eases monitoring); litter box must fit the whole body for proper posturing (too-small box → house soiling); allow scratching/climbing/perching.
  • Dogs — soft resting place raised off the floor (comfort, dryness, thermoregulation); covered crate or partial front barrier for a hiding option; an off-centre drain keeps a dry rest spot while the floor dries.
  • Indoor-outdoor units are ideal for welfare but costly, energy-wasting, freeze shut at the guillotine door in cold climates, and trap humidity/mould in hot-humid climates.
⚠ Trap: forceful-handling housing is a categorical welfare violation — "acceptable if length of stay <7 days" or "acceptable with the right PPE" are distractors; it is not time-limited or PPE-fixable.

Cohousing & Group-Size Caps

  • Random cohousing is unacceptable — cohouse the minimum number needed for a social benefit.
  • Caps — cats ≤6 adults per enclosure (regardless of room size); dogs 2–4 adults, pairs preferred.
  • Space per cat — ≥18 sq ft (1.7 m²) floor per adult cat, more than individual housing so animals can keep distance by choice; spread resources out + provide elevated perches/hiding.
  • Feline pre-entry — individual health & behaviour assessment, no signs of contagious disease, confirmed negative FeLV (feline leukemia virus) and FIV (feline immunodeficiency virus), and time to mount a vaccine response before entry.
  • Match for age, sex, health, behaviour. Pups/kittens <20 weeks not cohoused with unfamiliar animals (unless benefit > risk, e.g. orphan + surrogate).
  • Dogs introduced outside their enclosures first. Monitor closely after a new animal enters & at feeding; exams + body-weight checks catch hidden conflict. All-in/all-out (all leave before new added) allows full sanitation between groups.
⚠ Trap: cats that fight at initial introduction are ~40× more likely to keep fighting — watch swapped values (10×, 50×) or wrong outcome (vaccine failure, ↓LOS); the claim is specifically continued fighting.

Isolation & Quarantine

  • Isolation must not open directly into another housing area — use a corridor/vestibule (also the PPE (personal protective equipment) don/doff space); provide handwashing sink, treatment space, dedicated supplies, an observation window, and current-use/precaution labels.
  • Different species must not share an isolation room; give separate areas for different highly contagious diseases (parvo dogs apart from respiratory dogs) to prevent co-infection.
  • Negative pressure = infectious-disease isolation — air flows IN when the door opens (pathogens contained); ≥12–15 ACH (air changes per hour) (up to 20 for highly infectious respiratory); 100% exhaust outdoors, never recirculated (most critical for canine iso — CDV (canine distemper virus), CIRD (canine infectious respiratory disease)); differential −2.5 to −8 Pascals (Pa), verified on a gauge; anteroom preserves the gradient.
  • Positive pressure = surgery / neonates / immunocompromised (FIV/FeLV-positive in recovery) — air flows OUT (pathogens excluded); ≥15 ACH, HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air)-filtered supply; full HEPA justified in surgery suites, not facility-wide.
  • Community-cat TNR (trap-neuter-return) hold — physically separate from general population (unknown vaccine/parasite status, stress-shedding), dedicated entry, nonporous drainable floor, 10–15 ACH.
  • Makeshift fallback (no true isolation) — house contagious dogs ≥25 ft (7.6 m) from unaffected dogs and cover enclosure doors; less effective than true isolation.
⚠ Trap: negative pressure = isolation (air in); positive pressure = surgery / immunocompromised (air out). Reversing them is a classic error — outbreaks in an immunocompromised ward often trace to wrong pressure direction.

Ventilation & Air Changes

  • Ventilation standard — 10–20 room air changes per hour (ACH) with fresh, non-recirculated air; rates may need to rise at full capacity (animals add heat, humidity, carbon dioxide (CO₂)). The engineering baseline "10–14 ACH" is not the ASV figure.
  • Doors — barred enclosure doors are preferred over plexiglass/fully-enclosed units (better airflow + interaction); fully enclosed units require individual-unit mechanical ventilation. HVAC = heating, ventilation & air-conditioning.
  • Isolation air — canine-isolation air exhausted outside, never recirculated; separate feline-isolation exchanges are lower priority (cats spread respiratory pathogens by droplet/spacing, not over distance).
  • Filtration — MERV (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value) 6–8 pleated filters suffice for retrofits; full HEPA is cost-prohibitive → reserved for surgery, not facility-wide.
  • Adjuncts only — UVGI (ultraviolet germicidal irradiation) & air purification never the sole method of air-quality/disease control; CO₂ monitors verify ventilation performance.
  • FURI (feline upper respiratory infection) — controlled by proper design + air systems + management (droplet spacing, stress reduction), not by sheer isolation-room count.

Noise · Light · Temperature

  • Ambient temperature — 64°F (18°C) to 80°F (26.6°C); breed, body condition, health, haircoat, facial conformation & age affect thermoregulation → monitor individuals; if thermostat/airflow can't keep an animal comfortable, add bedding, frozen treats/ice, or relocate.
  • Relative humidity (RH) — 30–70%.
  • Hearing ranges — cats 20 Hz–65 kHz, dogs 40 Hz–60 kHz, humans 20 Hz–20 kHz; sounds comfortable to humans hit cats/dogs far harder. Chronic noise >80–85 decibels (dB) causes physiological stress; dog kennels reach 95–115 dB during barking; peaks spike cortisol (HPA = hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis).
  • Noise design — individual enclosed rooms (walls to ceiling, laminated-glass door + sniff hole) are most effective but need individual mechanical ventilation (tradeoff vs the ASV barred-door preference); acoustic panels & ceiling tiles cut peaks 5–10 dB; quiet latches/rubber bumpers stop metal startle; separate cats from dog wards with a solid wall (cat hearing more sensitive → priority); a daily quiet hour + predictable feeding + low classical music reduce vocalisation & cortisol.
  • Lighting — support circadian rhythm with natural light; limit loads to <1 watt per sq ft; photosensors dim/switch off artificial light within ~15 ft (4.6 m) of windows when daylight suffices.
+ check: conversion trap — 80°F = 26.6°C, not 36.6°C (which is ~98°F, near body temperature); a common slip in the source notes.

Surfaces & Cleanability

  • Floors — gold standard: resinous epoxy or urethane (sealed, seamless, impermeable); other accepted seamless options: MMA (methyl methacrylate), sealed/polished concrete, terrazzo; linoleum/tile only in low-risk areas.
  • Unacceptable surfaces — floors: paint, epoxy paint, wood, vinyl. Walls: wood, painted wallboard, vinyl wall coverings, standard concrete block — all porous, can't survive repeated sanitation.
  • Wall-to-floor junction — seal (cove) it to block water & pathogen accumulation, regardless of flooring type.
  • Drainage — floors gently sloped to drains; covers safe & removable; no standing water; no shared drainage between units (cross-contamination); outdoor enclosure floors also nonporous, durable, drainable.
  • Exhaust vents — rear of individual cat units (cuts air cross-contamination); low, near floor drains for dogs. (notes/design practice, not explicit ASV text)
