Protozoa

Protozoa (GI) · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Most GI protozoa run a DIRECT faecal-oral life cycle and hit the young & crowded; the two apicomplexan exceptions run INDIRECT two-host cycles — Toxoplasma (cat = only definitive host) and Neospora (dog definitive, cattle intermediate). High-yield levers: which stage is immediately infective (Giardia, Cryptosporidium), match the drug to the bug (fenbendazole vs ronidazole vs ponazuril), and never forget the zoonoses (Cryptosporidium, Toxoplasma in pregnancy).

Giardia duodenalis flagellate

Giardia duodenalis: most common intestinal protozoan of shelter dogs/cats; trophozoite (falling-leaf motility, "owl-eyes" 2 nuclei, ventral disc — seen in diarrhoea) + hardy cyst (transmissible stage — in formed stool).

  • LC: DIRECT (single host). Cysts immediately infective when shed → rapid ward/fomite spread.
  • Route: faecal-oral cysts; survive cool/moist/standing water; reinfection from cysts in the coat.
  • Who: young & densely housed worst; small-bowel diarrhoea (soft, pale, fatty, mucoid); many asymptomatic shedders.
  • Rx: fenbendazole 50 mg/kg PO q24h × 5 d + environment; bathe day 1 & end; if persists repeat the cycle — NOT metronidazole (dysbiosis/chronicity). Treat disease, not shedding.
  • Zoo: assemblages A/B potentially zoonotic (low; pets carry host-adapted C/D dogs, F cats).
⚠ Trap: stage by stool — trophozoite in diarrhoea, cyst in formed stool. Confirm cure by flotation, NOT antigen (stays antigen-positive after shedding stops). Fenbendazole is first-line, not metronidazole.

Tritrichomonas foetus cats

Tritrichomonas foetus (cats): protozoan flagellate colonising the large bowel; same species as bovine trichomoniasis.

  • LC: DIRECT, faecal-oral via contaminated litter boxes. Poor environmental survival (~2–3 d) → host/fomite control matters more than heavy decon.
  • Who: young cats (<2 y) in catteries/shelters; chronic intermittent large-bowel diarrhoea (fresh blood, mucus, tenesmus), normal appetite/weight, well between bouts; often self-resolves over months–years.
  • Dx: fresh smear (forward rolling, single axostyle, 1 nucleus, undulating membrane), InPouch TF culture, or PCR (most specific). ~22–54% co-infected with Giardia.
  • Rx: ronidazole 30 mg/kg PO q24h × 14 d — reversible neurotoxicity (lethargy/ataxia/tremor; stop if seen).
⚠ Trap: metronidazole treats Giardia but NOT Tritrichomonas — use ronidazole. Distinguish on smear: T. foetus = forward rolling + 1 axostyle + 1 nucleus; Giardia = falling-leaf + owl-eyes (2 nuclei) + ventral disc.

Cryptosporidium spp. zoonotic

Cryptosporidium spp.: coccidian apicomplexan; dogs = host-adapted C. canis, cats = C. felis (C. parvum/C. hominis = ruminant/human).

  • LC: DIRECT. Oocysts immediately infective when shed — no sporulation needed → contamination faster than other coccidia.
  • Route: faecal-oral oocysts.
  • Who: watery/mucoid small-bowel diarrhoea; self-limiting in immunocompetent adults; severe/life-threatening in neonates/young/immunocompromised.
  • Rx: no consistently effective drug → supportive (fluids, diet); azithromycin empirical; paromomycin nephrotoxic in cats — avoid. Control = hygiene.
  • Zoo: ZOONOTIC — handwashing is the key measure, esp. immunocompromised people (HIV/transplant); dog/cat host-adapted strains = limited risk.
  • Dx: modified Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) — oocysts stain pink/red; routine float poor (oocysts 4–5 µm); ELISA/IFA/PCR.
⚠ Trap: oocysts are chlorine/bleach-resistant (tough wall). A shelter using bleach as primary disinfectant will NOT decontaminate — use steam >60 °C (140 °F) or 6% hydrogen peroxide / desiccation.

Cystoisospora coccidia

Cystoisospora (coccidia): host-specific — cat coccidia don't infect dogs or humans, and vice versa.

  • LC: DIRECT, faecal-oral (oocysts sporulate after brief environmental development).
  • Who: disease of naive puppies/kittens in heavily contaminated, crowded housing.
  • Rx (DOC): ponazuril (toltrazuril sulfone) 20–50 mg/kg PO SID × 1–3 d = coccidiocidal. Sulfadimethoxine 50–60 mg/kg PO SID × 14–21 d = only labelled, but merely coccidiostatic.
⚠ Trap: signs precede shedding — enterocyte destruction (clinical signs) occurs BEFORE oocyst production, so a sick animal can have a negative fecal float. One negative float does not rule out coccidiosis.

Toxoplasma gondii zoonotic

Toxoplasma gondii: apicomplexan; cat = the ONLY definitive host (sole source of oocysts).

  • LC: INDIRECT two-host. Definitive = felids (shed oocysts only ~2 wk during acute infection); intermediate = any warm-blooded host (tissue cysts).
  • Route (3): ingest sporulated oocysts from environment; eat raw/undercooked meat with tissue cysts; transplacental.
  • Who: disseminated disease in the immunosuppressed — fever, anorexia, ocular, pulmonary, CNS signs.
  • Rx: clindamycin 10–12 mg/kg BID × 2–4 wk (or pyrimethamine + sulfonamide; ponazuril cuts shedding in identified shedders).
  • Zoo: ZOONOTIC — important, esp. primary infection in pregnancy.
⚠ Trap: the cat is the ONLY definitive host and shedding is brief/self-limiting (~1–2 wk). Oocysts need >24 h to sporulate → daily litter removal clears them before infective. A seropositive cat sheds LESS (the safer cat).

Neospora caninum dog def. host

Neospora caninum: Toxoplasma-like apicomplexan but cycles dog ↔ cattle.

  • LC: INDIRECT. Dog = definitive host (sheds oocysts on first infection), cattle = intermediate; dog infected by eating raw beef/venison. + transplacental in intermediate host.
  • Who: congenitally infected pups → ascending flaccid hind-limb paralysis ~3 wk; adults → neuro signs, nodular dermatitis, myositis, myocarditis.
  • Rx: clindamycin or TMS, each + pyrimethamine, ~4 wk; treat ALL littermates incl. clinically normal ones.
  • Zoo: NOT zoonotic — key contrast with Toxoplasma.

Diagnosis & Shelter Control

  • Fecal flotation (zinc-sulfate centrifugation) → cysts/oocysts; intermittent shedding → check multiple samples before calling negative.
  • Direct saline wet-mount → motile trophozoites (Giardia falling-leaf; Tritrichomonas forward rolling).
  • Antigen ELISA / SNAP — Giardia; but confirm cure by flotation, not antigen.
  • Modified Ziehl-Neelsen (acid-fast) → Cryptosporidium oocysts. PCR → assemblage/species, zoonotic Qs, T. foetus. AAFP/ASV minimum plan for acute diarrhoea = float + Giardia antigen.
  • Environmental resistance — Giardia cysts resist QACs (quats); Cryptosporidium & coccidia oocysts resist bleach/chlorine. Need steam/heat >60–70 °C (140–158 °F), drying/desiccation, accelerated H₂O₂, or 6% hydrogen peroxide for oocysts.
⚠ Trap: immediately-infective agents (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) demand isolation-level daily sanitation; a quat-only or bleach-only protocol fails against them.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Toxoplasma gondii in cats across Europe — serology & PCR (45,523 cats): 31.9% positive by direct/indirect testing (PCR, IgG, IgM). Risk factors: age >5 years, European domestic shorthair/crossbreed, male (especially neutered) cats, and feline leukaemia virus (FeLV)– or feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV)–positive status. Regionality significantly influenced IgG, IgM and PCR results, and seasonality had a statistically significant effect (higher in the warmer months — consistent with warm-season oocyst sporulation/survival and more outdoor hunting).
  • Seropositivity for Toxoplasma gondii in cats with clinical signs & in households with women of childbearing age: ▢ abstract pending — send the summary and I'll complete this entry.
  • Toxoplasmal meningoencephalitis in a 14-week shelter kitten with presumed feline infectious peritonitis (FIP): clinical signs — failure to thrive, weight loss, decreased activity, neurological dysfunction. Necropsy histopathology showed disseminated necrotising meningoencephalitis with T. gondii bradyzoites and tachyzoites — a Toxoplasma mimic of FIP.
  • Rabbits as reservoirs — zoonotic Cryptosporidium & Giardia: despite abundant Cryptosporidium cuniculus in rabbits, human reports are relatively rare (except the United Kingdom & New Zealand); subtyping supports zoonotic potential. Giardia assemblage B dominates. It remains unclear whether pet rabbits or contaminated water are the main source of C. cuniculus infection in people.
  • Giardia genotyping — multilocus vs commercial beta-giardin qPCR: Giardia duodenalis has assemblages A–G; A & B are zoonotic (C, D, F, G affect small animals but rarely cause human disease). Of 140 samples, 92.1% were assemblage A or B. Beta-giardin qPCR is a cheaper, less-laborious way to flag zoonotic A/B assemblages in dog/cat faeces.
  • Four methods for diagnosing canine Giardia: direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) is the laboratory reference standard, though microscopy after flotation remains most-used in practice. DFA and zinc-sulfate flotation were the most useful; rapid antigen tests are a useful alternative/complement to DFA/zinc-sulfate.
  • Tritrichomonas foetus in cats, Poland (2020–2024): detected in 10.6% of cats, highest in animals <1 year (14.9%) — a common infection and an important differential for large-bowel diarrhoea.
  • Gastrointestinal parasites in urban cats, Kazakhstan (multi-city, 1,256 samples): 17.7% infected with ≥1 intestinal parasite — most prevalent Cystoisospora felis (7.2%), Toxocara cati (6.2%), Giardia (6.4%), Cystoisospora rivolta (2%). Stray cats more likely to harbour C. felis/C. rivolta; female cats higher Giardia odds; kittens <6 months ~2× more likely positive for T. cati, C. felis and Giardia than adults.
