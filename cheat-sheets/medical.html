Medical (Non-Infectious)

Medical (Non-Infectious) · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Shelter medical care is proactive and population-aware: it starts at or before intake, weighs the individual against the group, and is bounded by a defined capacity for care. It runs on veterinary oversight (a valid veterinarian-client-patient relationship (VCPR)), written evidence-based protocols, disciplined prescribing, complete records, a fixed monitoring cadence, and mandatory multimodal, pre-emptive pain control — with a written emergency plan behind it all.

Capacity for Care

  • Health (after the World Health Organization / WHO) = complete physical, mental & social well-being — not merely absence of disease.
  • Care timeline — medical care must begin at or before intake and continue throughout the stay; on admission the shelter is responsible for all needed medical/wellness care → or for promptly finding an outcome that meets those needs.
  • Preventive pillars — vaccination, parasite control, nutrition, appropriate handling & housing (each has its own subdomain).
  • Five Freedoms (Brambell 1965 / FAWC — the welfare basis the ASV = Association of Shelter Veterinarians built its Standards of Care on): from ① hunger & thirst · ② discomfort · ③ pain, injury & disease · ④ fear & distress · ⑤ to express normal behavior.
  • Capacity to provide care (ASV) is dynamic — 6 factors: resource availability · duration of care · number of animals needing treatment · transmission likelihood + consequences · likelihood of recovery · potential for a live outcome. → Treat per written protocol vs Cannot treat → alternative placement or humane outcome.
  • Population vs individual — population health suffers when disease spread is likely, when long length of stay (LOS) pushes the shelter over capacity, or when one animal's cost drains others' resources.

Veterinary Supervision & VCPR

  • Every shelter needs a formal relationship with a veterinarian who has direct knowledge of the organization, its population & facilities.
  • Direct supervision — vet readily available on the premises, has assumed responsibility.
  • Indirect supervision — vet need not be on premises; has given written/oral instructions, is reachable by phone/immediate communication, has assumed responsibility. Most preventive & common-condition care runs under indirect supervision.
  • Direct knowledge — vet regularly visits & examines the population in person. (Definitions track the 2019 AVMA Model Practice Act.)
  • Telemedicine — remote communication technology for exam & treatment; broadens access, removes geographic barriers, cuts travel stress, supports biosecurity; vet uses professional discretion on suitability.
  • VCPR (veterinarian-client-patient relationship) — ASV supports legislation allowing a VCPR to be created remotely (telephone/digital/electronic) and prescribing through it; keep a record per encounter, ensure follow-up, respect license jurisdiction.

Protocols, Prescribing & Records

  • Evidence-based protocols are essential, developed with the shelter's vet; compliance monitoring at population & individual level is part of oversight. Cover 6 areas: diagnosis · treatment · animal housing · sanitation · decision-making · communication. Outside a protocol / not responding → consult the vet.
  • Prescribing standard — meds only by prescription or written protocol; needs a reasonable presumptive diagnosis, ability to administer as directed, and a plan to monitor success/failure.
  • Antibiotic stewardship — giving drugs when not needed (e.g. antibiotics to prevent a viral infection) causes side effects & promotes resistance. Dispensing follows federal/state regs.
  • Prescription label — 7 components: ① prescribing vet's name · ② clinic/shelter name, phone & address · ③ patient ID & species · ④ date dispensed & expiration date · ⑤ drug name, form & amount · ⑥ directions for use · ⑦ cautionary statements.
  • Medical records — identifying info + signalment (age, sex, species, reproductive status) + a dated list of exam findings, vaccinations, diagnostics, procedures & treatments (drug dose + route); a copy goes with the animal when it leaves.
  • Program deficiency indicators — health/welfare decline after intake; sick/injured held without prompt treatment; wide-scale disease outbreaks; deaths/euthanasia from shelter-acquired disease/injury; chronically high disease rates.
⚠ Trap: the ASV term is wide-scale disease outbreaks (a key deficiency indicator) — not "side-scale".

Monitoring & Examination

  • Intake — cursory health check by trained personnel (confirm age, sex, description, ID, microchip scan); a comprehensive physical exam ideally within 24 h of intake; isolate signs of infectious disease until judged low-risk.
  • Daily — visual observation of every animal ≥ every 24 h, ideally before cleaning; note food intake & enclosure condition (feces, urine, vomit).
  • Monthly — reweigh + repeat body condition score (BCS).
  • Every 6 months — comprehensive exam for long-term residents, including those in foster; more often for chronic conditions or any new concern.
  • Baselines: normal dog/cat rectal temp ≈ 100.5–102.5°F (38.1–39.2°C); flag weight loss >10% of body weight (e.g. >1 lb / 0.45 kg in a 10 lb / 4.5 kg cat)+

Problem-Oriented Approach

  • Gold standard for small-animal assessment. A problem = any abnormality needing medical/surgical management or interfering with quality of life (Lorenz); in shelters it also includes public-health/safety concerns.
  • Four steps — ① Database collection (minimum database on every patient: complete history + complete physical exam incl behavioral observation) → ② Problem identification (list historical, physical, behavioral, welfare & public-health/safety problems at current understanding) → ③ Plan formulation (diagnostics, initial therapy, staff/adopter education within mission, resources & legal holding periods) → ④ Assessment & follow-up (appraise, document progression, reassess regularly — critical for long-stay).
  • Minimum database at intake — history + physical exam (incl microchip scan) + behavioral observation. Pre-adoption add: feline leukemia virus / feline immunodeficiency virus (FeLV/FIV) test (cats), heartworm test (dogs, endemic areas), fecal if diarrhea.
  • Geriatric add-ons (older animals offered for adoption) — packed cell volume / total solids (PCV/TS), urine specific gravity + dipstick, and — resources allowing — fine-needle-aspirate (FNA) cytology of all lumps & bumps.

FeLV/FIV Testing & Isolation

  • General principle — assess each cat individually; in general screen all cats for retroviruses to optimize individual & population health.
  • Should test ↑risk — cats entering group housing, or ill cats.
  • Individually-housed, low-risk, healthy — weigh transmission risk against financial/management implications.
  • Healthy feral cats in TNR (trap-neuter-return) — testing is not recommended.
  • Isolation vs quarantine — isolation separates suspected/confirmed infectious cases until judged low-risk; quarantine of healthy animals at intake is not generally recommended (only for documented high-risk exposure) — unnecessary holds ↑ LOS & harm welfare.
⚠ Trap: do NOT euthanize a clinically healthy cat on a single positive point-of-care retroviral test — confirm per AAFP (American Association of Feline Practitioners) guidelines, seek informed placement, and send the test date & type with the cat. Blanket intake quarantine of healthy animals is a distractor.

Pain Assessment & Analgesia

  • Core rule — pain must be recognized & treated; failure to provide treatment for pain is unacceptable; treatment can include euthanasia.
  • Assessment — via behavior + knowledge of causes; if a condition is painful in humans, assume it is painful in animals; several validated pain scales exist.
  • Unrelieved pain → weight loss, muscle breakdown, ↑ blood pressure, prolonged recovery, mental/emotional distress.
  • Pre-emptive & multimodal — of appropriate strength & duration; when pain is anticipated (surgery), give control before the painful event; controlled drugs supervised by a vet per statute.
  • Non-pharmacological adjuncts (supplement, never replace, analgesia) — littermates present, quiet environment, massage, physical therapy, heat, deep bedding.
  • Reassess frequently; if relief is inadequate → emergency medical care.

Degenerative Joint Disease & Chronic Pain

  • DJD / OA (degenerative joint disease / osteoarthritis) — a very common, under-recognized source of feline chronic pain; manage with a multimodal chronic-pain plan.
  • NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug) — e.g. robenacoxib for chronic musculoskeletal (MSK) pain; supported for longer-term feline use (see journals).
  • Frunevetmab — an anti-nerve-growth-factor (anti-NGF) monoclonal antibody for feline DJD/OA pain, given subcutaneously (SC) or intravenously (IV).
  • Multimodal plan — weight management, environmental modification (ramps, low-sided litter, soft/warm bedding), plus adjunct analgesics.

Emergency Triage & Pharmacology

  • Written emergency plan — timely vet care for any injured/distressed/significantly ill animal; states how staff recognize & report, and whether care is on-site or at an outside clinic; foster/off-site covered with after-hours instructions. 3 paths: on-site stabilization · transfer to a partnered 24-h emergency/specialty facility · prompt humane euthanasia when suffering cannot be relieved. Non-vet staff must be trained to trigger the protocol.
  • Triage = ABC primary survey (Airway → Breathing → Circulation, in order) — control life-threatening hemorrhage with direct pressure concurrently.
    • A airway patency; stridor/stertor, open-mouth breathing (cat), cyanosis.
    • B effort, auscultate, percuss; orthopnea, dull lung sounds, abdominal push.
    • C mucous-membrane (MM) color, capillary refill time (CRT), pulse quality.
  • Shock read — pale/muddy MM + CRT >2.5 s + weak pulses = decompensatory hypovolemic shock; brick-red MM + CRT <1 s + bounding pulses = hyperdynamic/early distributive-septic shock.
  • Core drugs — epinephrine (asystole/PEA/anaphylaxis) · atropine (bradycardia/organophosphate) · mannitol (traumatic brain injury/↑ intracranial pressure — volume-resuscitate first) · diazepam then propofol/alfaxalone (status epilepticus) · furosemide (acute congestive heart failure/pulmonary edema) · lidocaine (ventricular arrhythmias) · methocarbamol (tremors, e.g. feline permethrin).
  • 50% dextrose for hypoglycemia — hyperosmolar, dilute & give slow IV/IO (intravenous/intraosseous); neonates have minimal glycogen.
  • "Big five" minimum database (critical case) — PCV/TS, blood glucose, BUN/creatinine, urine specific gravity/dipstick, thorough physical exam.
⚠ Trap: brick-red MM + very fast CRT + bounding pulses = hyperdynamic (distributive/septic) shock, NOT hypovolemia. Legal status of an animal must never bar necessary treatment.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Robenacoxib long-term safety (cat, chronic musculoskeletal disease): robenacoxib was not associated with more adverse events than placebo when given for 4–12 weeks to cats with chronic musculoskeletal (MSK) disease — supports longer-term use for feline chronic pain.
  • Frunevetmab (anti-nerve-growth-factor antibody) for feline degenerative-joint-disease pain (pilot field study): owner questionnaires showed significant improvement in frunevetmab-treated cats, with no difference between intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC) routes of administration; most adverse events were dermatitis/alopecia related to the activity-monitor collars rather than the drug.
  • Robenacoxib for feline degenerative-joint-disease (DJD) pain (randomised, blinded pilot): over two consecutive 3-week courses, robenacoxib-treated cats had higher total activity (>10% increase at 3 and 6 weeks), significantly less owner-assessed disability, and improved temperament/happiness by 6 weeks.
