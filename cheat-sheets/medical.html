Medical (Non-Infectious)

Medical (Non-Infectious) · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Shelter medical care is proactive and population-aware: starts at/before intake, weighs individual vs group, bounded by capacity for care — running on VCPR + written protocols, disciplined prescribing, records, a fixed monitoring cadence, multimodal pre-emptive pain control, and a written emergency plan.

Capacity for Care

  • Health (WHO) = complete physical, mental & social well-being — not merely absence of disease.
  • Care timeline — begins at or before intake, continues through the stay → shelter provides all needed care, or finds an outcome that meets those needs.
  • Preventive pillars — vaccination, parasite control, nutrition, handling & housing.
  • Five Freedoms (Brambell/FAWC — basis for the ASV = Association of Shelter Veterinarians Standards of Care): ① hunger/thirst · ② discomfort · ③ pain/injury/disease · ④ fear/distress · ⑤ express normal behavior.
  • Capacity to provide care (ASV) is dynamic — 6 factors: resources · duration · number needing treatment · transmission likelihood+consequences · likelihood of recovery · potential live outcome. → Treat per protocol vs Cannot treat → placement or humane outcome.
  • Population vs individual — population health suffers with likely disease spread, long LOS over capacity, or one animal draining others' resources.

Veterinary Supervision & VCPR

  • Needs a formal relationship with a vet who has direct knowledge of the org, population & facilities.
  • Direct supervision — vet readily available on premises, has assumed responsibility.
  • Indirect supervision — vet off premises but reachable, gave instructions, assumed responsibility. Most preventive & common-condition care runs here.
  • Direct knowledge — vet regularly visits & examines the population in person (per 2019 AVMA Model Practice Act).
  • Telemedicine — remote exam/treatment; broadens access, cuts travel stress, supports biosecurity; vet's discretion on suitability.
  • VCPR — ASV supports it being created remotely + prescribing through it; record per encounter, ensure follow-up, respect license jurisdiction.

Protocols, Prescribing & Records

  • Evidence-based protocols developed with the shelter vet; cover 6 areas: diagnosis · treatment · housing · sanitation · decision-making · communication. Outside protocol / not responding → consult vet.
  • Prescribing standard — meds only by prescription/protocol; needs a reasonable presumptive diagnosis, ability to administer as directed, plan to monitor.
  • Stewardship — no drugs when not needed (antibiotics won't prevent a viral infection). Dispensing per federal/state regs.
  • Rx label — 7 components: ① vet's name · ② clinic/shelter name, phone, address · ③ patient ID & species · ④ date dispensed + expiration · ⑤ drug name, form, amount · ⑥ directions · ⑦ cautionary statements.
  • Records — ID + signalment (age, sex, species, repro status) + dated exam findings, vaccines, diagnostics, procedures & treatments (drug/dose/route); copy goes with the animal when it leaves.
  • Deficiency indicators — decline after intake; sick/injured held without prompt treatment; wide-scale outbreaks; deaths/euthanasia from shelter-acquired disease; chronically high disease rates.
⚠ Trap: the ASV term is wide-scale disease outbreaks — not "side-scale".

Monitoring & Examination

  • Intake — cursory check by trained staff (age, sex, description, ID, microchip scan); comprehensive exam ideally within 24 h; isolate infectious signs until low-risk.
  • Daily — visual obs of every animal ≥ every 24 h, ideally before cleaning; note food intake & enclosure (feces/urine/vomit).
  • Monthly — reweigh + repeat BCS.
  • Every 6 months — comprehensive exam for long-term residents incl foster; sooner for chronic/new concerns.
  • Baselines — normal dog/cat rectal temp 100.5–102.5°F (38.1–39.2°C); flag weight loss >10% body weight.

Problem-Oriented Approach

  • Problem = any abnormality needing medical/surgical management or interfering with QOL (Lorenz); shelters add public-health/safety concerns.
  • 4 steps — ① Database collection (minimum database: full history + PE incl behavioral obs) → ② Problem ID (list historical, physical, behavioral, welfare & public-health problems) → ③ Plan (diagnostics, therapy, education — within resources & legal holds) → ④ Assessment & follow-up (document, reassess — critical for long-stay).
  • Minimum database at intake — history + PE (incl microchip scan) + behavioral obs. Pre-adoption add: FeLV/FIV (cats), heartworm (dogs, endemic areas), fecal if diarrhea.
  • Geriatric add-ons — PCV/TS, urine specific gravity + dipstick, and (resources allowing) FNA cytology of all lumps & bumps.

FeLV/FIV Testing & Isolation

  • Assess each cat individually; in general screen all cats for retroviruses.
  • Should test ↑risk — cats entering group housing, or ill cats.
  • Individually-housed, low-risk, healthy — weigh transmission risk vs cost/management.
  • Healthy feral cats in TNR (trap-neuter-return) — testing not recommended.
  • Isolation separates suspected/confirmed cases until low-risk; quarantine of healthy intakes not generally recommended (only documented high-risk exposure) — unnecessary holds ↑ LOS & harm welfare.
⚠ Trap: do NOT euthanize a clinically healthy cat on a single positive point-of-care retroviral test — confirm per AAFP guidelines, seek informed placement, send test date & type with the cat. Blanket intake quarantine is a distractor.

Pain Assessment & Analgesia

  • Pain must be recognized & treated; failure to treat pain is unacceptable; treatment can include euthanasia.
  • Assessment — via behavior + knowledge of causes; if painful in humans, assume painful in animals; validated pain scales exist.
  • Unrelieved pain → weight loss, muscle breakdown, ↑ blood pressure, prolonged recovery, distress.
  • Pre-emptive & multimodal — appropriate strength/duration; when pain is anticipated (surgery), give control before the event.
  • Non-drug adjuncts (supplement, never replace) — littermates present, quiet environment, massage, physical therapy, heat, deep bedding.
  • Reassess frequently; inadequate relief → emergency care.

Degenerative Joint Disease & Chronic Pain

  • DJD / OA — common, under-recognized feline chronic pain; manage with a multimodal chronic-pain plan.
  • NSAID — e.g. robenacoxib for chronic musculoskeletal pain; supported for longer-term feline use.
  • Frunevetmab — anti-nerve-growth-factor (anti-NGF) monoclonal antibody for feline DJD/OA pain; SC or IV.
  • Multimodal plan — weight management, environmental mods (ramps, low-sided litter, soft/warm bedding), adjunct analgesics.

Emergency Triage & Pharmacology

  • Written emergency plan — states how staff recognize & report and whether care is on-site or at an outside clinic; foster/off-site covered with after-hours instructions. 3 paths: on-site stabilization · transfer to a partnered 24-h emergency/specialty facility · prompt humane euthanasia when suffering can't be relieved. Non-vet staff trained to trigger it.
  • Triage = ABC primary survey (Airway → Breathing → Circulation, in order) — control life-threatening hemorrhage with direct pressure concurrently.
    • A airway patency; stridor/stertor, open-mouth breathing (cat), cyanosis.
    • B effort, auscultate, percuss; orthopnea, dull lung sounds, abdominal push.
    • C MM color, CRT, pulse quality.
  • Shock read — pale/muddy MM + CRT >2.5 s + weak pulses = decompensatory hypovolemic shock; brick-red MM + CRT <1 s + bounding pulses = hyperdynamic/early distributive-septic shock.
  • Core drugs — epinephrine (asystole/PEA/anaphylaxis) · atropine (bradycardia/organophosphate) · mannitol (TBI/↑ICP — volume-resuscitate first) · diazepam then propofol/alfaxalone (status epilepticus) · furosemide (acute CHF/pulmonary edema) · lidocaine (ventricular arrhythmias) · methocarbamol (tremors, e.g. feline permethrin).
  • 50% dextrose for hypoglycemia — hyperosmolar, dilute & give slow IV/IO; neonates have minimal glycogen.
  • "Big five" minimum database (critical case) — PCV/TS, blood glucose, BUN/creatinine, urine SG/dipstick, thorough PE.
⚠ Trap: brick-red MM + very fast CRT + bounding pulses = hyperdynamic (distributive/septic) shock, NOT hypovolemia. Legal status must never bar necessary treatment.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Robenacoxib long-term safety (cat, chronic musculoskeletal disease): robenacoxib was not associated with more adverse events than placebo when given for 4–12 weeks to cats with chronic musculoskeletal (MSK) disease — supports longer-term use for feline chronic pain.
  • Frunevetmab (anti-nerve-growth-factor antibody) for feline degenerative-joint-disease pain (pilot field study): owner questionnaires showed significant improvement in frunevetmab-treated cats, with no difference between intravenous (IV) and subcutaneous (SC) routes of administration; most adverse events were dermatitis/alopecia related to the activity-monitor collars rather than the drug.
  • Robenacoxib for feline degenerative-joint-disease (DJD) pain (randomised, blinded pilot): over two consecutive 3-week courses, robenacoxib-treated cats had higher total activity (>10% increase at 3 and 6 weeks), significantly less owner-assessed disability, and improved temperament/happiness by 6 weeks.
  • Renal AA amyloidosis in shelter cats: affected cats had higher urine protein:creatinine (UPC) and urine albumin:creatinine (UAC) ratios than unaffected cats; renal AA amyloidosis is associated with mixed proteinuria, specific kidney lesions and elevated urine biomarkers — urine apolipoprotein C-III may assist diagnosis.
  • Renal AA-amyloidosis in shelter cats (clinicopathology / histopathology / ultrastructure): AA-amyloidosis is frequent in shelter cats and chronic kidney disease is the foremost cause of death; amyloid deposits in both renal cortex and medulla, associated with azotaemia and proteinuria.
  • FLUTD / urethral-obstruction long-term outcomes (shelter cats, interview-based): recurrent urinary signs occur in up to 51% of affected cats and obstruction recurs in 15–47%, but in this cohort only 32.3% had recurrent signs and 6.5% re-obstructed. Housing, household composition and diet showed no statistical association with recurrence; all respondents would adopt a similar cat again.
  • Plasma supplementation in orphaned neonatal kittens (retrospective): plasma was NOT significantly associated with reduced 30-day mortality despite early survival-curve differences, though it delayed onset of diarrhoea and weight loss; intake weight and weight gain were the strongest survival predictors.
  • Enterococcus lactis SF68 probiotic for amoxicillin-clavulanate dysbiosis (cats): the probiotic lessened amoxiclav-associated clinical signs, partly via positive effects on the microbiota and bile-acid metabolism — consider probiotic supplementation in cats needing amoxiclav.
  • Metronidazole vs synbiotic for canine acute diarrhoea (n=27): metronidazole gave higher E. coli than the synbiotic on days 6 and 30, no effect on C. perfringens, and significantly lower C. hiranonis; metronidazole harmed the microbiome without improving clinical outcome — a synbiotic may be the preferred option.
  • C-reactive protein (CRP) stability with freezing (dogs): keep the CRP assay consistent when monitoring; a freeze-thaw does not lower the result if stored at −80°C; dogs with signs of H3N2 canine-influenza infection had increased CRP.
  • Telmisartan antioxidative effects (healthy dogs, n=8): telmisartan 10 mg PO once daily for 28 days caused no significant change in arterial blood pressure, CBC or cytokines/chemokines, but significantly upregulated serum superoxide dismutase, total antioxidant capacity and plasma glutathione peroxidase on some dates — antioxidative effects in healthy dogs.
  • Neurological adverse effects of isoxazolines (cats & dogs): signs include ataxia, muscle tremor, convulsions or hyperaesthesia; most reports involve fluralaner or sarolaner; the true incidence remains unclear.
  • Osteoarthritis prevalence in dogs >8 years (shoulder / elbow / hip / stifle): prevalence was 39.2% / 57.4% / 35.9% / 36.4%; no correlation between higher OA grade and weight or age, but significantly higher OA in heavier weight-groups; sex and neuter status did not affect OA.
  • Canine OA joint-supplement trial — Antinol + meloxicam vs 4Cyte (green-lipped mussel): at 6 weeks, improvement in peak vertical force of the index limbs was greater in the Antinol + meloxicam group than the 4Cyte group — Antinol and meloxicam were more useful; green-lipped mussel (4Cyte) less so.
  • Topical blood products + ophthalmic antibiotics (canine infectious keratitis): prefer equine over canine blood products (less antibiotic interference), serum over plasma (greater antibiotic effect), and give the blood product >15 min after the last antibiotic eyedrop to minimise albumin-antibiotic binding in the tear film.
  • Burkholderia-associated ocular disease (cats): enucleated eyes from shelter cats had a high relative abundance of Burkholderia (43.8–98.3%; the most abundant genus in 94% of samples); 50% were FHV-1-positive, 6% FCV-positive — consider Burkholderia when investigating shelter-cat eye disease.
  • "Pandora's box" — 58 previously-hoarded cats with chronic otitis media-interna (OMI): signs = inappetence, pruritus/alopecia, nasopharyngeal signs, otitis externa/interna, purulent aural discharge, polyps, thickened tympanic bulla; 54% cultured Streptococcus equi subsp. zooepidemicus. Ventral bulla osteotomy (VBO) complication rates were 69% (1st surgery) and 63% (2nd); of 101 complications 55% were serious (life-threatening perioperative in 7). Otitis interna pre-op and surgery by a generalist (vs specialist) were risk factors for otitis externa at recheck — surgery succeeded in most, but the high complication rate questions whether it's worth doing without a specialist.
  • RECOVER newborn-resuscitation guidelines (dogs & cats): neonatal resuscitation at birth should start with airway clearance, tactile stimulation and temperature control, then positive-pressure ventilation, escalating to CPR.
  • "Good Samaritan" animals — postmortem morbidity/mortality (2014–2022): leading causes of death / euthanasia-requiring morbidity were euthanasia for rabies testing (cats) and vehicular trauma (dogs); other significant causes included trauma, infectious disease and neoplasia.
  • Longevity & mortality in cats (necropsy, 3,108 cases, 1989–2019): mean age at death 9.07 years; cancer was the commonest cause of death (35.81%; found in 41.3% of cats); multiorgan/systemic causes 21.72%. Renal abnormalities were present in 62.84% but the primary cause of death in only 13.06%. Intact cats (both sexes) had significantly shorter lifespans than desexed cats; FeLV-positive status was associated with reduced longevity, FIV was NOT.
  • Feline cystocentesis bacteriuria (UK, 2,712 samples): 15.7% were culture-positive — E. coli most common (43.7%), then other Enterobacterales (26.4%), Enterococcus (14.9%), Staphylococcus (9.2%). E. coli resistance: cephalexin 20.7%, amoxicillin 16.7%; Enterobacterales resistance highest to amoxicillin (64.1%) and cephalexin (52.2%); Enterococcus 94.3% resistant to trimethoprim-sulfa; Staphylococcus 20% to amoxicillin.
  • Canine blood xenotransfusion vs feline allotransfusion (311 cats): xenotransfusion (dog→cat blood) is used for haemorrhage or hypoproliferative anaemia, most often for financial reasons. Post-transfusion mean PCV was higher in the xeno group (and at 48–96 h), but transfusion-related adverse effects were more frequent (37.1% xeno vs 19.4% allo) — though acute reactions were actually more common in the ALLO group, and adverse reactions were not associated with survival to discharge. Survival to discharge was marginally lower with xeno (~2%) but long-term survival was good — canine blood can save feline lives in an emergency when typed feline blood is unavailable/infeasible.
  • Diphenhydramine ± glucocorticoid for feline allergic reactions: no outcome difference between diphenhydramine alone vs with an added glucocorticoid; the ideal treatment remains unknown, but in human and veterinary medicine glucocorticoids are NOT indicated for acute allergic reactions.
  • Neutrophil:lymphocyte ratio (NLR) + PCA for canine mammary tumours: pre-treatment NLR has prognostic value for post-surgical survival in dogs with mammary tumours.
  • Feline chronic gingivostomatitis in feral cats (South Korea): 26.7% of feral TNR cats had chronic gingivostomatitis, sometimes with tongue lesions.
  • Canine shelter population health profile (Minas Gerais, Brazil, 329 dogs, 2019–2020): many dogs appeared healthy, but nutritional, dermatological, otological and disease-management measures should be implemented once health changes are identified.
  • Behavioural changes with homelessness + dermatological disease (case report): emphasises treating both the behaviour and the medical/dermatological disease together to encourage treatment attempts and socialisation.
  • Canine incisor eruption timing (case report): incisors erupted early (I3 + I2 ~4.5–6 wk, first incisor ~7 wk); from ~2.5 months inter-tooth spacing increased, especially the upper arch; by 3–5 months the top incisors showed striking gaps about one tooth-width wide.
  • Infrared thermometry for feline body temperature: infrared (cutaneous) thermometers were unreliable compared with rectal temperature in cats.
  • Pre-mortem mortality risk factors in kittens <8 weeks (kitten nursery): elevated death risk with the lightest weight group (13×), FPV diagnosis (13×), weight loss (>9×), URI (4×), anorexia (3×), low BCS at intake (2×) and diarrhoea (2×).
  • Morbidity & applied drugs in shelter cats (Czech Republic): cats with ≥1 health record had significantly longer LOS than those without; health records were highest in kittens <6 months. Most-used drug classes were antibiotics, antiparasitics and complementary/protective substances; commonest antibiotics were penicillins, tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones — usually for upper/lower respiratory infection and GI issues.
  • Health-welfare indicators in group-housed shelter cats: cat age predicted coat condition and BCS; LOS predicted abnormal-posture/lameness scores and a URTD composite index. Despite some health improvement during the stay, a non-negligible number of cats had permanent or long-term deterioration.
  • Discospondylitis in cats (n=17): median age ~9 y, more males, mostly DSH; median sign duration 3 weeks. All had spinal hyperaesthesia and 64.7% neurological dysfunction; 2 had positive cultures (Staphylococcus, E. coli). Treated with antibiotics for a median of 3 months (first-line amoxiclav or cephalosporins), analgesia (NSAIDs ± gabapentin) and 4 weeks' exercise restriction; no recurrence.
  • Early maternal separation & telomere length in kittens: premature separation did NOT shorten telomere length by 2 months of age in human-raised vs cat-raised kittens.
  • Prebiotic fibre blend in puppies: puppies fed a prebiotic fibre blend grew and developed normally with serum and stool characteristics indicative of improved GI health (though the study's comparison markers/indicators appear weak).
