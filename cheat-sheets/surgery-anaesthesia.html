Surgery & Anaesthesia

Surgery & Anaesthesia · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Peri-operative safety in the shelter theatre — pre-op risk stratification, breed/drug adjustments, anaesthetic mortality patterns, emergency response, and structured recovery monitoring (recovery is the highest-risk window).

Pre-op Assessment

  • History — relevant items: food, and how long the patient has been fasted.
  • PE must be performed by a vet or vet tech — HR, RR, demeanour, MM (mucous membranes), CRT (capillary refill time), hydration, pulse, sex & reproductive status.
  • Fasting — ~6 h rule for dogs (AAFP guideline: food can generally be withheld, though this is based on only 1 study); no fasting data for cats — avoid prolonged fasting (hypoglycaemia risk in small/young patients).
  • Pre-anaesthetic bloodwork rarely changes management in healthy young animals — her lecture notes cite the anaesthetic protocol being altered in only ~0.2% of cases; some clinics use just PCV/TP, glucose & BUN instead of a full panel.
  • Age alone is not an ASA/risk factor — assess disease potential, not chronological age.

ASA Classification physical status

  • I–II — healthy / mild systemic disease, able to compensate without intervention.
  • III — unable to fully compensate on its own (e.g. renal insufficiency, fever, low-grade heart murmur) — give analgesia/fluids/antipyretics first.
  • IV — severe systemic disease (shock, severe dehydration, high-grade heart murmur, trauma).
  • V — moribund, not expected to survive 24 h without surgery.

Risk Factors breed / genetic / signalment

  • MDR1 / ABCB1 mutation (multidrug-resistance 1 gene, herding breeds) → drug sensitivity; reduce doses of acepromazine (ACP) and butorphanol.
  • Sighthounds (e.g. Greyhound) — hyperlactataemia, stress, and prolonged recovery after barbiturates (thiopental); plan for their low body fat.
  • Brachycephalic — pre-oxygenate; lower respiratory compliance, so prefer sternal over lateral recumbency.
  • Small/toy breeds & cats — higher risk of hypothermia & fluid overload.
  • Temperament — give anxiolytics as part of premedication for fractious/anxious patients.

Meds to Adjust

  • Antihypertensives — discontinue / withhold, esp. ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors (e.g. benazepril), on the morning of anaesthesia.
  • Insulin — give half the dose 2–4 h before, with feeding.

Dental Procedures

  • Shelter dental work should follow ASV-style shelter anaesthesia guidelines.
  • Non-anaesthetic dental is NOT recommended — inadequate assessment/treatment without anaesthesia, and restraint stress carries a real risk of dental fracture.

Anaesthetic Mortality

  • ~<0.05% in dogs, ~0.11% in cats (healthy patients).
  • Deaths cluster ~3 h post-op; 47–60% of anaesthetic deaths occur post-operatively, most within the first 3 h.
  • Higher-risk cats — ASA category >III; instability (anxiety, pain, dysrhythmias, electrolyte imbalance, dehydration); time of day (later-in-the-day procedures carry more risk — practitioner fatigue, cases stacking up).
⚠ Trap: Recovery is the highest-risk period — monitor closely.

Induction / Intubation

  • Chamber & mask induction NOT recommended — should apply to only ~10–15% of animals (reserve for unhandleable / feral).
  • Intubate for — unhandleable dogs, unknown fasting status, respiratory / pharyngeal disease, and brachycephalic dogs.
  • Pre-oxygenate before induction.
  • ETT length (endotracheal tube) — measure/cut to the point of the shoulder before placing; check the cuff.
  • Corneal lubricant — reapply roughly every 5 min under GA to prevent corneal drying.
  • BOAS (brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome) patients — keep a range of ETT sizes ready; higher regurgitation/vomiting risk.
  • Alprazolam premed, cats — 0.125 mg/cat significantly reduces the propofol dose needed at induction.

Hypoventilation / Apnoea

  • Triggers to act — end-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂; carbon dioxide) >55 mmHg (hypoventilating); respiratory rate (RR) <8/min; apnoea >30 s.
  • Start manual IPPV (intermittent positive-pressure ventilation) — tidal volume 10–15 mL/kg at 8–12 breaths/min; check airway/ETT position first.
  • Reversal agents — naloxone (opioids), atipamezole (alpha-2 agonists), flumazenil (benzodiazepines); doxapram (respiratory stimulant, 5–10 mg/kg IV) — use when ETCO₂ is high/patient is hypoventilating.

Vital Sign Monitoring

  • Normal HR — dogs ~60–120 bpm; cats notably higher. + check: exact cat HR range was hard to read in her notes — confirm before using (typical range is roughly 140–220 bpm).
  • Normal RR — ~8–20 breaths/min.
  • Normal ETCO₂ — 35–45 mmHg; <30 suggests hyperventilation, >55 suggests hypoventilation.
  • SpO₂ <90% — increase O₂, check probe accuracy & ETT position; <85% = critical, act immediately.

Fluid Therapy

  • Maintenance — isotonic crystalloids, ~5–10 mL/kg/h.
  • Shock bolus — dogs 80–90 mL/kg; cats 50–60 mL/kg (one blood volume — give as incremental boluses, reassess between each).

CPR cardiopulmonary resuscitation

  • Follow ABC (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) — intubate, place an IV catheter, attach ECG.
  • Compressions — 100–120/min, compressing ⅓–½ of chest width.
  • Positioning — sternal recumbency for cats & dogs <7 kg; lateral recumbency for larger dogs.
  • Epinephrine (adrenaline) every 3–5 min; vasopressin can alternate with epinephrine.
  • Atropine once for PEA (pulseless electrical activity) / asystole.
  • Ventricular fibrillation (VF) — lidocaine / amiodarone; defibrillation — monophasic 4–6 J/kg, biphasic 2–4.5 J/kg.
  • Reverse any reversible anaesthetic agents.

Cardiovascular Support

  • Hypotension — inotropes: dopamine, dobutamine (for poor contractility); vasopressors: phenylephrine, norepinephrine (vasoconstrictors).
  • Bradycardia — atropine 0.02–0.04 mg/kg IV; glycopyrrolate 0.005–0.01 mg/kg IV (doesn't cross the blood–brain barrier — prefer if CNS is affected).
  • Low HR with adequate BP — OK to just monitor, doesn't always need treatment.

Perioperative Hypothermia

  • Occurs in ~22% of dogs perioperatively.
  • Warm IV fluids to 38–40°C; use forced-air warming.
  • Cover extremities but keep them visible enough to monitor.

Pain Scoring

  • CMPS-SF (Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale – Short Form), dogs — intervene at ≥6/24. + check: denominator — notes appear to read 6/24 for dogs; confirm the intervention threshold denominator.
  • UNESP-Botucatu scale, cats — short form scored out of 24; her lecture notes cite ~7.8, with an intervention cut-off around >6. + check: exact cut-off — cut-offs vary by version/study, confirm against the one you're using.
  • Feline Grimace Scale — validated; re-score across hours.
  • QOL (quality-of-life) scales for dogs & cats.
  • Cats are consistently under-treated for pain relative to dogs.
  • Rabbits often mask pain (prey species) — tend to need higher NSAID doses + a local anaesthetic block than expected.
  • A mechanical (nociceptive) threshold ≠ a clinical pain score ≠ actual pain — don't conflate the three.

Local / Regional Blocks

  • Testicular, incisional, intraperitoneal (IP), and coccygeal (tail-base) blocks.
  • Pain relief should always be multimodal.

Post-op Monitoring & Discharge

  • Check the patient hourly for the first 4 h, then every 2 h.
  • Discharge criteria — BAR (bright, alert, responsive); core temp >37°C (98.6°F); stable heart rate (HR) & respiratory rate; pain score below the intervention threshold; orientated; has urinated once; tolerates water without retching.
  • Wean the E-collar and exercise restriction gradually while the patient is still recovering — don't stop either abruptly. (Rotate staff / take breaks.)
  • Surgical-site checks — immediately at recovery, then day 2, 7, 10–14.
  • Vulvar bleeding >1–2 days after a routine spay is not normal — even light spotting warrants re-examination.

📄 Journal Articles

  • Anaesthetic mortality — cats (worldwide): 0.63% (74.5% of deaths postoperative). Higher with cachexia, higher ASA status, and abdominal / orthopaedic-neuro / thoracic surgery; mechanical ventilation raised risk. Protective: alpha-2-agonist sedatives, pure opioids in premed, and locoregional techniques.
  • Anaesthetic mortality — dogs (worldwide): 0.69% (81% postoperative). Higher with age, obesity, higher ASA, and urgent / unscheduled / short procedures; some sedatives, analgesics, hypnotics and locoregional anaesthesia were protective.
  • Recovery hypoxaemia (dogs): SpO2 <95% predicts PaO2 <80 mmHg; transient postop hypoxaemia occurred in up to 28%. Risk factors: BCS >3/5, dorsal recumbency, FiO2 1.0 and absence of PEEP.
  • Gabapentin + trazodone MAC-sparing (dogs): oral gabapentin 20 mg/kg + trazodone 8 mg/kg 2 h pre-op reduced isoflurane MAC by ~0.33% with no significant haemodynamic effect (small study, 6 dogs).
  • Pregabalin ≈ gabapentin (cats): pre-anaesthetic sedation with pregabalin 4 mg/kg vs gabapentin 10 mg/kg produced indistinguishable sedation and recovery.
  • Acepromazine vs medetomidine (canine laparoscopic ovariectomy): acepromazine is a viable premed alternative — no significant increase in surgical time, difficulty or complication rate.
  • Butorphanol vs maropitant with dexmedetomidine (cats, OVH): both minimise dexmedetomidine-induced emesis; butorphanol gave deeper early sedation, maropitant lower postop pain — choose by priority.
  • Tramadol vs morphine vs methadone (cats, OVH): no difference between them; all gave adequate postop analgesia to ~6 h but were insufficient intra-operatively (77% needed intraop rescue); tramadol → more post-anaesthetic hyperthermia.
  • Grapiprant vs robenacoxib (cats, OVH): grapiprant was non-inferior to robenacoxib for postsurgical pain.
  • Cats under-treated for pain vs dogs (owner survey): analgesics prescribed for 16% of dogs vs 6.7% of cats after sterilisation; 28% of dogs and 17% of cats appeared painful at home.
  • Feline personality affects pain scoring: "independent" cats scored higher on the Glasgow CMPS-Feline and Feline Grimace Scale than "social" cats (pre- and postop) — consider temperament when titrating analgesia.
  • Autoligation of the spermatic cord (small dogs ≤11.4 kg): across 215 shelter dogs, open-castration autoligation was safe — no intraop haemorrhage, postop bleeding or scrotal haematoma; overall 8.4% minor, self-limiting bruising.
  • Lidocaine intratesticular block vs cord instillation (feline castration): both reduced surgical nociception; the intratesticular block is preferred (more stable intraop vitals, less postop pain).
  • Dilute epinephrine for feline scrotal haematoma: a dilute-epinephrine topical scrotal wash did NOT prevent scrotal haematomas (weight was the only risk factor).
  • Secondary vulvar haemorrhage after OVH (case report): fatal delayed vulvar bleeding on day 12 (no haemoabdomen); uterine-stump suppurative & chronic mural inflammation with vascular involvement — recognise and intervene early.
  • Peripheral insulation & core temperature (cats): highly-insulating limb covers slowed core-temp fall, giving a lowest temp 1–2°F higher than controls; active warming beat passive insulation, but the 0.7°F extra may not justify a heat source.
  • GA body temperature (dogs, student OVH/orchiectomy): female dogs lost more heat intra-operatively than males.
  • Anaesthesia-free dentistry (dogs): NO demonstrable medical benefit; not a viable alternative to an anaesthetised dental for periodontal disease (recheck thiol scores 0.09 anaesthetised vs 4.35 AFD).
  • Corneal dermoid removal (case report): an accessible surgical removal is a reasonable alternative to enucleation/referral in resource-limited settings; outcome acceptable, though corneal clarity may reduce at the site.
  • Perioperative pain in pet rabbits (German vet survey): 24.5% used no pre-op analgesia; 95.5% gave multimodal pre/intraop analgesia; metamizole + meloxicam were most common — practice judged suboptimal in several cases.
  • Antibiotics in canine pyometra OVH (51 bitches): good prognosis after OVH; cephazolin was the best option for E. coli (the commonest isolate); ~7 days average — a stewardship opportunity. Her lecture notes add: for an uncomplicated pyometra, OVH alone may be sufficient without a prolonged antibiotic course.
  • Endotracheal-tube cleaning protocols (dogs): proportion of ETTs with no post-cleaning bacterial growth — water scrub 60%, detergent scrub 56%, detergent + chlorhexidine-gluconate soak 80%, detergent scrub + bleach soak 68%. No protocol was fully reliable; more research is needed on a safe, effective ETT-cleaning method.
  • Intraperitoneal/incisional ropivacaine vs lidocaine (dog, major abdominal surgery): diluted ropivacaine 4 mg/kg (diluted to 5 mL/kg) as an intraperitoneal (IP) + incisional irrigation gave lower postoperative pain scores than lidocaine or saline for up to 24 h after intubation. A separate trial found UNDILUTED ropivacaine (2 mg/kg IP + 1 mg/kg incisional splash) was NOT beneficial for postop analgesia — so dilution/volume of spread appears to matter.
  • Live-animal training & student confidence (canine orchiectomy): veterinary students who had both observed and performed the procedure were more confident than those who had neither — schools should prioritise hands-on live-animal training to build orchiectomy confidence.
  • Bristol Rabbit Pain Scale — intervention cut-off: consider analgesic intervention in rabbits scoring ≥5 on the Bristol Rabbit Pain Scale.
  • Ketamine–dexmedetomidine + local for feline orchiectomy: ketamine 7 mg/kg + dexmedetomidine 10 µg/kg intramuscular (IM), combined with a local anaesthetic, was sufficient for castration; the combination can cause hypertension (study also compared atipamezole reversal in the postop period).
  • Dexmedetomidine — analgesic roles (small-animal mini-review): introduced as a sedative, dexmedetomidine is also an effective analgesic both as a single dose and as a constant-rate infusion; it can prolong the duration of a sensory (local) block and so reduce the demand for systemic analgesics — a versatile multimodal adjunct.
  • Opioid-free injectable anaesthesia (kitten OVH): in healthy kittens (~1.55 ± 0.46 kg / ~3.4 lb), ketamine 4 mg/kg + dexmedetomidine 40 µg/kg + midazolam 0.25 mg/kg IM gave adequate postoperative pain relief WHEN combined with an intraperitoneal bupivacaine splash, meloxicam ± buprenorphine.
  • Intra-anaesthetic body-temperature decline (dog): temperature dropped fastest over the first 0–15 min, more slowly from 15–60 min, then plateaued from 60–240 min; the greatest change from baseline occurred around 75 min (range 15–240) — most heat is lost early, so warm before and during induction.
  • Robenacoxib vs meloxicam (cat OVH): robenacoxib 2 mg/kg and meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg both controlled postoperative pain; meloxicam may give slightly superior pain control in this population.
  • Heated humidified breathing circuit (dog): adding a heated humidified breathing circuit on top of a warm-water blanket reduced the incidence of post-anaesthetic hypothermia. Her lecture notes cite active airway warming cutting post-extubation hypothermia incidence in dogs from ~66.7% to ~12.5%. + check: confirm these two percentages belong to this study before quoting them on the exam.
  • Yamamoto New Scalp Acupuncture (YNSA) for post-orchidectomy pain (dogs): adjunct acupuncture reduced pain scores and/or rescue-analgesia (e.g. fentanyl) requirements after canine orchidectomy. + check: from her lecture notes (shorthand) — confirm study detail before citing.
  • Spritztube extraglottic airway (rabbit): a rabbit-specific Spritztube worked as a supraglottic airway device to maintain isoflurane anaesthesia in spontaneously breathing rabbits; small sample — one rabbit developed respiratory distress after placement.
  • Vasectomy & ovary-sparing spay vs gonadectomy (6,018 dogs): longer exposure to gonadal hormones — regardless of reproductive status — was associated with LOWER odds of general health problems and of both problematic and nuisance behaviours. NO significant health-outcome difference between vasectomy vs castration, or between ovary-sparing spay vs traditional spay. Gonadectomised dogs had HIGHER odds of orthopaedic problems than sexually intact dogs or those retaining gonads.
  • Feline ovarian pedicle tie — clinician uptake: the biggest barrier to using (or endorsing as standard-of-care) the pedicle-tie technique is unfamiliarity and it not being routinely taught in veterinary school; encouragingly, more recent graduates are more likely to perform it.
  • Dexmedetomidine propofol-sparing in TIVA (cat OVH): as a total-intravenous-anaesthesia (TIVA) adjuvant — especially at 1–3 µg/kg/h — dexmedetomidine cut propofol use by ~70%+, improved cardiorespiratory stability and intra-operative analgesia, and gave a quicker, smoother recovery.
  • Post-castration pain & analgesia dosing (rabbit): rabbits displayed significantly more pain-associated behaviour at 1 h and 5 h after surgery; those on LOW-dose meloxicam 0.2 mg/kg showed more pain behaviours than those given HIGH-dose meloxicam 0.6 mg/kg plus a lidocaine/bupivacaine local infusion — favours higher-dose multimodal analgesia.
  • Grapiprant vs carprofen (cat OVH): grapiprant did NOT provide adequate analgesia in the first 3 h after ovariohysterectomy compared with carprofen, and dosing it every 12 h gave no added benefit.
  • Reflective blankets + wool socks for warming (dog celiotomy): covering the cervical and thoracic regions with a reflective blanket and wrapping the limbs in reflective blankets + wool socks is an affordable passive heat-support method to reduce perioperative hypothermia in dogs undergoing celiotomy (abdominal surgery).
  • Transdermal gabapentin (cat, pilot): gabapentin in a transdermal base penetrated feline skin in vitro, was absorbed systemically, and may lower pain scores; study dose 5 or 10 mg/kg every 8 h for 5 days.
  • Feline Grimace Scale — rater agreement/reliability: inter-rater reliability of the final Feline Grimace Scale (FGS) score was good (the muzzle and whiskers were the least reliable action units); intra-rater reliability was excellent for students and vets, good for owners and nurses. Female raters gave higher FGS scores than males (though males were under-represented).
  • Local infiltration options for cat OVH (bupivacaine vs bupivacaine-lidocaine-epinephrine vs dexamethasone vs meloxicam): early postoperative pain scores were similar across all four as part of multimodal analgesia; meloxicam edged ahead by ~3 h post-recovery, outperforming the bupivacaine-lidocaine-epinephrine group.
  • Surgical-prep rinse — alcohol vs water & body temperature (paediatric dog & cat): using water or isopropyl alcohol as the preparatory rinse produced the same degree of perioperative temperature change — rinse choice did not worsen heat loss.
  • Lip-commissure-to-eyelid transposition flap for feline eyelid agenesis (case series): a suitable technique to treat cat eyelid agenesis when specialist referral isn't an option due to limited resources (e.g. shelters or low-cost clinics).
  • Mechanical thresholds vs CMPS-Feline (feline acute pain, n = 29): pre-op mechanical (nociceptive) thresholds were higher than post-op; Glasgow Feline Composite Measure Pain Scale (CMPS-Feline) scores did not differ significantly between the pre- and post-op periods for the investigator, and inter-observer agreement was only fair pre-op and poor post-op — highlighting a limitation of this scale.
