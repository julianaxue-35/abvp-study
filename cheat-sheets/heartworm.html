Heartworm Disease

Heartworm — Dirofilaria immitis · Physical Health of Animal (29%) · Shelter Medicine

Dirofilaria immitis is largely clinically silent, slow, and costly to treat, so shelter protocols balance testing, prevention, and selective treatment. Antigen tests detect adult-female worm antigen (not microfilariae); the American Heartworm Society (AHS) 3-dose melarsomine protocol with doxycycline is the gold-standard adulticide; and strict exercise restriction is the single biggest factor preventing fatal pulmonary thromboembolism.

Life Cycle & Biology

  • Two-host cycle — mosquito (intermediate host) ↔ dog (definitive host).
  • The mosquito picks up microfilariae in a blood meal; these mature to infective L3 over 10–14 days.
  • In the dog, L3 enters at the bite and matures L3→L4→adult in the pulmonary arteries; prepatent period ~6 months, after which adults produce microfilariae.
  • A dog can be its own reservoir; cats and ferrets cannot. Microfilariae cannot mature without passing through a mosquito.
  • Wolbachia — gram-negative intracellular endosymbiont essential to heartworm development and reproduction, passed vertically (adult female → microfilariae); the target of doxycycline.
  • Do not screen before ~7 months of age (prepatent period ~6 months).

Diagnosis: Antigen vs Microfilaria run both

  • Antigen test = primary screen — detects circulating antigen of adult FEMALE worms.
  • Microfilariae are detected separately by a concentration test — the Modified Knott's.
  • Run BOTH (AHS) — a small fraction of infected dogs are antigen-negative but microfilaria-positive.
  • Dogs on preventives may give a false-negative antigen from antigen–antibody immune complexes; heat-treating the serum unmasks the blocked antigen.
  • "No antigen detected" does not confirm freedom from infection; confirm every positive antigen before any adulticide.
⚠ Trap: the antigen test detects adult-female worm antigen, NOT microfilariae. Microfilariae are found by concentration (Modified Knott's) — this is exactly why AHS runs both.

Discordant Tests & Look-alike

  • Antigen+ / Microfilaria+ (patent) — adult females plus circulating microfilariae = active transmission reservoir. Treat, and make microfilariae non-infective before any relocation.
  • Antigen+ / Microfilaria− (prepatent or occult) — adult-female antigen but no microfilariae: either too early (prepatent) or immune-cleared / preventive-suppressed (occult). Still infected, still treat; lower immediate transmission risk. Give a macrocyclic lactone (ML, a monthly preventive) or a 28-day doxycycline course before relocation so a prepatent dog does not turn microfilaria-positive in transit.
  • Antigen− / Microfilaria+ (the exam trap) — first confirm the microfilariae are truly D. immitis on the Knott's; if so, antigen is usually blocked by immune complexes → heat-treat serum and repeat to unmask it (or an early / low female-worm burden). Treat as positive.
⚠ Trap — look-alike: the non-pathogenic subcutaneous worm Acanthocheilonema (Dipetalonema) reconditum also circulates microfilariae; misidentified as D. immitis, a dog is wrongly called heartworm-positive. A Modified Knott's with careful morphology distinguishes the two.

Pre-Transport / Safe Relocation AHS · ASV

  • Screen every dog older than 6 months for both antigen and microfilariae, within 30 days of transport.
  • Make microfilariae non-infective FIRST — a single dose of topical moxidectin (eliminates most microfilariae), or an ML plus a topical permethrin + dinotefuran + pyriproxyfen product that kills/repels mosquitoes so the dog cannot seed them; doxycycline further blocks transmission (Wolbachia-suppressed microfilariae cannot develop inside the mosquito).
  • Recheck the Knott's in 7 days — two negative tests 7 days apart give reasonable assurance, then relocate.
  • If the dog has had melarsomine, do NOT transport for ≥4 weeks after an injection — transport stress/exertion raise the risk of fatal pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE).
  • Never relocate a positive dog silently — disclose status and treatment plan to the receiving organisation. (ASV = Association of Shelter Veterinarians.)

Radiographic Staging

  • Most objective severity measure — thoracic radiography assesses heartworm-associated cardiopulmonary disease and is the basis for staging the dog.
  • Nearly pathognomonic vascular signs — enlarged, tortuous, and truncated peripheral (intralobar/interlobar) branches of the pulmonary arteries, with variable pulmonary parenchymal disease.
  • Severe disease — marked right-heart enlargement produces the classic "reverse D" cardiac silhouette.
  • Echocardiography confirms infection when adult worms are seen, but is an inefficient screening test, especially with light worm burdens.

AHS Treatment Protocol 3-dose

  • Melarsomine — the only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved adulticide; effective against worms ≥120 days old; given by deep intramuscular (IM) injection into the epaxial lumbar muscles (L3–L5).
  • 3-dose (split) protocol — safest and most effective (~99% vs 90.8%): Day 0 ML + doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO BID × 28 days; melarsomine 2.5 mg/kg dose 1 (~Day 60); doses 2 & 3 given 24 h apart (~Days 90 / 91). (PO = by mouth; BID = twice daily.)
  • Prednisone taper after each injection — 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h (wk 1) → 0.5 mg/kg q24h (wk 2) → 0.5 mg/kg every other day (wks 3–4).
  • Strict cage rest / leash-only throughout AND for ≥6–8 weeks after the last injection — the single most important factor preventing fatal PTE.
  • Doxycycline / Wolbachia — suppresses Wolbachia, reduces post-treatment pulmonary pathology, lowers microfilarial counts, and renders microfilariae non-infective to mosquitoes.
  • Slow-kill (ML-only) NOT recommended except specific cases (prior melarsomine anaphylaxis; grave non-heartworm prognosis); contraindicated in performance / non-restrictable dogs and where ML resistance is suspected.
  • Caval syndrome — heavy worm burdens partially obstruct the tricuspid valve, develop acutely, and are usually fatal within ~2 days unless worms are surgically extracted.
  • 30–120-day susceptibility gap — MLs reliably kill only worms ≤30 days; melarsomine only ≥120 days. Worms 30–120 days old were classically "too old for the preventive, too young for the adulticide." Modern view: no gap if you start at diagnosis — the full 3-dose melarsomine + doxycycline + ongoing ML clears all stages; delaying does not improve efficacy and lets disease progress.
  • Shelter "moxi-doxy" alternative — when melarsomine is unaffordable/unavailable: topical moxidectin monthly + doxycycline 10 mg/kg PO q12–24h × 28 days, repeated until antigen-negative. Slower, may allow some pulmonary progression, but serious complications uncommon and far better than no treatment. Exercise restriction still applies.

Post-Adulticide Follow-up & Retreatment

  • Two rechecks, two questions — the microfilaria (Knott's) test asks "is the microfilaricide working / is the dog still a reservoir?"; the antigen test asks "did the adulticide work — are the adults gone?" They are decoupled (a dog can be microfilaria+ / antigen−, or antigen+ / microfilaria−).
  • Post-treatment retest — check microfilariae ~4 weeks after the last injection (~Day 120); if positive, give a microfilaricide and recheck.
  • Microfilaricide kinetics are gradual — moxidectin (incl. the sustained-release ProHeart depot) drops counts sharply by ~day 30 but may not fully clear until ~5 months (~day 150). Persistent low microfilaremia at a few months is usually slow clearance, not failure — judge by the trend. Do NOT stack a second ML onto an active ProHeart depot (same class, no benefit, higher reaction risk).
  • Retreatment trigger = the 9-month ANTIGEN test — AHS moved this from 6 to 9 months (2018) because residual antigen from dying worms lingers up to ~3 months. By 9 months, any antigen = surviving adults or new infection.
  • If antigen+ at 9 months (confirm on recheck; heat-treating can unmask): retreat with 30 days doxycycline + 2 doses melarsomine; a borderline result may allow an extra ~3 months. Antigen− at 9 months = cured, even if microfilariae cleared slowly.
⚠ Trap: retreatment is decided by the 9-month antigen test, NOT by microfilaria status — antigen and microfilaria results are decoupled.

Feline & Ferret Heartworm

  • Cat — low worm burdens; an infected cat poses no transmission risk to other cats. Melarsomine is NOT recommended (high risk of a fatal pulmonary reaction); most cats self-cure over ~18–48 months. Management is supportive: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day, tapered over a few weeks, for HARD (heartworm-associated respiratory disease), plus maintained monthly prevention. Even the death of a single worm can trigger a fatal acute pulmonary reaction / sudden death — counsel adopters.
  • Feline diagnostics — no single test is sensitive enough; combine antibody + antigen + imaging. Antibody detects both sexes from ~2 months post-infection (shows exposure occurred, not that it persists). Antigen detects only adult females, ~5.5–8 months post-infection; 30–50% of infected cats have no female worm → antigen-negative.
  • Screening policy — routine heartworm screening is NOT indicated in asymptomatic shelter cats; test symptomatic cats and counsel adopters.
  • Ferret — highly susceptible; even a single worm can catastrophically obstruct the tiny heart. Microfilaremia is transient and antigen tests are frequently false-negative (low burden / single-sex male). Bilirubinuria is the most consistently reported clinicopathologic change.

Prevention ASV PS

  • The ASV endorses the AHS guidelines and urges year-round prevention for dogs, cats, and ferrets — some transmission risk exists everywhere. Screen at-risk dogs; provide prevention even without screening; treat infected dogs promptly.
  • Heartworm management should not divert resources from other animals; a shelter without treatment capacity may transfer infected dogs to a partner with capacity, or release to an informed adopter with clear follow-up.
  • High-prevalence areas: screen by microfilaria first (cheaper); low-prevalence: antigen-test first. A treated dog is not heartworm-free until two antigen tests 6 months apart are negative.
  • MLs (ivermectin, selamectin, milbemycin, moxidectin) are reliable preventives only against worms ≤30 days old; DEET (an insect repellent) is unsafe in dogs, cats, and ferrets.
  • Permethrin spot-ons block mosquito transmission (e.g. permethrin + dinotefuran + pyriproxyfen on relocated dogs reduces the chance an infected dog seeds local mosquitoes).
⚠ Trap: permethrin is NEVER used on cats — it causes severe, potentially fatal toxicity.
